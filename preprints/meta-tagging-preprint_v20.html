Abstract

Academic knowledge is written almost entirely in natural language, which makes it readable by humans but hard to query across disciplines that use different words for related things. Earlier versions of this work described a tag layer that assigns every paper the same small set of field-neutral facets, under one non-negotiable rule: every tag must be licensed by an exact, verbatim sentence from the paper's own text. That format proved feasible and verifiable — 18,416 evidence strings re-checked against their sources, three failed — and it returned an explicit null result: free-text names for a phenomenon almost never collide across fields. 500 distinct strings over 511 papers, two of which cross a disciplinary boundary. The conclusion drawn was that a canonical vocabulary, not free text, is the missing ingredient.

This version describes such a vocabulary, and a working prototype that carries it. Choosing what belongs under one term is applying a definition — usually without stating it, and always without measuring it. So the tags become records rather than strings: a concept holds its attestations, its senses, and the relations between them, each grounded in a quotation. And the definition that decides membership becomes measurable: it can be treated as a binary classifier over cases the literature itself adjudicated and ranked against rivals in a single run, after three logical gates that no statistical measure detects — circularity, recursion without a base case, and a free parameter the author never fixed. We report inter-coder reliability on the act of application itself (κ = 0.547, n = 140), a measurement almost absent from comparable interpretive-tagging work.

The change is what a tag now is. Previously a tag proved provenance: the sentence exists in the paper. That is a string comparison, and a string comparison establishes where a tag came from and nothing about what it means. A tag now carries a sense, and the term that names it is one whose extension has been measured against evidence. Corpus at this version: 538 records across 86 disciplines.

1. What changed, and why

The layer worked as a format and failed as a join. Both halves of that sentence come from the previous version's own results, and this version is the response to the second half.

What the earlier versions established

Every paper receives the same tags — not by topic but by the shape of its contribution: claim type, phenomenon, claim relations, replication status, limitations. The tag list is a single shared registry (SCHEMA/tags.json, version 0.5): 45 tags in seven layers, each defined once for all papers. And one rule governs everything:

No tag without a quotation. Every tag must be licensed by an exact sentence from the paper, and the check is not a judgement but a string comparison — and a string comparison cannot be talked into agreeing. A tag whose quotation is not found is dropped, not repaired. 18,416 strings re-checked; three failed.

And what they found did not work

The measurement returned a null result and the previous version reported it plainly: the phenomenon facets, written as verbatim-anchored free text, rarely collide across fields. A novelty scan surfaced no cross-disciplinary link the literature had not already drawn. Where a controlled vocabulary existed — the statistical layer — joins were real, the broadest term spanning 31 disciplines. The stated conclusion: a canonical join vocabulary, not free text, is the missing ingredient.

That conclusion is where this version begins, and it is not the technical matter it appears to be. Building a canonical vocabulary means deciding which items belong under one term. That decision is a definition, applied — and until now applied silently. Every tagging project in the field applies definitions this way. None of them measures them.

The change, in one sentence

The work here makes the tagging meaningful: conceptually, by making a concept a record that holds its senses rather than a string that recurs; and operationally, by measuring the definition that decides what falls under it.

The two are the same move seen from two sides. A concept becomes a record so that it can hold a sense; the definition is measured so that the sense is not merely asserted. Neither half works alone: a record with no measured definition is a filing cabinet, and a measured definition with nowhere to live changes nothing about the corpus.

What follows from it, concretely

  • A concept is a record. 510 concept records built from the corpus, each holding one PaperSource per paper — because twenty-five mentions in one paper are one author's position, not twenty-five — and one Attestation per mention, carrying the sentence, its role (defines / exemplifies / refutes / mentions) and whether the paper affirms, denies or leaves open.
  • A concept can hold more than one sense, with the relations between them recorded and grounded. Freud's consciousness and the cognitive one are not one object, and averaging them produces a number describing nobody. Disagreement is the datum, not the noise.
  • The definition is measured, not asserted — §§6–11 below.
  • Provenance and meaning are separate gates. The verbatim check proves the sentence exists. A second gate asks whether that sentence licenses the concept, and whether two papers using the word mean the same thing by it. The first gate is mechanical; the second is the one that decides whether the layer is worth anything.
The claim this version makes is narrow and it is the honest one. Not that the corpus has grown, and not that new cross-disciplinary links were found. It is that a tag now carries meaning that can be checked — a sense, an evidence base, and a membership rule whose extension has been measured. That is the difference between an index and a knowledge layer, and it is what the earlier null result showed was missing.

7. The three gates

A definition is a promise: it says what is admitted and what is excluded. It can be tested only where cases already have an adjudication — and here the adjudication is not ours but the literature's. A case is positive if a paper states explicitly that it is the concept, negative if a paper states explicitly that it is not. Silence is not negation. Every case carries a verified verbatim quotation.

What the gates disqualify, and why before scoring rather than after

Three definitions in the corpus scored highly, and in each a defect emerged that no statistical measure detects. They are therefore disqualified before scoring and left unranked.

Gate 1 — circularity

The definiens contains the definiendum. Our circular control ("whatever people call art") achieves an almost perfect MCC precisely because it copies the answer. No measure will ever catch it.

Gate 2 — recursion without a base case

Levinson's historical definition: "a thing intended for regard as prior artworks were correctly regarded." It defines art by reference to prior art and never states what the first art was. The mathematical defence (0 is a number; n+1 is a number) works only when the base is given. Here there is n+1 and no 0.

Gate 3 — failure of point identification

Gaut's cluster account: an object is art if it meets enough of ten criteria, none of which is necessary — and he never says how many "enough" is. Dutton, who defends the same approach, concedes the point explicitly: "A recognition-criteria approach ... does not tell us in advance how many of the criteria need be present."
We decomposed the ten criteria and coded 120 cases against each. Moving the threshold from k = 1 to k = 10 moves the score across a range of 0.6 MCC, and if k may be chosen per case the account attains MCC 1.000. A definition with a free parameter is not falsifiable, and its score measures whoever applies it rather than the corpus.

Why the gates precede scoring

All three share one property: no statistical measure sees them. A circular definition classifies excellently precisely because it copies the answer; a definition with a free threshold receives whatever score its applier selected for it. If the gate runs after scoring, the defective definition already sits at the top of the table and has to be struck out afterwards — which is exactly what happened to us once, when an automated search was allowed to build a definition from a criterion list one of whose members contained the word "art". A gate applied after generation is a gate applied too late.

Measurement versus refinement — a distinction that must be recorded

A definition changes when a case defeats it, so every definition carries a version history. But one difference must not be blurred:

Measurement — the wording was fixed before the cases were seen. The score is a test.
Refinement — the wording was fixed after it became clear which cases failed. The score is not a test, because the cases are already known.

Both are legitimate, and neither may be reported as though the two were measured under the same conditions. The apparatus retains every version, never deletes, and marks each one accordingly.

Sub-concepts must themselves be defined

A definition resting on an undefined term transfers the adjudication to unwritten judgement. In a case we measured, defining the internal term according to what the corpus actually does with it — without altering a single word of the definition itself — raised the score from +0.380 to +0.664. A definer does not repair their own terms; those are settled outside the definition. The definition therefore unfolds recursively until further decomposition stops changing the score.

9. The confusion matrix

Here the "classifier" is the definition, and "ground truth" is what the papers themselves state. This is not absolute truth; it is the truth the corpus supplies, and that is precisely what makes the measurement relative to a corpus.

The four cells

  • TP — the definition admits a case the papers declare to be the concept. TP = |{ x : D(x)=1 AND corpus(x)=yes }|
  • FP — the definition admits a case the papers declare not to be the concept. FP = |{ x : D(x)=1 AND corpus(x)=no }|
  • FN — the definition excludes a case the papers declare to be the concept. FN = |{ x : D(x)=0 AND corpus(x)=yes }|
  • TN — the definition excludes a case the papers declare not to be the concept. TN = |{ x : D(x)=0 AND corpus(x)=no }|
Every FN refutes necessity. Every FP refutes sufficiency. This is the whole translation from the logical vocabulary into a measurable one.

What the matrix covers, and what it does not

Three of the six criteria are computed from these four cells: coverage (recall = TP/(TP+FN)), discrimination (precision = TP/(TP+FP)), and prediction — the same four cells on a held-out sample not used in constructing the definition. The remaining three lie outside it. Typicality ranking requires an order rather than labels, and is measured by rank correlation between the ranking a definition predicts and frequency of attestation in the corpus. Applicability is measured by κ. Minimality is a property of the definition rather than of the cases, and is measured by ablation: remove one condition, re-score; a condition whose removal changes nothing is ornament.

Three states, not two

A case is positive when a paper states explicitly that it is the concept, and negative when a paper states explicitly that it is not. The third state is silence, and silence is not negation. If silence were counted as negative, every paper that did not mention the Mona Lisa would be recorded as claiming that it is not art. This is the error that destroys measurements of this kind, and it is very easy to make when counting automatically.

The unit of analysis is accordingly neither the paper nor the passage but the (case, paper) pair. The same case may be positive in one paper and negative in another, and that disagreement is the most valuable datum in the corpus rather than noise: collapsing to the paper loses it, collapsing to the case loses who said it. Variance is decomposed with the paper as a random effect (ICC = between / (between + within)), so that twenty-five attestations from a single paper inflate nothing.

11. Reliability of application — κ

Applicability is not one more row in the table: it is a precondition for everything else. If two people applying the same definition to the same case reach different answers, the four cells of the matrix measure the applier rather than the definition.

We measured this directly. A blind sample of 140 (case × definition) pairs across seven definitions was coded by a second coder that was not the system which produced the first coding. The question put on every row was not "is this art" but "does this definition, read literally, apply to this thing".

The hypothesis was refuted, and that is the finding. We stated in advance, in the coding sheet itself, that we expected κ for the cluster account to collapse relative to the others, because it does not fix how many of its ten criteria count as "enough". It did not collapse — +0.417, only +0.090 from the mean of the others. To the extent this sample allows, Gaut is right: the threshold converges in practice even when it is left unstated.
What is of concern. An overall κ of 0.547 is moderate rather than substantial on the conventional scale (Landis & Koch, 1977). Two coders disagree on roughly a quarter of the judgements on which every ranking in this document rests. For comparison, on a controlled layer of statistical terms we measured κ = 0.767 with the identical apparatus. The difference is not noise: it is the cost of applying a verbal definition as against matching a term from a closed list, and it is the open problem of the field.

One incidental result deserves recording. The definition with the highest agreement is the one disqualified at the recursion gate. "Intended for regard as prior works were regarded" is easy to agree on precisely because the test is simple to apply and demands no substantive judgement. A stable definition is not thereby a good one, and the two measures must be reported side by side.

What is shared, and what differs

Shared: all of them tag a phenomenon in a text — a metaphor, a place name, delegitimisation, an idiom. Different: for them tagging is the object; here it is the substrate — the evidence against which a definition is tested. The question asked is not "what is in this text" but "which definition of the concept withstands this corpus".

Two weaknesses recur across nearly all of this work, and they are precisely where the present method contributes.

1 · Categories without definitions. Gutman's 103 topics carry no definition, and he concedes the consequence in a footnote: two interpreters may assign different topics to the same word, and he defers the reliability measurement to future work. 2 · Reliability that is not measured. Of the eight, only Rivlin-Angert measured it — three coders, a 170-sentence sample, κ = 0.82. For Dekel non-measurement is an explicit methodological position; in the MEHDIE paper there is no accuracy figure of any kind, and the evidence offered is a single worked case.

This is what the present method adds: a mandatory verbatim quotation for every tag, and a measurable definition for every category.

Why it works there and breaks here

Rusinek's Kima has the same shape as the record used here — place → name variant → dated attestation against concept → wording → quotation. The architectures are identical. The only difference is that a place has a referent: one can transliterate it to IPA and check whether two spellings sound the same. An abstract concept admits no such test. That is the gap, and it is the open problem for all of us.

Interoperability — what must be emitted

Connection requires no agreement between projects. It requires three format decisions:

  • An opaque identifier instead of a label. Where the tag is the word, any change of wording silently breaks every external link. A stable identifier, with the words hanging off it as skos:prefLabel and skos:altLabel.
  • A textual anchor that is not an offset. A TextQuoteSelector (quotation plus preceding and following context) survives re-editions; character offsets do not. Offsets for tools that need them are derived from it at export time.
  • Resolution to an external authority — Wikidata, GeoNames. Once two layers point at the same identifier they join without any bilateral arrangement.

Two export formats carry the community's actual practice: two-tier TEI (metadata in teiHeader, span annotations in standOff), and a pair of Gephi CSV files (nodes and edges).

And in the other direction: defining Gutman's 103 categories with this instrument would supply the reliability measure his work lacks, and would supply this work a test case that is not its own — which is the more valuable half of the exchange, because on that corpus the authors hold no competing definition.

13. Limitations

This list belongs in the body of the text and not in a footnote. A new measure that arrives with its limitations is received better than one that arrives without them, and a reviewer will find them regardless.
  • Reliability is moderate, and it bounds everything else. κ = 0.547 over 140 blind pairs. Two coders disagree on roughly a quarter of the judgements the rankings rest on.
  • Comparison is valid within a single run only. The same definition scored +0.714 in one run and +0.591 in another on identical cases; the only difference was the wording of the instruction given to coders. MCC is not a property of a definition but of the pair (definition × instruction). Numbers from two tables must therefore never be compared.
  • The negatives are not a representative sample. They are contrast cases someone troubled to write down, not a random sample of what is not the concept. Accuracy and specificity are therefore inadmissible, and the corpus was deliberately extended with hard negatives — advertisements, games, sport — where permissiveness is caught.
  • There is no access to authorial intent. What was not written is not measured.
  • Recall has a ceiling. What was missed cannot be known without knowing the whole set; coverage is therefore coverage relative to a documented pool, and must be named as such.
  • The corpus is still small. 34 papers. The bootstrap unit is the paper, so the effective n is 34 rather than 372 — which is why the lower places in a ranking are not separable.
  • The Wittgensteinian position cannot be scored, as stated in §5.

Appendix A — formulae

All measures are computed from the four cells. TP and FN are counted over cases the corpus adjudicated positive, FP and TN over those it adjudicated negative.

precision = TP / (TP + FP) — discrimination
recall = TP / (TP + FN) — coverage
F1 = 2 · precision · recall / (precision + recall)
MCC = (TP·TN − FP·FN) / sqrt((TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN))

Why MCC and not F1. F1 ignores TN, so on a positively skewed corpus a classifier that says yes to everything obtains a high F1. MCC gives that same classifier exactly 0.000 (Matthews, 1975; Chicco & Jurman, 2020). F1 is reported beside it, never alone.

Reliability

κ = (p_o − p_e) / (1 − p_e)
p_o = observed agreement · p_e = agreement expected by chance from the two coders' marginals (Cohen, 1960). Interpretation of values follows Landis & Koch (1977). For more than two coders or mixed scales, Krippendorff's alpha (Hayes & Krippendorff, 2007).

Variance between papers and within them

The same case may be judged in several papers. Variance is decomposed by analysis of variance:

ICC = σ²_between / (σ²_between + σ²_within) (Shrout & Fleiss, 1979)
High ICC — papers disagree with one another and are internally consistent ⟶ the disagreement is cross-disciplinary, and the senses should be split.
Low ICC — the same paper holds several positions ⟶ the concept is contested within a single author, which is a finding in itself.

This also answers "count cases or count papers": a hierarchical model with the paper as a random effect counts both, and twenty-five attestations from one paper inflate nothing.

Confidence intervals and comparison

Confidence intervals are computed by bootstrap (Efron, 1979) at the level of the paper and not the case, because cases from one paper share a single author's usage and are not independent; resampling cases yields intervals that are too narrow and a significance that does not exist. Two definitions on the same cases are compared with a paired test (McNemar, 1947), and a tie is declared when neither beats the other in 95% of resamples.

Appendix C — when the method proposes a definition itself

The apparatus does not only rank definitions submitted to it; it can assemble one from atomic conditions drawn from the definitions already in the corpus, and score it like any other. This is what a user asks for when they have no candidate of their own.

Here the important lesson about order of operations was learned. In the first run the search was allowed to see the full inventory of conditions, including one phrased as "produced with the intention of making a work of art". The search selected exactly that one, obtained the highest score in the table, and had to be struck out afterwards — a fully circular definition that the machine rediscovered on its own within seconds. Circular conditions are now flagged in the data itself and removed from the vocabulary before the search begins. A gate applied after generation is a gate applied too late.
A second lesson, general and not specific to any one concept. In a later run the method proposed a definition resting on the property "exhibits an individual point of view". It scored highly — until seven negative control cases were added (an opinion column, a party manifesto, a closing argument in court, a scientific paper, a book review, a restaurant review, a personal blog post). The definition admitted all seven, and the score collapsed.

The method was not at fault. The corpus did not contain what could have taught it. No academic paper writes "an opinion column is not a work of art", because no one needs to. Hence the principle, which holds for any concept: a definition cannot be falsified by a corpus that does not contain what would falsify it.

The control cases are held in a separate file and marked explicitly as the researcher's labelling rather than the literature's, and every score is reported with and without them. They are not evidence; they are an instrument of control, exactly like the deliberately broken definitions run alongside the real ones.
