In everyday life, we are often exposed to a mix of different sounds. An essential task for our brain is to separate the important sounds from the unimportant ones. For example, stepping out onto a busy street, you may at first be very aware of the noise of traffic. Later, you may start to ignore the din and instead only notice sounds that break the monotony: a honking car horn or maybe a stranger's voice. This is because the neurons in the auditory pathway respond differently to common and rare sounds. In particular, excitatoryterm neurons in the region termed the ‘auditory cortexterm’ send fewer nerve impulses in response to frequent sounds, but respond vigorously to rare sounds. This phenomenon is called ‘stimulus-specific adaptationterm’, but it is not known exactly which neurons in this brain region enable this process to occur.

Now, NatanpersName et al. have combined different cutting-edge neuroscience techniques to identify the circuit of brain cells that drives this stimulus specific adaptationterm. A technique called optogenetics was used to effectively ‘turn off’ each of two kinds of inhibitory neuron in the auditory cortexterm of mice, by exposing the brain to colored light from a laser.

Across sensory modalities, cortical neurons exhibit adaptationterm, attenuating their responses to redundant stimuli ( Das and Gilbert, 1999 ; Ulanovsky et al., 2003 ; Garcia-Lazaro et al., 2007 ; Asari and Zador, 2009 ; Khatri et al., 2009 ). Adaptationterm to stimulus context is thought to increase efficiency of sensory coding under the constraints of limited resources ( Barlow, 1961 ). Yet, the neuronal-circuit mechanisms that facilitate adaptationterm in the cortexterm remain poorly understood. In the primary auditory cortexterm (A1), the vast majority of neurons exhibit stimulus-specific adaptationterm (SSAterm, Figure 1 ). When presented with a sequence of two tones, one of which occurs frequently (termed ‘standardterm’) and another rarely (termed ‘deviantterm’), the neuron's response to the standardterm toneterm becomes weaker, but the response to the deviant toneterm·def remains strong ( Ulanovsky et al., 2003 ; Szymanski et al., 2009 ; Farley et al., 2010 ; Fishman and Steinschneider, 2012 ). Auditory cortexterm, like other sensory cortices, contains morphologically and physiologically diverse inhibitory interneurons, which form dense interconnected networks with excitatoryterm neurons ( DeFelipe, 2002 ; Douglas and Martin, 2004 ). While different interneuronterm types have been hypothesized to carry out specialized complementary functions in sensory processing ( DeFelipe, 2002 ; Markram et al., 2004 ; Isaacson and Scanziani, 2011 ; Kepecs and Fishell, 2014 ; Marlin et al., 2015 ), their function in driving changes in dynamic auditory processing has not been previously established. We hypothesized that the two most common types of interneurons in the cortexterm, parvalbumin- (PVs) and somatostatin-positive interneurons (SOMs) ( Xu et al., 2010 ; Rudy et al., 2011 ), facilitate SSAterm in excitatoryterm neurons of A1 in a complementary fashion. PVs, a subset of which receive direct thalamicterm inputs ( Staiger et al., 1996 ), may amplify SSAterm in excitatoryterm neurons by providing a constant inhibitory drive; equally strong inhibitory drive would attenuate the weak response to standardterm tones relatively more than the strong response to deviantterm tones, leading to a greater differential between standardterm vs deviant toneterm·def spiking response. SOMs, which target distal dendrites of pyramidal cells ( McGarry et al., 2010 ; Gentet et al., 2012 ), have excitatoryterm synapses that exhibit facilitation upon repetitive stimulation ( Reyes et al., 1998 ; Silberberg and Markram, 2007 ). Therefore, inputs from SOMs may exert a stimulus-specific increase in suppressionterm of excitatoryterm neurons that is selective to the standardterm toneterm and does not generalize to the deviant toneterm·def. Alternatively, they may contribute to selective adaptationterm in excitatoryterm neurons through differential post-synaptic integration.

To tease apart the function of different inhibitory types in SSAterm, we tested whether optogeneticterm·mth suppressionterm of either PVterm or SOMterm interneurons during sound presentation reduced SSAterm in putative excitatoryterm neurons in the auditory cortexterm ( Hamilton et al., 2013 ; Pi et al., 2013 ; Weible et al., 2014 ). We found that both types of interneurons contribute to SSAterm in the cortexterm, with PVs providing constant inhibition, and SOMs increasing their effect with repeated tones.

SSAterm was measured from the firing rate (FR) of neurons in response to tones presented as a series of ‘oddball’ stimuli. On the other hand, for SOMs, modulation of their activity in the deviantterm tone-evoked regime drives small to no changes in excitatoryterm neuronal activity, whereas modulation of SOMterm activity in the spontaneous and standardterm tone-evoked regime drives significant changes in excitatoryterm neuronal activity ( Figure 7A , right inset). The majority of neurons in the auditory cortexterm selectively reduce their responses to frequent, but not rare sounds, exhibiting SSAterm. However, the cortical mechanisms involved in the production and stimulus-specificity of SSAterm within the auditory cortexterm are not well understood. Here, we found that, in addition to adaptationterm at the level of thalamocortical inputs, two distinct types of interneurons, PVs and SOMs, differentially contributed to SSAterm in the primary auditory cortexterm. Optogeneticterm·mth suppressionterm of either PVs or SOMs led to a reduction in SSAterm in putative excitatoryterm neurons ( Figure 3 ). Suppressionterm of PVs led to an equal increase in the FR of the putative excitatoryterm neurons in response to the standardterm toneterm and the deviant toneterm·def ( Figure 4 ). By contrast, suppressionterm of SOMs significantly increased the response to the standardterm toneterm but lacked a significant effect on the response to the deviant toneterm·def ( Figure 4 ). This series of findings expands on the ‘adaptationterm in narrowly tuned units’ modelterm·mth, which proposes that repeated presentation of the standardterm stimulus drives adaptationterm within more narrowly tuned inputs, such as thalamocortical inputs ( Mill et al., 2011 ; Taaseh et al., 2011 ; Nelken, 2014 ). Our data indicate dual effects of cortical inhibition on SSAterm: (1) PVs contribute to SSAterm by providing a constant level of inhibition, resulting in a relatively higher inhibitory drive during the presentation of the standardterm, as compared to the deviantterm. Taking into account the non-linear synaptic input to FR output function of a typical pyramidal neuron, the constant inhibition amplifies the effect of thalamocortical depression in suppressing the response of the neuron to repeated stimulus ( Figure 7A ). (2) The selective increase of the inhibitory drive from SOMs for standardterm stimulus as compared to the deviantterm stimulus responses might be explained by a shift in the non-linear transfer function between inputs to SOMs and their outputs to excitatoryterm neurons, possibly due to facilitation of SOM-to-excitatory neuron synapses ( Beierlein et al., 2003 ; Silberberg and Markram, 2007 ) ( Figure 7A ).

Surprisingly, we found that, despite the differential effect of PVterm and SOMterm suppressionterm on tone-evoked responses in putative excitatoryterm neurons, both PVs and SOMs exhibit SSAterm. This finding is consistent with previous results that found that thalamocortical synapses onto inhibitory neurons and synapses from inhibitory neurons to excitatoryterm cells can be depressing ( Tan et al., 2008 ; Ma et al., 2012 ). How does suppressionterm of these interneurons result in differential reduction in SSAterm in excitatoryterm neurons? Our modelterm·mth provides an intuition for this effect: the mutually coupled excitatoryterm–inhibitory network modelterm·mth demonstrates that the observed differential effects of PVterm and SOMterm suppressionterm may be due to their differential action on excitatoryterm neuronal responses in the unadapted and adapted state ( Figure 7 ). Tone-evoked responses of PVs would fall on the linear portion of the transfer function between PVterm activity and excitatoryterm neuron depolarization, while the same tones maximally affect inputs from SOMs onto excitatoryterm neurons, with SSAterm shifting the inputs to the linear, more sensitive range of inputs from SOMs. Thus, SSAterm may serve an additional function: to adjust the responses of neurons in a range that is more sensitive to small changes in the inputs from both excitatoryterm and inhibitory neuronal populations. More generally, the simulation demonstrates that a circuit element, such as PVs or SOMs, that itself adapts may further amplify adaptationterm in the excitatoryterm neurons.

To estimate the differential contribution of PVs or SOMs inputs to the excitatoryterm neurons, we measured the difference in the FR of neurons due to optogeneticterm·mth partial suppressionterm of their firing. This measurement provides an estimate of the change in the FR of the putative excitatoryterm neurons with the change in combined inputs to the inhibitory neurons, thereby allowing estimation of the synaptic transfer function ( Figure 7A , insets). A simple biologically plausible network incorporating these transfer functions can reproduce the observed responses ( Figure 7 ). There are several caveats to this interpretation. First, the FR may not linearly translate onto synaptic input strength because of the spiking non-linear rectification between the inputs and outputs of the putative excitatoryterm neuron: a small change in FR in the low-FR regime might correspond to a greater change in the synaptic drive than a similar-sized change in FR in the high-FR regime. However, our findings would still hold were this the case: in examining the effect of SOMterm suppressionterm on response to the deviantterm, the actual difference in the synaptic drive between the deviantterm and the standardterm would then be even greater than observed. At the other end of the non-linearity, the analysis of neuronal responses sorted based on their FR to the standardterm toneterm and the deviant toneterm·def revealed that the ‘ceiling effect’ would not contribute to a decreased effect of photostimulation on the response to the deviantterm in SOM-Cre mice ( Figure 4—figure supplements 4–7 ). Second, PVs and SOMs may inhibit not only the excitatoryterm neurons, but also each other. SOMs make synapses onto PVs ( Isaacson and Scanziani, 2011 ; Ma et al., 2012 ; Cottam et al., 2013 ), thereby potentially suppressing them with repeated presentation of the standardterm. Therefore, when SOMs are suppressed, some PVs may be disinhibited and provide a stronger suppressionterm of excitatoryterm neurons. The null effect on responses to the deviant toneterm·def during SOMterm suppressionterm could result from a combination of increase in inhibition from disinhibited PVs in addition to reduced inhibition of SOMs onto excitatoryterm neurons. Including inhibition from SOMs to PVs in the proof-of-principle modelterm·mth supported experimental findings ( Figure 7—figure supplement 2 ). Third, other interneuronterm types, such as vasopressin-positive interneuronterm may be involved in the circuit ( Pi et al., 2013 ), and the changes that we observe may reflect several inhibitory stages of processing.

One must be cautious in translating the data from our experiments as a strict description of neuronal activity in awake animals, as our results were based on recordings from mice under light isoflurane anesthesiaterm. Other forms of anesthesiaterm, such as pentobarbital-based ( Cheung et al., 2001 ; Gaese and Ostwald, 2001 ), ketamine ( Otazu et al., 2009 ) and high concentrations of isoflurane ( Cheung et al., 2001 ; Ter-Mikaelian et al., 2007 ), can affect multiple aspects of sound-evoked responses in the auditory cortexterm. Nonetheless, our results are likely to extend for awake mice, since isoflurane anesthesia-induced effects on neuronal activity decrease as the concentration of isoflurane is reduced to the levels used in our recordings ( Land et al., 2012 ). In addition, all recordings and manipulations were performed under identical anesthetic conditions, and our conclusions are based on the relative comparison of the effects of suppressing PVs and SOMs, which are expected to hold under awake conditions ( Centanni et al., 2013 ).

While not demonstrated directly, SSAterm has been linked to detection of deviantterm sounds ( Ulanovsky et al., 2003 ), which may be facilitated by a relatively enhanced neuronal response to a change in the ongoing sound ( Nelken et al., 2003 ; Winkler et al., 2009 ; Grimm and Escera, 2012 ). By suppressing the responses to a frequently presented toneterm, the responses of neurons to a rare stimulus become relatively enhanced. However, whether and how modulating SSAterm in the auditory cortexterm affects auditory behavior has not yet been tested. Inhibitory interneurons may prove to have a complementary role in shaping auditory perception in addition to receptive field reorganization driven by synaptic plasticity ( Froemke et al., 2013 ). The use of optogeneticterm·mth methods to test the function of inhibitory interneurons in SSAterm overcomes the limitations of lesion or pharmacological studies ( Elliott and Trahiotis, 1972 ; Duque et al., 2014 ), which only allow for prolonged, non-selective inactivation ( Moore et al., 2001 ). By combining optogeneticterm·mth manipulation of interneuronterm activity with behavioral measurements, future experiments will explore whether interneuron-mediated SSAterm indeed affects the auditory behavior of the subject, such as enhanced ability to detect unexpected events.
