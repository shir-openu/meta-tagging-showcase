senseSignal-to-noise cutoff - average merged intensity divided by estimated error, conventionally cut around one to two but sensitive to error calibration — “3.2.1. Signal-to-noise ratio One obvious way to judge data significance is from the average signal-to-noise ratio of the merged intensities as a function of resolution. This is calculated as [after ‘correcting’ the σ′( I h l ) estimates; § 2.1], i.e. for each reflection h the average intensity over symmetry mates 〈 I h 〉 is divided by its estimated error σ(〈 I h 〉) and this ratio is averaged in resolution bins [reported as Mn(I/sd) in the program output]. Commonly used resolution-cutoff levels are typically in the range 1–2: even in a resolution bin with 〈 I /σ〉 = 1 a proportion of intensities are significantly above the noise level [∼5–7% I > 3σ, ∼20–25% negative]. 〈 I /σ〉 is a good criterion for resolution cutoff, but it does suffer from uncertainties in the estimation of σ( I ), both from inadequacies in the integration program and the necessary σ( I ) ‘correction’ (see § 2.1; Ian Tickle, in a private communication, has pointed out that the major correction applies to large intensities and therefore would not affect the weak high-resolution data relevant to determination of resolution cutoff). It should be noted that 〈 I /σ〉 is not independent of measures of internal consistency because the corrections to σ′( I ) are adjusted to match the scatter of observations. Thus, σ′( I ) estimates are still likely to be underestimates of the true standard deviations”
senseR-factor mismatch - merging R factors diverge as observations weaken whereas refinement R factors tend toward a constant — “3.2.2. Measures of internal consistency The traditional R factors measuring internal consistency, R merge or better R meas (§ 3), are not suitable measures for setting a resolution cutoff (Evans, 2011 ▶ ; Karplus & Diederichs, 2012 ▶ ), despite their apparent popularity with referees. As pointed out by Karplus and Diederichs, these R factors cannot be compared with the R factors in refinement, since R merge and R meas both tend to infinity as the data become weaker, while R cryst (either R work or R free ) tends to a constant (see Appendix A 1 and Luzzati, 1953 ▶ ). This means that there is no sensible way of setting a maximum acceptable level. Note that this is a difference between an R factor on intensity, which can be measured as zero or negative, and an R factor on amplitude | F |, which cannot be negative and will be biased positive if, for example, the TRUNCATE procedure of French & Wilson (1978 ▶ ) has been applied (or indeed any protocol which sets negative values to positive). R merge or R meas are useful metrics for monitoring variation with batch (§ 3.1), and their value for the strongest intensities (top intensity bin or low-resolution bin) is a good indicator of overall data quality. A large R factor for the strong data may indicate a problem [a serious cause for concern if R merge (strong) > 0.10; ideally it should be <0.05], but they are not good indicators for weak data. In general, since R factors are dependent on the distribution of the data (see, for example, Murshudov, 2011 ▶ ) they are not good indicators of model quality or internal consistency, whereas the correlation coefficients are indicators of the degree of linear dependence between data sets and are less dependent on the distribution of the data, so they may be better indicators”

conceptdata-scaling program — program class that places measurements on a common scale before merging — “1. Introduction Following integration of the spots on a set of X-ray diffraction images to produce a list of reflection intensities, a series of operations are performed on the data, usually referred to as ‘data reduction’. These processes include determination of the point group and if possible the space group, checking for consistent indexing where there are alternatives, putting all of the data on a common scale, deciding whether to reject parts of the collected data or to cut the resolution and estimating the structure amplitude | F | from intensity. Statistics on the internal consistency of the data also provide a good indication of the overall quality of the data set. Algorithms and methods for data reduction have been well documented in many papers ( e.g. Fox & Holmes, 1966 ▶ ; Otwinowski et al. , 2003 ▶ ; Evans, 2006 ▶ , 2011 ▶ ; Kabsch, 2010 ▶ ) and the details will not be repeated here. Scaling attempts to correct for contributions to the measured intensities arising from experimental conditions which vary during data collection, such as variations in the incident-beam intensity, the volume of the crystal illuminated, absorption in the primary or secondary beam and average radiation damage. This is performed by trying to make all symmetry-related or replicated measurements of a reflection intensity equal, i.e. to make the data as internally consistent as possible. This paper describes a new data-scaling program, AIMLESS , and discusses criteria for deciding the ‘resolution’ of a measured data set. In the CCP 4 context (Winn et al. , 2011 ▶ ), AIMLESS is used immediately after the program POINTLESS , which determines the likely point group and possible space group, as well as optionally combining data from multiple files each containing a ‘sweep’ of data and putting them on a common indexing system if necessary (Evans, 2011 ▶ ). 1 AIMLESS is then followed by CTRUNCATE , which calculates the structure amplitudes | F | from the intensities and outputs various intensity statistics mainly to detect twinning”

conceptscale factors — parameters putting intensities onto a common scale — “3. Analysis of data quality Once we have a set of scale factors to put all the intensities on a common scale and improved estimates of the error on each intensity, then we can analyse the data for internal consistency and for signal-to-noise ratio. We can do this as a function of image number (equivalent to time or crystal rotation) to detect radiation damage and weak or inconsistent regions of data and against resolution to decide on a high-resolution cutoff (see §4 ). Note that internal consistency measures are likely to underestimate the true error, since symmetry-related observations may suffer from the same systematic error. Internal consistency has traditionally been measured by R factors relating an individual observation I h l (after scaling) to the (weighted) average of all symmetry-related or replicate observations of the unique reflection h , 〈 I h 〉. The multiplicity-weighted R meas is an improvement over R merge , as it is relatively insensitive to data multiplicity (Diederichs & Karplus, 1997 ▶ ; Weiss & Hilgenfeld, 1997 ▶ ; Weiss, 2001 ▶ ), whereas R merge tends to increase with increasing multiplicity, even though the averaged intensities are improving. R p.i.m. provides an estimate of data quality after merging multiple observations. An alternative way of measuring internal consistency for the analysis against resolution is to split the observations randomly into halves and then calculate the linear correlation coefficient between the halves. This is arguably the most reliable measure and is discussed further below”

Note that internal consistency measures are likely to underestimate the true error, since symmetry-related observations may suffer from the same systematic error. Internal consistency has traditionally been measured by R factors relating an individual observation I h l (after scaling) to the (weighted) average of all symmetry-related or replicate observations of the unique reflection h , 〈 I h 〉. The multiplicity-weighted R meas is an improvement over R merge , as it is relatively insensitive to data multiplicity (Diederichs & Karplus, 1997 ▶ ; Weiss & Hilgenfeld, 1997 ▶ ; Weiss, 2001 ▶ ), whereas R merge tends to increase with increasing multiplicity, even though the averaged intensities are improving. R p.i.m. provides an estimate of data quality after merging multiple observations. An alternative way of measuring internal consistency for the analysis against resolution is to split the observations randomly into halves and then calculate the linear correlation coefficient between the halves. This is arguably the most reliable measure and is discussed further below”

conceptR factors — residual statistics used for internal consistency and refinement — “3.2.2. Measures of internal consistency The traditional R factors measuring internal consistency, R merge or better R meas (§ 3), are not suitable measures for setting a resolution cutoff (Evans, 2011 ▶ ; Karplus & Diederichs, 2012 ▶ ), despite their apparent popularity with referees. As pointed out by Karplus and Diederichs, these R factors cannot be compared with the R factors in refinement, since R merge and R meas both tend to infinity as the data become weaker, while R cryst (either R work or R free ) tends to a constant (see Appendix A 1 and Luzzati, 1953 ▶ ). This means that there is no sensible way of setting a maximum acceptable level. Note that this is a difference between an R factor on intensity, which can be measured as zero or negative, and an R factor on amplitude | F |, which cannot be negative and will be biased positive if, for example, the TRUNCATE procedure of French & Wilson (1978 ▶ ) has been applied (or indeed any protocol which sets negative values to positive). A large R factor for the strong data may indicate a problem [a serious cause for concern if R merge (strong) > 0.10; ideally it should be <0.05], but they are not good indicators for weak data. In general, since R factors are dependent on the distribution of the data (see, for example, Murshudov, 2011 ▶ ) they are not good indicators of model quality or internal consistency, whereas the correlation coefficients are indicators of the degree of linear dependence between data sets and are less dependent on the distribution of the data, so they may be better indicators”

conceptanisotropy — directional variation in diffraction-data quality and resolution — “3.2.3. Anisotropy Most data sets are anisotropic to some extent, which complicates analysis and decision making. The anisotropy of the data is analysed in AIMLESS using both CC 1/2 and 〈 I /σ〉. The two or three orthogonal principal directions of anisotropy are constrained by the lattice symmetry: no anisotropy for cubic symmetry, two principal directions, the unique c ( c *) axis and the ab plane, for tetragonal, hexagonal and trigonal, three orthogonal axes for orthorhombic, the unique b ( b *) axis and two orthogonal axes in the ac plane for monoclinic and three general orthogonal axes for the triclinic system. The general directions for triclinic and in the monoclinic ac plane are determined from the eigenvectors of a fitted anisotropic scale factor. Anisotropy is then analysed in cones around the principal axes (default semi-angle 20°) and within the same angle of a principal plane, or alternatively as projections onto the two or three principal directions. In the former case, observations are weighted with a cosine weight declining from 1 along the principal direction to 0 at the edge of the region. Plots of CC 1/2 and 〈 I /σ〉 then allow assessment of resolution in different directions in the same way as for the overall resolution and with the same difficulties”

conceptweak data — high-resolution observations whose usefulness is uncertain — “A major cause of user indecision and conflicts with journal referees is the resolution cutoff. We cannot set definite rules for this, as it depends on what the data are to be used for. It is therefore a mistake to prematurely apply a harsh cutoff at the data-reduction stage: data can always be excluded later. It is also unclear how best to handle anisotropy: do you choose the best direction or the worst? It is probably best to include data to the limit in the best direction. Tests carried out here to relate the resolution statistics to final model building and refinement do suggest that extending the data somewhat beyond the traditional limits such as 〈 I /σ〉 = 2 may improve structure determination, as do the ‘paired-refinement’ tests of Karplus & Diederichs (2012 ▶ ). At the very least, adding these weak data seems to do no harm for the purposes of either automatic or manual model building. The main problem is that we have become accustomed to using the nominal resolution as an indicator of model quality, and it is not a good indicator, particularly as important biological and chemical conclusions from a structure often depend on local rather than global correctness. Nor indeed can any global score can indicate the correctness of any local structural feature. Clearly, well measured data to 1.5 Å resolution contain more information than a data set to 3.5 Å resolution and are therefore likely to lead to a more correct structure, but nominal resolution in itself just tells us how many reflections were used, rather than their quality. From our limited tests here, it seems that changing the resolution cutoff over a considerable range ( e.g. from 2.2 to 1.9 Å) makes only a small difference, so the exact cutoff point is not a question to agonize over, but it seems sensible to set a generous limit so as not to exclude data containing real (if weak) information. Introduction

Following integration of the spots on a set of X-ray diffraction imagesterm to produce a list of reflection intensitiesterm, a series of operations are performed on the data, usually referred to as ‘data reductionterm’. These processes include determination of the point groupterm and if possible the space groupterm, checking for consistent indexingterm where there are alternatives, putting all of the data on a common scaleterm, deciding whether to reject parts of the collected data or to cut the resolutionterm and estimating the structure amplitude | F | from intensity. Statistics on the internal consistencyterm of the data also provide a good indication of the overall quality of the data set. Algorithms and methods for data reductionterm have been well documented in many papers ( e.g. Fox & Holmes, 1966 ▶ ; Otwinowski et al. , 2003 ▶ ; EvanspersName, 2006 ▶ , 2011 ▶ ; Kabsch, 2010 ▶ ) and the details will not be repeated here. Scaling attempts to correct for contributions to the measured intensities arising from experimental conditionsterm which vary during data collection, such as variations in the incident-beam intensityterm, the volume of the crystal illuminatedterm, absorption in the primary or secondary beamterm and average radiation damageterm. This is performed by trying to make all symmetry-related or replicated measurements of a reflection intensity equal, i.e. to make the data as internally consistent as possible.

This paper describes a new data-scaling programterm, AIMLESSterm , and discusses criteria for deciding the ‘resolutionterm’ of a measured data set. In the CCP 4 context (Winn et al. , 2011 ▶ ), AIMLESSterm is used immediately after the program POINTLESSterm , which determines the likely point groupterm and possible space groupterm, as well as optionally combining data from multiple files each containing a ‘sweep’ of data and putting them on a common indexing system if necessary (EvanspersName, 2011 ▶ ). 1 AIMLESSterm is then followed by CTRUNCATEterm , which calculates the structure amplitudes | F |term from the intensities and outputs various intensity statistics mainly to detect twinning.

2. The scaling program AIMLESSterm

AIMLESSterm is a new implementation in C++ of a classic scaling methodterm, designed to make it easy to add new scaling models and algorithms. It is a replacement for the earlier CCP 4 program SCALAterm (EvanspersName, 2006 ▶ , 2011 ▶ ) and at present uses a very similar scale modelterm. The inverse scaleterm g h l comes from the refined scale modelterm and is a function of the crystal rotation angleterm ϕ as a proxy for primary beam direction s 1 and for radiation doseterm (or time) and the secondary beam direction s 2 : g = g primary (ϕ)exp[−2 B (ϕ)sin 2 θ/λ 2 ] g secondary ( s 2 ). The smoothed primary scale factorsterm g primary (ϕ) and relative B factorsterm B (ϕ) are determined at suitable intervals in ϕ and are interpolated using Gaussian weightsterm. Parameter restraint termsterm (ties) include a sphericity restraintterm on g secondary ( s 2 ), tying all spheri­cal harmonic coefficients to zero and optional ties between adjacent primary scales and relative B factorsterm.

Note that the scaling process is generally hugely overdetermined (many more observations than parameters, e.g. 45 000 observations for 30 parameters) and that weak intensities do not contain much useful information about the scales, so that the scaling can be made faster (roughly linearly) by working with a selected subset of strong reflectionsterm. 2.1. Standard error estimatesterm

Initial estimates of the standard errorterm·var of each intensity observation, σ( I h l ), are generally underestimated by all integration programs, so AIMLESSterm , like SCALAterm , updates the σ( I ) estimates in an attempt to make the average standard errorterm·var match the average scatter of observations as a function of intensity only. The mismatch arises partly from the unknown ‘gain’ of the detector ( i.e. detector units per photon), which scales the Poisson-statistic error estimatesterm (as well as correcting for the detector point-spread function), and partly from a variety of instrumental instabilitiesterm which cause an increase in error with increasing intensity. If the error estimate were correct in explaining the observed discrepancies within the data set, then the normalized deviationsterm (where n h is the number of observations of reflection h and here I h l is the scale-corrected value, i.e. I h l / g h l ) should be distributed with a mean of 0.0 and a standard deviation of 1.0. 2 The standard errorterm·var estimate is then adjusted to optimizing the values of the ‘correction’ factors SdFacterm, SdBterm and SdAddterm to make variance(δ h l ) equal to 1.0 over all intensity ranges. The value of the term inside the square root is (arbitrarily) set to a minimum value of 0.1σ( I h l ) 2 to avoid possible negative values (a rare possibility). A separate set of correction factors is determined for each ‘run’ (usually, although an option is available to use the same value for all runs) and separately for fully recorded and partially recorded observations (if relevant). This is performed by minimizing summed over all intensity ranges i , currently with equal weights w i on each intensity binterm and loose restraints on SdBterm parameters to avoid extreme values. Final values of variance(δ h l ) as a function of intensity for each run are plotted in the output of the program as an indication of the success (or otherwise) of this ‘correction’. Sdfacterm can be identified primarily with the uncertainty in the detector gainterm and Sdaddterm with general instability factors which lead to an error proportional to intensity (see, for example, Diederichs, 2010 ▶ ), but SbB has no obvious physical interpretation. However, this factor helps to flatten the plot of variance(δ h l ) against intensity and may be justified as an empirical ‘correction’ factor. It should be noted that this ‘correction’ to the error estimates is a fairly crude approximation, as it assumes there are no major residual systematic errors (such as those arising from radiation damageterm, for example) and that the correction can be parameterized purely on intensity.

3. Analysis of data qualityterm

Once we have a set of scale factorsterm to put all the intensities on a common scaleterm and improved estimates of the error on each intensity, then we can analyse the data for internal consistencyterm and for signal-to-noise ratioterm. We can do this as a function of image numberterm (equivalent to time or crystal rotation) to detect radiation damageterm and weak or inconsistent regions of dataterm and against resolutionterm to decide on a high-resolution cutoff (see §4 ). Note that internal consistency measuresterm are likely to underestimate the true error, since symmetry-related observations may suffer from the same systematic error.

Internal consistencyterm has traditionally been measured by R factorsterm relating an individual observation I h l (after scaling) to the (weighted) average of all symmetry-related or replicate observations of the unique reflection h , 〈 I h 〉. The multiplicity-weighted R measterm is an improvement over R mergeterm , as it is relatively insensitive to data multiplicityterm (Diederichs & Karplus, 1997 ▶ ; Weiss & Hilgenfeld, 1997 ▶ ; Weiss, 2001 ▶ ), whereas R mergeterm tends to increase with increasing multiplicityterm, even though the averaged intensities are improving. R p.i.m.term provides an estimate of data qualityterm after merging multiple observations.

An alternative way of measuring internal consistencyterm for the analysis against resolutionterm is to split the observations randomly into halves and then calculate the linear correlation coefficientterm between the halves. This is arguably the most reliable measure and is discussed further below.

3.1. Analysis against ‘batch’ or image numberterm

Analysis of various parameters as a function of batch or image numberterm, a proxy for crystal rotation, time or radiation doseterm, is useful to determine whether the crystal has suffered unduly from radiation damageterm or whether there are any other parts of the data which should be discarded. AIMLESSterm plots similar graphs of R mergeterm and of scale and relative B factorsterm to those produced by SCALAterm (see, for example, Fig. 2 of EvanspersName, 2011 ▶ ). Increasing negative values of the relative B factor are an indicator of deterioration with dose, although the B factor is also affected by factors other than radiation damageterm. AIMLESSterm adds two new plots against batch numberterm: a rough estimate of the maximum resolutionterm for each image and a cumulative completenessterm (for all data and anomalous pairs; see Fig. 1 ▶ ). The ‘maximum resolutionterm’ is estimated from the point at which 〈 I /σterm( I )〉 falls below 1.0 for each batch: note that this 〈 I /σterm( I )〉 is without averaging multiple measurements (which would not generally occur on the same image), so will be smaller than the 〈 I /σterm〉 after averaging (§ 3.2.1). The plot shows the noisy values for each batch, as well as a smoothed plot typically averaged over a 5° range. This is only a rough estimate of resolutionterm (see § 4), but serves to illustrate any trends. The cumulative completenessterm plot helps in deciding whether cutting back data from the end because of radiation damageterm would compromise the completeness. Such decisions are more complicated if the data have been measured from multiple ‘sweeps’ or multiple crystals.

3.2. Analysis against resolutionterm

In order to estimate the useful ‘resolutionterm’ of the data, i.e. the resolutionterm at which the data may be truncated without losing significant information, AIMLESSterm plots various measures of signal to noise and internal consistencyterm against resolutionterm. There is at present no general consensus on the optimum criteria for interpretation of these plots and how to estimate the point at which adding additional high-resolution data is not adding anything useful: the true ‘resolutionterm’ of a data set has often been a point of contention with referees of papers.

3.2.1. Signal-to-noise ratioterm

One obvious way to judge data significance is from the average signal-to-noise ratioterm of the merged intensities as a function of resolutionterm. This is calculated as [after ‘correcting’ the σ′( I h l ) estimates; § 2.1], i.e. for each reflection h the average intensity over symmetry mates 〈 I h 〉 is divided by its estimated error σ(〈 I h 〉) and this ratio is averaged in resolution binsterm [reported as Mn(I/sd) in the program output]. Commonly used resolution-cutoff levelsterm are typically in the range 1–2: even in a resolutionterm bin with 〈 I /σterm〉 = 1 a proportion of intensities are significantly above the noise levelterm [∼5–7% I > 3σ, ∼20–25% negative]. 〈 I /σterm〉 is a good criterion for resolution cutoffterm, but it does suffer from uncertainties in the estimation of σ( I ), both from inadequacies in the integration program and the necessary σ( I ) ‘correction’ (see § 2.1; Ian Tickle, in a private communication, has pointed out that the major correction applies to large intensities and therefore would not affect the weak high-resolution dataterm relevant to determination of resolution cutoffterm). It should be noted that 〈 I /σterm〉 is not independent of measures of internal consistencyterm because the corrections to σ′( I ) are adjusted to match the scatter of observations. Thus, σ′( I ) estimates are still likely to be underestimates of the true standard deviations.

3.2.2. Measures of internal consistencyterm

The traditional R factorsterm measuring internal consistencyterm, R mergeterm or better R measterm (§ 3), are not suitable measures for setting a resolution cutoffterm (EvanspersName, 2011 ▶ ; Karplus & Diederichs, 2012 ▶ ), despite their apparent popularity with referees. As pointed out by Karplus and Diederichs, these R factorsterm cannot be compared with the R factorsterm in refinement, since R mergeterm and R measterm both tend to infinity as the data become weaker, while R crystterm (either R workterm or R freeterm ) tends to a constant (see Appendix A 1 and Luzzati, 1953 ▶ ). This means that there is no sensible way of setting a maximum acceptable level. Note that this is a difference between an R factor on intensity, which can be measured as zero or negative, and an R factor on amplitude | F |, which cannot be negative and will be biased positive if, for example, the TRUNCATE procedure of French & Wilson (1978 ▶ ) has been applied (or indeed any protocol which sets negative values to positive). R mergeterm or R measterm are useful metrics for monitoring variation with batch (§ 3.1), and their value for the strongest intensities (top intensity binterm or low-resolution bin) is a good indicator of overall data qualityterm. A large R factor for the strong data may indicate a problem [a serious cause for concern if R mergeterm (strong) > 0.10; ideally it should be <0.05], but they are not good indicators for weak dataterm. In general, since R factorsterm are dependent on the distribution of the dataterm (see, for example, MurshudovpersName, 2011 ▶ ) they are not good indicators of model qualityterm or internal consistencyterm, whereas the correlation coefficientsterm are indicators of the degree of linear dependence between data sets and are less dependent on the distribution of the dataterm, so they may be better indicators (see Appendix A 1 ).

A better measure for assessing the ‘resolutionterm’ of a data set is the correlation coefficientterm between random half data sets, CC 1/2term (labelled ‘CC_Imean’ in older versions of SCALAterm ; EvanspersName, 2006 ▶ , 2011 ▶ ; Karplus & Diederichs, 2012 ▶ ). This statistic is plotted against resolutionterm in both SCALAterm and AIMLESSterm (Fig. 2 ▶ ). A related statistic, Fourier shell correlationterm, has been used for assessing the resolutionterm of electron-microscopy reconstructions since the early 1980s (see, for example, Rosenthal & Henderson, 2003 ▶ ; Henderson et al. , 2012 ▶ ). The advantage of a correlation coefficientterm is that it has a well defined range: +1.0 for a good correlation and 0 for no correlation. CC 1/2term is generally close to 1 at low resolutionterm and falls sharply to near zero at higher resolutionterm as the intensities become weaker (Fig. 2 ▶ ).

3.2.3. Anisotropyterm

Most data sets are anisotropic to some extent, which complicates analysis and decision making. The anisotropyterm of the data is analysed in AIMLESSterm using both CC 1/2term and 〈 I /σterm〉. The two or three orthogonal principal directions of anisotropyterm are constrained by the lattice symmetry: no anisotropyterm for cubic symmetry, two principal directions, the unique c ( c *) axis and the ab plane, for tetragonal, hexagonal and trigonal, three orthogonal axes for orthorhombic, the unique b ( b *) axis and two orthogonal axes in the ac plane for monoclinic and three general orthogonal axes for the triclinic system. The general directions for triclinic and in the monoclinic ac plane are determined from the eigenvectors of a fitted anisotropic scale factorterm. Anisotropyterm is then analysed in cones around the principal axesterm (default semi-angle 20°) and within the same angle of a principal plane, or alternatively as projections onto the two or three principal directions. In the former case, observations are weighted with a cosine weight declining from 1 along the principal direction to 0 at the edge of the region. Plots of CC 1/2term and 〈 I /σterm〉 then allow assessment of resolutionterm in different directions in the same way as for the overall resolutionterm and with the same difficulties.

4. Tests of resolutionterm cutoffs

How can we decide where to apply a resolution cutoffterm? On the one hand, using high-resolution data which are so weak as to be insignificant will add nothing useful, may add unwanted noiseterm to maps and may lead us to overconfidence in the quality of our model (although the relationship between data qualityterm and model qualityterm is not clear). On the other hand, we do not want to exclude useful data which might aid structure solution and improve the final model. Anisotropyterm in the data complicates this decision, as it is not clear whether it is better to include data based on the best direction, the worst, or something in between. Anisotropic cutoffsterm are likely to cause artefacts in map calculationterm. The problem of anisotropic data and how to deal with them in refinement and map calculation is an open question, and future work needs to address this problem, with the goal of developing clear protocols.

To examine these questions, a number of tests were carried out using data integrated beyond what would normally be considered acceptable using the example data sets listed in Table 1 ▶ .

4.1. Comparison with simulated dataterm

By comparing refinement against measured data with refinement against simulated dataterm, we can judge the resolutionterm point at which the measured data become no better than simulated dataterm and compare this with the data-processing statisticsterm. These simulated intensities were converted to amplitudes (denoted F randomterm ) with the CCP 4 program CTRUNCATEterm using the same procedure as used for the experimental data (French & Wilson, 1978 ▶ ).

Following refinement against intensities, REFMACterm calculates R factorsterm in a way that mimics R factorsterm on F (MurshudovpersName et al. , 2011 ▶ ), but these R factorsterm rise sharply with resolutionterm (ultimately to infinity), rather than flattening out as do those on | F |.

Another estimate of the significance of the data is shown by the maximum-likelihood scale factor Dterm . The best electron densityterm after refinement is calculated using coefficients 2 mF o − DF c , where m is dependent on D . With increasing resolutionterm the R factorsterm and D values for experimental data converge towards the values from simulated dataterm, but the point at which they coalesce relative to the data-processing scores varies between different scores and different data sets.

4.2. Tests with automated model building

There have been anecdotal reports that extending the resolutionterm to include weak dataterm may help automated model-building proceduresterm. Unfortunately, this has been hard to prove: the examples tried here either worked at all resolutions or largely failed at all resolutions. Example 2 was used to test model building from a rather poor map with experimental phasesterm to 2.5 Å resolutionterm and model building with the Buccaneer / REFMAC pipelineterm (Cowtan, 2012 ▶ ) tested at resolutions of 2.3, 2.4, 2.5 and 2.6 Å. Models built at all of these resolutions had some correct and some incorrect parts, with the assigned sequence being largely wrong, but there was no consistent pattern over the different resolutions in the number of residues built or sequence assignment or in the correctness of the models. Another test was performed on example 3, a 2:1 complex, starting with a molecular-replacement modelterm with the two large molecules (595 residues each) and building the smaller 91-residue molecule. In this case the smaller component was built more or less consistently at any resolutionterm between 2.57 and 3.3 Å, although perhaps with fewer errors away from these extremes at between 2.7 and 3.1 Å. Similar results were obtained with example 4, omitting the last domain (207 residues): this could be rebuilt more or less successfully at resolutions between 1.8 and 2.8 Å, although maybe slightly more correctly at higher resolutions. However, extending the resolutionterm at least seemed to do no harm. It might indicate that the Buccaneer / REFMAC pipelineterm for model building at least is more dependent on phase errorterm rather than quality of structure-factor amplitudesterm (and hence resolutionterm). In cases of difficulty, it may be worth trying model building with data to different resolutions.

4.3. Electron densityterm in OMIT mapsterm

The effect of resolutionterm on the visual appearance of difference mapsterm was tested on regions of the model which were not included in refinement. This is important in manual building and completion of structures. Part of a structure was omitted, the remaining coordinates were perturbed by a random shift of up to 0.3 Å to reduce model biasterm and the structure was refined in REFMACterm with different resolutionterm cutoffs. Fig. 3 ▶ shows two examples of maximum-likelihood difference mapsterm at different resolutions for examples 4 and 1. There is not much difference between the maps, although there may be a slight improvement in sharpness in example 1 (Fig. 3 ▶ b ) on extending from 2.4 to 2.0 Å resolutionterm, consistent with the refinement results in § 4.1 (Fig. 2 ▶ a ). Again, as for the automated model building (§ 4.2), extending the resolutionterm seems at least to do no harm.

5. Discussion

The program AIMLESSterm performs essentially the same task as SCALAterm and gives similar results. However, it is significantly faster (about three times) and is a better framework for adding new scaling models and analyses. In due course, the three programs POINTLESSterm , AIMLESSterm and CTRUNCATEterm may be combined into one.

The extensive statistics produced by AIMLESSterm are mostly similar to those produced by SCALAterm , so the questions and decisions for the user are as discussed in EvanspersName (2011 ▶ ): (i) What is the point groupterm (Laue group)? (ii) What is the space groupterm? (iii) Is there radiation damageterm and should the most damaged regions of data be excluded? (iv) What resolution cutoffterm should be applied (see below)? (v) Is there a detectable anomalous signal? (vi) Are the data twinned? (vii) Is this data set better than those previously collected?

With regard to this last point, one traditional way of improving weak or incomplete data is to merge data from different crystals, provided that they are isomorphous. With the advent of cryocoolingterm, this has fallen out of fashion except for the most desperate cases, but recent work from Hendrickson’s group (Liu et al. , 2012 ▶ , 2013 ▶ ) makes a good case for merging data from many crystalsterm to enhance the very weak anomalous signal from sulfurterm. The current tools for checking isomorphismterm between crystals are rather undeveloped, but this technology is improving (see Giordano et al. , 2012 ▶ ). With the fast data collectionterm on modern synchrotrons, it is common to collect several or many more-or-less equivalent data sets and merging them may be better than just choosing the best. At the end, model qualityterm depends on data qualityterm and merging many data setsterm usually improves the signal-to-noise ratioterm in the data; however, it is not clear how to merge data when there is severe non-isomorphismterm or radiation damageterm. Blindly merging data may do more harm than good. It is necessary to analyse the joint distribution of all data setsterm and to merge using this distribution. In an ideal world all data sets would be used without merging, thus ensuring that the extraction of information from the data would be optimal at all stages of structure analysis: the distribution of the dataterm and the amount of information available at each stage of analysis would define what needs to be used.

A major cause of user indecision and conflicts with journal referees is the resolution cutoffterm. We cannot set definite rules for this, as it depends on what the data are to be used forterm. It is therefore a mistake to prematurely apply a harsh cutoff at the data-reduction stage: data can always be excluded later. It is also unclear how best to handle anisotropyterm: do you choose the best direction or the worst? It is probably best to include data to the limit in the best direction. Tests carried out here to relate the resolutionterm statistics to final model building and refinement do suggest that extending the data somewhat beyond the traditional limits such as 〈 I /σterm〉 = 2 may improve structure determinationterm, as do the ‘paired-refinementterm’ tests of Karplus & Diederichs (2012 ▶ ). At the very least, adding these weak dataterm seems to do no harm for the purposes of either automatic or manual model building. The main problem is that we have become accustomed to using the nominal resolutionterm as an indicator of model qualityterm, and it is not a good indicator, particularly as important biological and chemical conclusions from a structure often depend on local rather than global correctness. Nor indeed can any global score can indicate the correctness of any local structural featureterm. Clearly, well measured data to 1.5 Å resolutionterm contain more information than a data set to 3.5 Å resolutionterm and are therefore likely to lead to a more correct structure, but nominal resolutionterm in itself just tells us how many reflections were used, rather than their quality. From our limited tests here, it seems that changing the resolution cutoffterm over a considerable range ( e.g. from 2.2 to 1.9 Å) makes only a small difference, so the exact cutoff point is not a question to agonize over, but it seems sensible to set a generous limitterm so as not to exclude data containing real (if weak) information. There is no reason to suppose that cutting back the resolutionterm of the data will improve the model. These tests were performed with current programs and our current procedures at all stages could be improved to extract the maximum information from weak noisy dataterm.
