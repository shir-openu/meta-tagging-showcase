Introduction Electronics with tissue-like properties enable a natural integration between electronic functionality and the biological world. Such integration is increasingly important for treating numerous medical diseases like Parkinson’s, for which electrical deep-brain stimulation has proven to be highly effective 1 . Moreover, electronic interfaces with biological tissue provide valuable scientific and diagnostic insights into complex medical phenomena, ranging from atrial fibrillation 2 to Alzheimer’s 3 , 4 . However, conventional conductive materials have difficulty forming long-term stable and conformal interfaces with many biological tissues due to severe mechanical mismatchterm·dom between the two material types. For example, brain tissue typically has elastic moduliterm·dom < 1 kPa 5 , whereas common electrode materials like silicon and tungsten have moduli of 50 GPa and 130 GPa, respectively 6 . Even conducting polymersterm·dom (CPsterm·dom), which have superior flexibility compared to inorganic conductors, typically have moduli in the 1 GPa range 6 . Furthermore, interfacing materials should be stretchable enough to maintain conformal contact with dynamic tissue surfaces. For example, the surface of the brain has an intrinsic displacement of over 10 μm from respiration alone 6 . Mechanical mismatchterm·dom of tissue-interfacing materials can lead to reduced efficacyterm·dom of both recording and stimulation, which may be further exacerbated by immunological responses that lead to scarring 6 – 8 . Thus, there is a strong demand for materials that can be rationally designed to possess mechanical properties that mimic tissue, without compromising their electronic performance. Ideally, such materials should be able to be tuned to form mechanically compliant interfaces with a wide range of biological tissues, from ultra-soft tissues such as the brain (0.5–1 kPa) 5 to stiffer tissues such as the skin 9 and certain regions of the heart (100–500 kPa) 10 .

To mimic the mechanical properties of these tissues, hydrogelsterm·dom are a promising class of synthetic materialsterm·dom due to their high water content (70–99 wt%) that is similar to tissue 11 . Electrically conducting hydrogelsterm·dom made with CPsterm·dom, including poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) polystyrene sulfonateterm (PEDOT:PSSterm) 12 – 15 , polyanilineterm (PANIterm) 16 – 18 , and polypyrroleterm (PPyterm) 19 – 22 have been reported, although there are few examples of mechanically robust gels that can stretch to conform to dynamic tissue surfaces. Among these CPsterm·dom, PEDOT:PSSterm is advantageous because of its biocompatibility 23 , 24 , and because it is commercially available in its doped form with reproducibly high conductivityterm·key, thus avoiding problems with batch to batch variation from in situ polymerization 25 and the use of alternative dopants that are less effective than PSS 26 . Reported examples of stretchable conductive hydrogelsterm·dom based on PEDOT:PSSterm either rely on in situ polymerization of EDOT within an inert hydrogelterm·dom matrix 13 , 14 , 27 , or involve blending PEDOT:PSSterm with hydrogel-forming precursors 15 . However, these strategies suffer from low conductivityterm·key (0.01–2.2 S m −1 ) with relatively high PEDOT:PSSterm content up to 30 wt%, which makes it difficult to selectively tune the mechanical properties of the gel 13 – 15 .

We hypothesize that the low performance of these gels arises from limited control of the CP network morphology, resulting in largely disconnected aggregates that require high loadings of CP to meet the percolation thresholdterm·dom for electronic conduction. Accordingly, we hypothesize that using gelationterm·key to ensure CP network connectivity should result in improved conductivityterm·key. Recently, it has been reported that PEDOT:PSSterm can form gels directly from aqueous solution, either by increasing the ionic strengthterm·dom 28 , increasing concentration 28 , or lowering pH 12 . Although these gels behave like solids, on their own they are highly brittle and difficult to handle. Still, the ability to form gels directly from commercially available PEDOT:PSSterm solutions presents a unique opportunity to control CP network connectivity to improve electronic conductivityterm·key.

In this work, we demonstrate a method for making conducting interpenetrating networksterm·dom (C-IPNsterm·dom) by infiltrating a loosely-crosslinked PEDOT:PSSterm gel with the precursors for a secondary polymer network (Fig. 1a ). The controlled gelationterm·key of commercially available PEDOT:PSSterm enables us to achieve record-high conductivities up to 23 S m −1 , while the fact that PEDOT:PSSterm can gel at a low solids content of 1.1 wt% enables us to orthogonally control the mechanical properties of the C-IPNterm·dom by tuning the secondary network properties. Using this method, we demonstrate that we can fabricate gels with ultra-soft moduli over three biologically relevant orders of magnitude (8–374 kPa) without compromising conductivityterm·key ( > 10 S m −1 ) or stretchabilityterm·key ( > 100%). Synthesis and characterization of C-IPNterm·dom gels Although the PEDOT:PSSterm hydrogelsterm·dom behave similar to solids, they are also highly brittle. To improve mechanical properties, we selected polyacrylic acidterm (PAActerm) as a secondary polymer network due to its biocompatibility 30 , 31 and high concentration of hydrogen bonding, which we hypothesized could further mechanically reinforce the C-IPNterm·dom. In addition, aqueous PEDOT:PSSterm solution has been shown to gel when exposed to strong acids, and we surmised that infiltrating the PEDOT:PSSterm hydrogelterm·dom with acidic monomers would result in additional reinforcement of the PEDOT:PSSterm matrix 12 . Finally, PAActerm hydrogelsterm·dom can be easily tuned by varying the concentration of monomers in water and the ratio of bisacrylamide to acrylic acid, which controls the degree of covalent crosslinking (Supplementary Figure 1 ).

To infiltrate the PEDOT:PSSterm gels with PAActerm monomers, the gels were soaked in an aqueous solution of acrylic acid, bisacrylamide, and a thermal radical polymerization initiator that activates above 60 °C. As expected, the acidity of the acrylic acid caused the PEDOT:PSSterm gels to shrink slightly during the exchange process (Supplementary Figure 2b ), which qualitatively strengthened the PEDOT:PSSterm/acrylic acid composite even before the acrylic acid was polymerized. Finally, the acrylic acid network was polymerized within the PEDOT:PSSterm gel by placing the gel in a sealed container in an oven at 70 °C for 30 min. The thermal initiator was selected instead of a UV initiator due to its relatively fast kinetics and insensitivity to oxygen, and because it allows acrylic acid to be polymerized within the bulk PEDOT:PSSterm gel, which is opaque due to its electronically conducting nature.

To confirm the presence of the secondary network inside the C-IPNterm·dom, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy was performed on C-IPNterm·dom and compared with PEDOT:PSSterm and PAActerm hydrogelsterm·dom (Fig. 1f ). Peaks attributed to carboxylic acid and hydrogen bonding between carboxylic acid groups can be identified in both PAActerm and C-IPNterm·dom samples, indicating that the PAActerm successfully was incorporated. In addition, scanning electron microscopic (SEM) imaging on the cross-section of a lyophilized C-IPNterm·dom sample confirms that the gel is homogeneous and porous (Fig. 1e ). Finally, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopyterm·mth (XPSterm·mth) measurements on PEDOT:PSSterm and C-IPNterm·dom suggest that the PEDOT:PSSterm network is chemically unaffected by the presence of PAActerm. In XPSterm·mth spectra of PEDOT:PSSterm, two S2p peaks correspond to the presence of sulphur in PEDOT and in PSS, respectively. The relative size of these two peaks is proportional to the ratio of PEDOT to PSS, whereas the locations of the peaks correspond to the binding energy of the sulphur in its two respective chemical environments 29 , 32 . No significant change in peak area or position was observed in the PEDOT:PSSterm after PAActerm was introduced (Supplementary Figure 2a ).

Although ionic liquid is used to induce PEDOT:PSSterm gelationterm·key, we wanted to remove it from the final C-IPNterm·dom gel because of its cytotoxicity. To facilitate diffusion of ionic liquid out of the gel, we soaked the PEDOT:PSSterm gels in deionized (DI) water and exchanged the water multiple times before infiltrating the PEDOT:PSSterm with acrylic acid. For our system, fluorine is a unique marker for the anionic component of the ionic liquid, yet no fluorine could be detected by XPSterm·mth on the C-IPNterm·dom. To confirm that the cationic component is also removed, we also performed 1 H-nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy on the solution obtained from washing the PEDOT:PSSterm gels with DI water and compared it with a spectrum of the neat ionic liquid (Supplementary Figure 3 ). The cation is clearly present in the wash solution. The broad peaks around 6.5–7.5 p.p.m. correspond to aromatic rings which are unique to PSS, suggesting that some PSS is also removed from washing the gel. The slight shifts in the peak at 6.2 p.p.m., which is attributed to the cation hydroxyl, may be due to the presence of PSS, since the rate of proton exchange between the hydroxyl group and solvent is highly dependent on acidic environment. Taken together, the XPSterm·mth and NMR data suggest that the ionic liquid can be easily removed from washing, which is expected given the highly porous nature of the hydrogelterm·dom.

The final C-IPNterm·dom gels continue to swell when immersed in water, equilibrating at a final weight that depends on the gel formulation. The formulation with highest solid content (C-IPNterm·dom 1) swells to approximately two times its original weight, whereas the formulation with the lowest solid content (C-IPNterm·dom 5) swells to nearly seven times its original weight (Supplementary Figure 8a ). The gels can also be dried and re-swelled successfully in water. Interestingly, the final weight of the gels after re-swelling exceeds the equilibrium weight of as-synthesized C-IPNterm·dom gels immersed in water, with the re-swelled weight ratio also dependent on the gel formulation. The formulation with highest solid content (C-IPNterm·dom 1) has a final re-swollen weight that is 1.3 times its equilibrium swelled weight prior to drying, whereas the formulation with lowest solid content (C-IPNterm·dom 5) has a final re-swollen weight that is nearly 6 times the equilibrium swelled weight (Supplementary Figure 8a ). This observation suggests that drying the C-IPNterm·dom hydrogelsterm·dom results in a reconfiguration of the polymer morphology that changes its swelling ability. Drying the hydrogelsterm·dom may encourage π–π stacking interactions between PEDOT:PSSterm that collapse the conducting polymerterm·dom network into aggregates, leaving larger continuous void fractions for water to penetrate during the re-swelling process. This hypothesis is supported by the fact that PEDOT:PSS-only hydrogelsterm·dom made directly from mixing with ionic liquid also re-swell to 1.8 ± 0.5 times their original weight after drying. Importantly, the re-swelled gels retain the same high stretchabilityterm·key and low modulus of the as-made gels (Supplementary Figure 8b ).

To characterize the mechanical properties of C-IPNterm·dom hydrogelsterm·dom, stress strain measurements were recorded under tensile elongation. Based on the formulation, the C-IPNterm·dom hydrogelsterm·dom have a wide range of elastic moduliterm·dom ranging from 8 kPa to nearly 400 kPa (Table 1 ). Electrical conductivityterm·key of C-IPNterm·dom gels was calculated from measuring the resistance of the gels using a four-point probe methodterm·mth. For all formulations, the conductivityterm·key was greater than 10 S m −1 . The highest conductivityterm·key was 23 ± 5.6 S m −1 and conductivityterm·key tended to decrease as a function of acrylic acid concentration in the PAActerm hydrogelterm·dom precursor solution (Table 1 ). The fact that the electrical conductivityterm·key could be maintained above 10 S m −1 despite large variations in elastic moduliterm·dom is likely due to the ability for aqueous PEDOT:PSSterm solutions to form connected CP networks at a low weight concentration relative to acrylic acid. Indeed, the weight percent of PEDOT:PSSterm in the final gel is significantly lower than previously reported stretchable PEDOT:PSSterm hydrogelsterm·dom (Supplementary Table 1 ). Although connectivity of conducting domains is critical to achieving high electrical conduction 35 , mechanical properties of composites, such as elastic modulus, are largely dictated by the relative amount of each component 36 , 37 . Thus, a composite material that is predominately made up of a soft material will be soft, even if it contains a small amount of rigid domains. Analogously, because of the dilute microgel structure of PEDOT:PSSterm in aqueous solutions, the relatively small amount of PEDOT:PSSterm needed to form a connected conductive pathway in C-IPNterm·dom enables its mechanical properties to be nearly orthogonally controlled by the non-conductive PAActerm network. This represents a clear advantage over other conductive, stretchable hydrogelsterm·dom that have been synthesized by polymerizing EDOT within an electrically inert hydrogelterm·dom matrix, wherein decent conductivities are only achieved with concentrations as high as 30 wt%.

Although the conductivityterm·key of C-IPNterm·dom stays within the same order of magnitude despite significantly different elastic moduliterm·dom, slight differences in conductivities can be attributed to differences in acrylic acid concentration, which is consistent with the different degrees of shrinking visually observed during the PAActerm infiltration step. The fact that the conductivityterm·key does not vary significantly suggests that these differences in PEDOT:PSSterm gel strengthterm·dom are small compared to enhancement in conductivityterm·key that comes from forming a connected conductive network. This finding makes sense given that, in conductive composites, the most dramatic increase in conductivityterm·key typically occurs at the percolation thresholdterm·dom 38 , 39 .

Given that the acidic monomers apparently interact with PEDOT:PSSterm, our infiltration method is required for them to effectively penetrate the PEDOT:PSSterm gel form a homogeneous blend. By contrast, directly blending PEDOT:PSSterm with acrylic acid resulted in rapid phase separation, probably due to the acidity of the monomers (Supplementary Figure 6a ). Although the mixture could be polymerized to form a soft gel with moderate stretchabilityterm·key (Supplementary Figure 6b ), the conductivityterm·key of the blend was significantly lower, at 0.21 S m −1 . As expected, this value is in the same range of conductivities reported in the recent work of Wu et al. 15 , who directly blended PEDOT:PSSterm with the monomers used to make mechanically reinforcing inert secondary networks (0.2–2.2 S m −1 ) 15 . In addition, the modulus and strain at break of PEDOT:PSSterm/PAActerm hydrogelsterm·dom made by blending were lower than for hydrogelsterm·dom made with PAActerm only, probably because a significant amount of monomer is lost when it crashes out with PEDOT:PSSterm upon blending (Supplementary Figure 6c ). Thus, we believe that our infiltration method enables a greater degree of orthogonal control over the selection of the two interpenetratingterm·key networks in the conductive gel. This feature also enables us to incorporate alternative secondary networks, besides PAActerm, into C-IPNterm·dom. For instance, composite hydrogelsterm·dom made by infiltrating PEDOT:PSSterm gels with polyacrylamideterm (PAAmterm) precursors exhibited mechanical properties that closely resembled gels made with PAAmterm alone (Supplementary Figure 7 ).

This combination of properties makes C-IPNterm·dom hydrogelsterm·dom promising for integration into wearable and implantable devices, for which the ability to couple high electronic conductivityterm·key with low modulus and high stretchabilityterm·key is particularly desired at soft biological interfaces such as the brain. In addition, the ability to tune our gel’s mechanical properties without compromising its conductivityterm·key makes it an attractive materials platform for tissue engineering and cell culture, since it is well known that cells are highly responsive to the mechanical properties of their surrounding environment 46 , 47 . C-IPNterm·dom hydrogelsterm·dom offer a route to enable electrical stimulation and recording while preserving the appropriate three-dimensional architecture and matched mechanical properties needed to mimic human tissue in vitro and to support cell viability.

In the future, C-IPNterm·dom can be integrated into more application-specific devices by taking advantage of the fact that it can be easily molded into different shapes and geometries for different target applications. Although there may be a lower resolution limit given by the approximate PEDOT:PSSterm microgel size of 250 nm 28 , sub-micrometer patterning is unnecessary for many biomedical applications. For instance, deep-brain stimulation electrodes typically have surface areas on the order of 1 mm 2 48 . By contrast, we have demonstrated size resolution down to 10 μm using a simple mold casting method. Furthermore, the fact that C-IPNterm·dom can be dried and re-swelled means that it can be compatibly integrated with other materials that may require dry or non-aqueous environments for processing. With their processability, mechanical tunability, and excellent electronic properties, C-IPNterm·dom gels are a highly versatile electronic material for future bio-interfacing applications.

Synthesis of C-IPNterm·dom hydrogelsterm·dom To induce gelationterm·key of PEDOT:PSSterm, ionic liquid was slowly added to the filtered PEDOT:PSSterm dispersion while stirring. Subsequently, the PEDOT:PSSterm/ionic liquid mixtures were poured into molds, briefly degassed, and then sealed and placed in a 70 °C oven for 12 h to ensure complete gelationterm·key.
