claim-typeexistence
transferfrom machine learning / deep learning (text sequence modelling): an LSTM plus convolutional-neural-network architecture, imported to classify students' coded learning-behaviour sequences — “Long short-term memory (LSTM) and the convolutional neural network (CNN) algorithm architecture were applied to identify behavioral sequences based on the data.”
transferfrom cognitive psychology: working memory as a capacity limit, used to define germane load and thereby to ground an instructional-design construct in a memory construct — “Germane cognitive load is defined as the amount of working memory capacity required for managing intrinsic load.”
mechanismCollaborating and discussing with peers distributes the information-processing burden between students, which lowers each student's mental load and frees effort for performance — “When they discussed scientific issues or provided assistance to each other, they shared the burden of information processing with each other, which decreased their mental load.”
mechanismThe transaction costs of collaborating are themselves effortful, so heavy interaction can push cognitive processing into overload rather than relieving it — “When communication and collaboration require students to invest considerable mental effort, high transaction costs in interactions may overload their cognitive process.”
mechanismcognitive load is treated as multi-dimensional, so a learner can be high on one dimension and low on another - which is why a single scale hides the types — “Cognitive load is a multi-dimensional cognitive construct representing load when doing a particular task (Paas & van Merriënboer, 1994 ).”
mechanismextraneous load comes from how the material was designed rather than from its difficulty, so it is the component an instructor can remove — “Extraneous cognitive load is caused by the poor design of instructional materials, which requires extra cognitive handling (Kirschner et al., 2006 ).”
mechanismself-efficacy is proposed as the link between load and outcome: a sense of competence assists the cognitive processing itself — “students with high self-efficacy may have a powerful sense of competence, which may assist in cognitive processes (Lin et al., 2020 ), academic performance (Wang et al., 2018 ), and achievement (Chang et al., 2018 )”
mechanismAR adds load as well as benefit - multitasking between the virtual and real layers pulls attention off the instructions — “In addition, multitasking is another issue related to the AR learning system, which can be a distraction factor that will lead students to ignore the instructions in their learning process (Garzón et al., 2019 ).”
relation — “Therefore, this study included self-efficacy as a factor to identify the underlying subgroups in AR-assisted science learning regarding students’ cognitive load, unlike previous studies (e.g. Thees et al., 2020 ), which only proved the effect of an AR-assisted learning system on science learning performance and cognitive load.”
relation — “This result is consistent with Lin et al.’s ( 2020 ) verified assumptions that hands-on activity using AR boosts students’ understanding of scientific concepts.”
relation — “This study provides further evidence for the study of Chin and Wang ( 2021 ), who revealed that AR technology could provide different authentic learning activities for students to understand the difficulties (e.g. cognitive overload) in learning.”
relation — “First, Sweller ( 1988 ) identified three types of cognitive load—intrinsic, extraneous, and germane—focusing on the interpretation of performance and task-based measures.”

replicationuntested
limitationThe profiles rest on only three self-reported cognitive-load dimensions within AR-based activities — “The first is that the AR cognitive load learning profiles were focused on AR-based learning activities and three types of cognitive load: mental load, mental effort, and performance.”
limitationObjective process measures such as eye and hand movement tracking were not collected and are needed to characterise the visual and motor behaviour behind the cognitive load — “Notably, other measures of cognitive processes, such as eye and hand movement tracking, may need to be considered for future study to elucidate understanding of the students’ visual and movement behavior, resulting in their cognitive load in the AR-assisted science learning environment.”
limitationThe literature it builds on disagrees with itself about the sign of the effect - more load has been reported as better, worse and neutral. — “For example, students processing more cognitive load may perform better (Lai et al., 2019 ; Thees et al., 2020 ), less favorably (Sung et al., 2014 ; Thees et al., 2020 ), or with no significant difference (Hwang et al., 2018 ; Wu et al., 2018 ) between the experimental and control groups.”
limitationThe three classical load types are stated to be an incomplete account, and the paper adopts a second framework alongside them rather than resolving the conflict. — “However, these three classical types only give a partial picture of cognitive load (Xie & Salvendy, 2000 ), and do not properly capture the mental effort-based aspect of cognitive load (Feldon, 2007 ).”
limitationOne cohort, one country, one task - 365 Chinese undergraduates in a single AR activity. — “a latent profile analysis confirmed that 365 Chinese undergraduates carried diverse dimensions of cognitive load simultaneously”

findingLatent profile analysis of three cognitive-load dimensions sorted the undergraduates into four profiles, named Low Engagement, Immersive, Dabbling and Organized. — “The latent profile analysis findings revealed four fundamental profiles: Low Engagement, Immersive, Dabbling, and Organized, characterized as carrying various respective cognitive loads.”
findingThe Immersive profile (100 students) combined the highest mental effort with the lowest mental load and the second-highest performance of the four profiles. — “In profile 2, 100 participants attained the highest mean values for mental effort, second-highest mean values for performance, and lowest mean values for mental load across the four profiles.”
findingImmersive-profile students reported the highest science learning self-efficacy of any profile. — “Students with the Immersive profile had the highest science learning self-efficacy.”
findingLow Engagement students scored lowest on every self-efficacy dimension measured. — “Low Engagement students displayed the lowest self-efficacy among all dimensions.”
findingOrganized-profile students outscored Dabbling students specifically on conceptual understanding and higher-order cognitive skills. — “Organized students recorded better conceptual understanding and higher-order cognitive skills than Dabbling ones.”
findingProfiles whose behaviour sequences included discussing with peers, taking tests and reading feedback had significantly higher self-efficacy than profiles whose behaviour was mainly taking tests and visiting resources. — “Among them, profiles with social interaction, test, and reviewing feedback behavior had a significantly higher score for self-efficacy than those patterns mainly based on test learning and resource visits.”
findingThe four-profile solution was preferred because it had the lowest AIC and adjusted BIC once the five-profile model was ruled out for containing a group under 10% of participants. — “Excluding the five-profile model, Akaike information criterion and adjusted Bayesian information criterion were the lowest for the four-profile model among all other models.”
findingDabbling and Organized students differed significantly only on conceptual understanding and higher-order cognitive skills, not on the other four self-efficacy dimensions. — “Moreover, post hoc tests indicated that only profiles 3 and 4 were not significantly different in the dimensions of AR science learning self-efficacy, excluding conceptual understanding and higher-order cognitive skills.”
findingThe Organized profile achieved the highest performance of all four profiles while carrying high mental effort and only medium mental load, alongside more communicative and collaborative behaviour. — “Students with the Organized profile showed the highest performance among other profiles, with a high level of mental effort, medium level of mental load, and more communicative and collaborative behaviors.”
findingOrganized and Dabbling students, though similar in mental load, differed behaviourally in that Organized students finished tasks progressively while Dabbling students re-browsed materials and retried tasks with new answers. — “Based on behavior patterns, it appeared that the key difference between the two profiles was that students in profile 4 progressively finished the tasks, while those in profile 3 kept repeatedly browsing learning materials and trying to complete the tasks with new answers.”
findingLearners fall into four cognitive-load types rather than varying along one dimension. — “The latent profile analysis findings revealed four fundamental profiles: Low Engagement, Immersive, Dabbling, and Organized, characterized as carrying various respective cognitive loads.”
findingThe profiles differ in self-efficacy, with Low Engagement lowest on every dimension and Immersive highest overall. — “Students with the Immersive profile had the highest science learning self-efficacy.”
findingOrganized learners outperformed Dabbling ones on conceptual understanding and higher-order skills. — “Organized students recorded better conceptual understanding and higher-order cognitive skills than Dabbling ones.”
findingWhat students actually did differed by profile, and the sequences involving social interaction and reviewing feedback went with higher self-efficacy than test-and-browse sequences. — “Among them, profiles with social interaction, test, and reviewing feedback behavior had a significantly higher score for self-efficacy than those patterns mainly based on test learning and resource visits.”
