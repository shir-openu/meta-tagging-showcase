phenomenonrepetition priming and repetition suppression— “Stimulus repetition in identification tasks leads to improved behavioral performance ("repetition priming") but attenuated neural responses ("repetition suppression") throughout task-engaged cortical regions.”
claim-typemechanism— “In this Discussion Paper, we review four different theoretical proposals that have the potential to link repetition suppression and priming, with a particular focus on a proposal that stimulus repetition affects improved efficiency through enhanced neural synchronization.”
method — evidence— “Below, we review four of the main theoretical proposals about what form these solutions might take (see also Grill-Spector et al., 2006 ).”
mechanismRepetition makes task-engaged cells fire more synchronously even as their average rates fall, so processing becomes more efficient and behaviour improves despite reduced activity.— “A very different proposal that may help to resolve this puzzle is that as cells are firing at lower overall rates, they are firing more synchronously with one another, leading to more efficient neural processing ( Gilbert, Gotts, Carver, & Martin, 2010 ; Gotts, 2003 ) ( Figure 1C ).”
mechanismCoincident input spikes sum on the receiving cell's membrane before the depolarization decays, so synchronous inputs cross spike threshold more reliably than the same number of asynchronous inputs.— “Small depolarizations that occur simultaneously (i.e. synchronously) in a receiving cell will be much more likely to sum above the voltage threshold needed to evoke an action potential.”
mechanismIn the Bayesian alternative, learned top-down predictions inhibit the bottom-up sensory evidence, so the residual prediction error - and hence the measured activity - shrinks with repetition.— “This process is commonly referred to in the literature on Bayesian networks as "explaining away" (e.g. Pearl, 1988 ), since as the appropriate causes of the sensory evidence are learned, the incorrect causes (i.e. prediction error) are reduced and explained away.”
contradicts the Facilitation model (Henson, 2003; James et al., 2000)— “Having reviewed four basic proposals as to how repetition suppression might afford repetition priming, the only view that we consider definitively ruled out by current data is the Facilitation Model, at least in its existing form.”
replicates Gilbert et al. (2010)— “We found that stimulus repetition led to repetition priming in both patients and increases in low-frequency evoked power (1–15 Hz) for virtually all task-engaged electrodes (i.e. those that exhibited significant evoked responses), replicating the basic pattern of Gilbert et al. (2010) .”
builds-on Gotts (2003) neocortical circuit model— “In a simplified neocortical circuit model that incorporated biologically proportionate numbers of excitatory and inhibitory cells and short-term plasticity mechanisms, Gotts (2003) showed that it was possible to simultaneously address short-lag repetition suppression and priming effects through enhanced synchronization.”
senseTHE PARADOX IN ONE SENTENCE, and it is a SIGN CONFLICT between two measures of one event: `Stimulus repetition in identification tasks leads to IMPROVED behavioral performance (repetition priming) but ATTENUATED neural responses (repetition suppression).` Behaviour gets better while the brain does less. Every mechanism in this paper exists to make those two facts one fact, and a corpus that recorded only `repetition improves performance` would lose the half that makes it interesting— “Stimulus repetition in identification tasks leads to improved behavioral performance ("repetition priming") but attenuated neural responses ("repetition suppression") throughout task-engaged cortical regions”
senseFOUR ACCOUNTS OF ONE RELATIONSHIP, and the review's job is to keep them separate rather than to pick: FACILITATION (activity advanced in time - refuted for the typical case by single-cell recording, which finds firing curves to repeated stimuli SUBSUMED UNDER those to novel ones), SHARPENING (the poorly tuned cells drop out), SYNCHRONY (lower rates, tighter timing), and EXPLAINING AWAY (the Bayesian form of facilitation). Everyone agrees on improved efficiency; `the exact form that it takes remains elusive`— “we review four different theoretical proposals that have the potential to link repetition suppression and priming, with a particular focus on a proposal that stimulus repetition affects improved efficiency through enhanced neural”
senseA REVIEW WHOSE VERDICT ON ITS OWN FAVOURITE IS A GAP: `Despite the promise that the Synchrony model holds for resolving the puzzle of repetition priming and repetition suppression, THERE ARE RELATIVELY FEW STUDIES THAT HAVE EVALUATED IT EMPIRICALLY.` The title says the same - `Multiple mechanisms IN NEED OF TESTING`. The contribution is an inventory of untested explanations, and the paper is explicit that its preferred one is preferred on promise rather than on evidence— “Despite the promise that the Synchrony model holds for resolving the puzzle of repetition priming and repetition suppression, there are relatively few studies that have evaluated it empirically”
controla CONTROL CONDITION against which spike-LFP coherence in the gamma range was compared — held — rules out that the synchronisation change is a general property of the recording rather than an effect of stimulus repetition— “Spikes elicited by the test grating were simultaneously more synchronized with the local field potential (LFP) (spike-LFP coherence) in the gamma frequency range (30–80 Hz) relative to a control condition in which the adapting grating was replaced with a random dot patch of matched luminance.”
controlthe extreme form of the Explaining Away account was tested against the observed pattern — held — rules out that ALL repetition suppression is high-level repetition expectation - the extreme version predicts a pattern the data do not show— “This would appear to rule out the extreme version of the Explaining Away view in which all repetition suppression effects are explained by relatively high-level repetition expectation.”
