The unmodified PRterm·dom, which consists of α-CDsterm and PEGterm, is soluble only in a few solvents, such as DMSO, some ionic liquids, mixtures of organic amides and lithium salts and high-pH aqueous solutions. It is insoluble in pure water because the α-CDterm molecules aggregate due to hydrogen bonding between the hydroxyl groups on α-CDsterm 24 . Thus, α-CDsterm could not move along the PEGterm chains, and the pulley effectterm·dom was restricted in water. As a result, the PRterm·dom slide-ring gelterm·dom appeared opaque and did not exhibit high extensibility. In high-pH aqueous solutions, such as a sodium hydroxide (NaOH) aqueous solution, the α-CDsterm do not aggregate through hydrogen bonding due to the ionization of the α-CDterm hydroxyl groups by NaOH; as a result, the slide-ring gelterm·dom could swell 19 . However, a stimuli-sensitiveterm·key hydrogelterm·key that is only useful in high-pH aqueous solutions does not have broad utility. Even when a nonionic, water-soluble hydroxypropylated PRterm·dom cross-linkerterm·key (HPR-Cterm; Fig. 1a ) was used, the resulting NIPA-based hydrogelterm·key did not exhibit greater extensibility than chemical poly(NIPAterm) hydrogelsterm·key 25 . Although this hydrogelterm·key was nearly transparent, the pulley effectterm·dom might not have worked well in this hydrogelterm·key because the solubility of HPR-Cterm in water might be insufficient.

These excellent mechanical properties of this hydrogelterm·key are most likely achieved because of the homogeneous network structure afforded by the pulley effectterm·dom. To confirm the structural homogeneity of the hydrogelsterm·key, their structures were analysed under uniaxial elongation by small-angle X-ray scatteringterm·mth (SAXSterm·mth). In general, the spatial inhomogeneity of cross-links is hidden by the fluctuation in the polymer chain concentration before the elongation. When the chemical gels are deformed, the inhomogeneous structure is exposed and the two-dimensional (2D) X-ray or neutron scattering patterns become elliptical 26 . Figure 3a shows 2D SAXSterm·mth patterns of as-prepared (elongation ratio ε =1) and vertically stretched ( ε >1) NIPAterm–AAcNaterm–HPR-Cterm hydrogelsterm·key cross-linked with 0.65 wt% HPR-Cterm. These patterns are almost isotropic, which is consistent with the results for slide-ring gelsterm·dom in a good solvent 27 . This hydrogelterm·key contains water, which is a good solvent; therefore, it has no internal aggregation structure. The SAXSterm·mth pattern of this hydrogelterm·key remains isotropic when the hydrogelterm·key is elongated by a factor of greater than four, which is in contrast to the elliptical pattern observed for a poly(acrylamide) gel when elongated by a factor of 1.5 (ref. 27) 27 . The angle of the sector was 20° in each direction. Figure 3b shows the sector-averaged scattering profiles of the hydrogelterm·key at elongation ratios of ε =1–4 in each direction. Hydrogelsterm·key using ionic PRterm·dom cross-linkerterm·key Furthermore, to obtain a wide variety of thermosensitiveterm·key hydrogelsterm·key with mechanical toughness and superb functionality, the thermo-responsiveness must be efficiently regulated while maintaining the enhanced mechanical properties. Thus, we demonstrate a case in which the thermo- and pH-responses of PR-cross-linked hydrogelsterm·key are regulated by controlling the spatial distribution of the ion concentration in the hydrogelsterm·key.

The pure poly(NIPAterm) hydrogelterm·key without any ionic species undergoes a volume change at 32 °C (ref. 21 ). For hydrogelsterm·key consisting of randomly copolymerized NIPAterm and ionic monomers, the characteristic temperature of the volume change generally increases with increasing ionic monomer concentration. However, the volume of the hydrogelterm·key does not change when a large amount of ionic monomers are present in the hydrogelterm·key 22 ; the strongly hydrated ions distributed throughout the whole polymer network inhibit the dehydration of the polymer chains. Interestingly, the temperature-sensitive swelling behaviours of random and graft copolymer hydrogelsterm·key composed of NIPAterm and ionic monomers are dramatically different. The volume-change temperatures of the graft copolymer hydrogelsterm·key have been reported to be similar to that of the pure poly(NIPAterm) hydrogelterm·key because the coil-globule transition of the poly(NIPAterm) portion is not affected by the grafted polymer composed of ionic monomers 32 , 33 .

On the basis of the graft copolymer hydrogelterm·key properties, we successfully prepared pure poly(NIPAterm) hydrogelsterm·key without ions that exhibit both mechanical toughness and thermo-responsiveness utilizing an ionic PRterm·dom derivative as a cross-linkerterm·key ( Fig. 4a ). This PRterm·dom cross-linkerterm·key (iPR-Cterm) has carboxyl groups on the α-CDterm molecules, making it highly water soluble. It was expected that iPR-Cterm can be well extended in a neutral poly(NIPAterm) network ( Fig. 4b ), as observed for HPR-Cterm in the polyelectrolyte network ( Fig. 2g ). The most distinctive feature of the iPR-Cterm gels is that the ionic groups are localized only on the PRterm·dom cross-linkerterm·key. The spatial distribution of the ionic groups on the polymer network is therefore remarkably different from that in the randomly copolymerized NIPAterm–AAcNaterm gels. As expected, the iPR-Cterm hydrogelsterm·key are as transparent, stretchable and tough in pure water ( Fig. 4d ; Supplementary Table 3 ; Supplementary Movie 3 ) as the NIPAterm–AAcNaterm–HPR-Cterm hydrogelsterm·key. The degree of dissociation of the carboxylic acid groups in the NIPAterm–AAcNaterm–HPR-Cterm hydrogelsterm·key changes with pH, and the osmotic pressure in the hydrogelsterm·key therefore changes with pH because the ionic AAcNaterm monomer is distributed throughout the polymer network. As a result, the swelling behaviour of the NIPAterm–AAcNaterm–HPR-Cterm hydrogelsterm·key is highly dependent on the solvent pH ( Fig. 5a ). However, the swelling behaviour of the NIPAterm–iPR-Cterm hydrogelsterm·key shows little dependence on the solvent pH when the amount of iPR-Cterm is lower than 3 wt% ( Supplementary Figs 5 and 6 ). Although the carboxyl groups of iPR-Cterm can also dissociate in water in response to changes in the pH, the dissociated ion does not affect the transition temperature significantly because the amount of iPR-Cterm is very small and the ionic groups are localized only on the iPR-Cterm cross-linkerterm·key. Thus, the pH dependence of the temperature response is successfully regulated by controlling the spatial distribution of the ionic groups in the hydrogelsterm·key via simple chemical modifications of the PRterm·dom.

Discussion We demonstrate the simple preparation of extremely stretchable hydrogelsterm·key with temperature and pH sensitivities by using PRterm·dom derivatives as cross-linkersterm·key and introducing ionic sites into the polymer network via two different methods. One method involves randomly copolymerizing NIPAterm and ionic AAcNaterm with HPR-Cterm as the cross-linkerterm·key. Because of the considerable swelling power of the polyelectrolyte hydrogelsterm·key, HPR-Cterm, which might be in a contracted state in the poly(NIPAterm) hydrogelterm·key, achieves a swollen coil state, and the pulley effectterm·dom is observed. The resulting hydrogelsterm·key exhibit significant pH sensitivity because of the electrostatic repulsion between the charges on the polymer chains and the effect of pH on the osmotic pressure of the counter ions. This finding is significant for improving existing polyelectrolyte hydrogelsterm·key. Although current polyelectrolyte hydrogelsterm·key have the potential to absorb and release large amounts of solvents 34 , recognize or react with specific molecules 35 , 36 , 37 and be employed in artificial muscles 5 , 6 , their brittleness must be reduced before they can be used in these applications. The PR-based cross-linkerterm·key developed here can lead to significant improvements in the mechanical properties of existing polyelectrolyte hydrogelsterm·key without compromising their characteristic properties. Alternatively, iPR-Cterm can be widely employed as a cross-linkerterm·key to improve the mechanical properties of a broad range of existing polymer gels, including non-polyelectrolyte gels, because it does not require the use of an ionic monomer to obtain enhanced mechanical properties. Therefore, the mechanical properties of existing well-studied stimuli-sensitiveterm·key hydrogelsterm·key can be improved without compromising their stimuli sensitivities by using iPR-Cterm.

In conclusion, extremely stretchable and tough thermosensitiveterm·key hydrogelsterm·key were obtained by using PRterm·dom cross-linkersterm·key and introducing ionic sites into the polymer network. Two different preparation methods were employed. Our results indicate that using a small amount of a supramolecular building block as a cross-linkerterm·key in a polymer network results in dramatic changes in its mechanical properties. Using the preparation methods described here, which are simple, like conventional polymer gel preparation techniques, the mechanical properties of various polymer gels can be improved. Thus, stimuli-sensitiveterm·key hydrogelsterm·key with good mechanical performance and controllable functionality can be produced for drug delivery systems, tissue engineering, artificial muscles and sensors. Substantial progress is being made towards the practical use of stimuli-sensitiveterm·key hydrogelsterm·key for these applications.

Methods Materials HPR ( M n =126,000; M w / M n =1.4; inclusion ratio of α-CDterm: 29%) and ionic PRterm·dom (iPR: M n =156,000; M w / M n =1.4; inclusion ratio of α-CDterm: 23%) were purchased from Advanced Soft Materials (Kashiwa, Japan) and were used without further purification. NIPAterm was purified by recrystallization from toluene/ n -hexane. AAcNaterm (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA), 2,2′-methylenebisacrylamide (BISterm; Acros Organics, Geel, Belgium), APS (Acros Organics), TEMED (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Tokyo, Japan), dibutyltin dilaurate (DBTDL; Tokyo Kasei Kogyo, Tokyo, Japan) and butyl hydroxyl toluene (BHT; Tokyo Kasei Kogyo) were purchased as reagent-grade materials and used as received. 2-acryloyloxyethyl isocyanate (Showa Denko K.K., Japan) was used as received. Milli-Q ultra-pure water was used in all the experimentsterm·mth.

Preparation of PRterm·dom cross-linkersterm·key (HPR-Cterm and iPR-Cterm) HPR or iPR (500 mg), the DBTDL catalyst (1 drop) and BHT (polymerization inhibitor, 0.78 mg) were dissolved in 30 ml of anhydrous DMSO. 2-Acryloyloxyethyl isocyanate (78 mg) was dissolved in 10 ml of anhydrous DMSO and the solution was added dropwise to the mixtures with vigorous stirring in the absence of light. The mixtures were then continuously stirred overnight at 40 °C to ensure that the reactions were complete. HPR-Cterm or iPR-Cterm was reprecipitated from the reaction mixture using an excess of methanol or acetone, respectively, and the precipitated product was refrigerated. The products were washed several times with methanol and acetone and then dried. The total number of vinyl groups per HPR and iPR was estimated from 1 H-NMR spectra and was found to be ca. 200 ( Supplementary Figs 1–3 ).

Preparation of hydrogelsterm·key The stretchable hydrogelsterm·key were prepared by conventional free-radical polymerization of the monomers with the PRterm·dom cross-linkersterm·key. For the polyelectrolyte hydrogelsterm·key, appropriate amounts of NIPAterm, AAcNaterm, HPR-Cterm or BISterm, and APS (initiator) were dissolved in water. The final concentrations of the ionic monomer and NIPAterm were 0.1 and 1.9 M, respectively, whereas the cross-linkerterm·key concentration changed. For the NIPAterm–iPR-Cterm hydrogelterm·key, appropriate amounts of NIPAterm, iPR-Cterm and APS were dissolved in water. The final concentration of NIPAterm monomer was 2 M, whereas the concentration of iPR-Cterm changed. In all the pre-gel solutions, the total concentration of the monomers excluding the cross-linkersterm·key was fixed at 2 M. Next, N 2 gas was bubbled through the pre-gel solutions for 30 min, which were then sonicated to remove excess nitrogen from the solution. The pre-gel solution was infused onto glass slides separated by Teflon spacers to prepare slab gels. Cylindrical gels were prepared using microcapillaries with an inner diameter of 270 μm. To initiate the polymerization below room temperature, a few drops of TEMED were added to the pre-gel solution. The gelations were performed at 4 °C for 24 h. The hydrogelsterm·key were thoroughly washed with water for 2 weeks to remove any unreacted monomer or reaction residue.

Mechanical strength tests The mechanical properties of the hydrogelsterm·key were measured with a TA Instruments RSA III rheometric solid analyser. The as-prepared 0.5-mm-thick hydrogelterm·key samples were cut into 5-mm × 30-mm rectangles for the stress–strain measurements. The hydrogelterm·key samples were placed and aligned between two fixtures and were tightly fixed by sample-loading bolt screws at both ends. We ensured that the hydrogelterm·key samples were smooth before each measurement. The transient strain-controlled multiple extension mode was used as the test set-up. The elongation rate was 0.1 mm s −1 . The measurements were conducted in air at room temperature (~25 °C). The reproducibility of the stress–strain curves was verified by successive measurements.

SAXSterm·mth SAXSterm·mth measurements were performed using the synchrotron radiation light source (beamlines BL-6A, BL-15A and BL-10C) at the Photon Factory, High Energy Accelerator Research Organization, KEK (Tsukuba, Japan). The wavelength λ of the X-ray beam was 1.50 Å, and the beam size was 0.7 mm (vertical) × 0.7 mm (horizontal). An X-ray charge-coupled device detector (C4880-50-26, Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan) equipped with a 150-mm diameter X-ray image intensifier was used as the SAXSterm·mth detector. The as-prepared 1-mm-thick hydrogelterm·key samples were cut into rectangles before they were analysed. The samples were placed in a uniaxial stretching machine for the deformation. The stretching direction of the hydrogelterm·key samples was vertical. The distance between the sample and the detector was ~2,300 mm, which was later calibrated according to the diffraction peaks of chicken tendon collagen (domain spacing, d =653 Å). The SAXSterm·mth patterns were recorded for 0.01 Å −1 < q <0.08 Å −1 , where q is the magnitude of the scattering vector and 2 q is the scattering angle. The typical exposure time was 30 s, where the scattering pattern did not change. Therefore, the beam damage and the drying of the gel were ignorable in this experimental condition.
