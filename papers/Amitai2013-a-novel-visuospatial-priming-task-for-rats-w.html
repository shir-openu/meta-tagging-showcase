phenomenonnegative visuospatial priming
claim-typemethod
transferfrom classical experimental psychology of arousal and performance: the Yerkes-Dodson inverted-U law, imported to explain why the same amphetamine dose helps low-NP and harms high-NP rats — “This “rate-dependent” effect of AMPH recalls the widely observed pattern described in the Yerkes-Dodson law, which states that performance, especially in difficult tasks, initially increases with physiological or mental arousal, but when levels of arousal become too high, performance is degraded”
mechanismAn inverted-U relation between dopamine transmission and inhibitory processing: amphetamine pushes low-NP animals toward and high-NP animals past an optimum, so the same drug raises or abolishes priming depending on baseline state — “The finding that AMPH improves NP in animals with normally low levels of NP, while reducing NP in animals with high levels of NP, suggests that an optimal level of dopamine transmission might produce maximal amount of negative visuospatial priming, and that levels either above or below that optimal point result in reduced or no negative priming.”
mechanismBrief primes are routed through preconscious, automatic processing, which is the route that generates negative priming; longer primes recruit more controlled processing and weaken it — “Conceivably, shorter duration primes may favor processing by preconscious/automatic mechanisms, which may be required for the negative priming effect.”
mechanismA species difference in speed-accuracy strategy - rats protect response speed and sacrifice accuracy - channels the priming cost into accuracy rather than latency — “The tendency of rats to maintain speed of responding at the cost of accuracy may therefore explain why the NP effect in our task primarily impacted response accuracy rather than response latencies.”
relation — “In this study, we aimed to describe the characteristics and heuristic values of a novel rat negative visuospatial priming paradigm ( Figure 1 ), based on a well-established human visuospatial priming task ( Swerdlow et al., 1996 ; Tipper, 1985 ).”
effect directionTourette syndrome → negative priming — reduces — “TS subjects exhibit diminished NP”
effect directionprime duration → negative priming — increases — “produced the most robust negative priming”
replicationreplicated
limitationRats that could not acquire stable performance had to be dropped, reducing the sample. — “Three of 16 rats failed to develop stable performance in the task and were excluded, leading to a final n of 13 rats.”
limitationThe task's whole-body response makes it insensitive to the tens-of-milliseconds latency effects that carry negative priming in humans. — “Changes in response latency on that scale would be difficult to detect in this rat NP task, which requires the rat to make a whole-body movement across the experimental chamber to respond to the stimulus.”
limitationNo animal model, including this one, can capture the full neurobiology of the varied forms of Tourette syndrome. — “perhaps more importantly, no animal model is likely to capture the full neurobiology relevant to all of the varied forms of TS.”

content-observations: finding · quantity · variable · definition · key-term — grounded in the full text
findingRats trained on the new task showed a robust negative priming effect, performing worse when the target appeared where the distractor had just been. — “In the studies reported here, rats exhibited robust negative visuospatial priming, reflected by degraded performance when the targets were presented in the location previously occupied by the non-target distractor during the preceding prime (NP trials), compared to unrelated target locations in prime and probe trial (baseline trials).”
findingThe authors report this as the first rodent paradigm measuring visuospatial priming. — “To our knowledge, this is the first documented rat task assessing visuospatial priming.”
findingShortening the prime from 0.5 s to 0.25 s strengthened the negative priming effect. — “Shorter prime duration resulted in accuracy priming values becoming more positive compared to the days before and after the challenge, indicating a stronger NP effect”
findingLengthening the prime from 0.5 s to 1 s weakened negative priming, the mirror image of the short-prime result. — “This pattern – that the priming effect became stronger when the prime duration was reduced – was consistent with the observation that lengthening the prime duration from 0.5 to 1 s weakened the priming effect”
findingEach animal's degree of negative priming was consistent across two repetitions of the short-prime challenge, suggesting NP is a stable individual trait. — “indicating that the degree of NP in this task appears to be a stable trait inherent to the animal”
findingAmphetamine abolished negative priming at every dose in rats that showed strong drug-free priming. — “Significant NP was observed in high NP rats after saline administration, but was abolished in these rats after AMPH administration at all doses.”
findingIn rats the negative priming effect appeared in accuracy rather than in response latency, unlike the human task where latency is the more robust index. — “The negative priming effect reported here was most robustly observed in response accuracy: significantly lower accuracy was detected in NP trials compared to baseline trials”
findingAmphetamine reliably increased premature responding at all doses, while leaving trials completed unaffected. — “Premature responses were increased significantly by all doses of AMPH”
quantity13 of 16 — final sample after three rats failed to reach stable task performance and were excluded — “Three of 16 rats failed to develop stable performance in the task and were excluded, leading to a final n of 13 rats.”
quantity0, 0.25, 0.5, or 1 mg/kg — d-amphetamine doses given subcutaneously 10 min before testing, in a counterbalanced within-subject design — “Rats then received 0, 0.25, 0.5, or 1 mg/kg D-amphetamine 10 min before being tested in the negative visuospatial priming task.”
quantity0.5 s to 0.25 s — the reduction in prime stimulus duration that produced the most robust negative priming — “rats were challenged by decreasing the duration of the prime stimuli from 0.5 s to 0.25 s.”

quantity0.5 s for seven rats, 0.75 s for three rats, 1 s for one rat, and 1.5 s for two rats — the shortest probe stimulus duration each rat could sustain at criterion performance — “The final probe stimulus duration achieved was 0.5 s for seven rats, 0.75 s for three rats, 1 s for one rat, and 1.5 s for two rats.”
quantityaround 15 sessions — average training needed to reach criterion on the visual discrimination stage — “On average, rats required around 15 sessions to reach criterion performance.”
