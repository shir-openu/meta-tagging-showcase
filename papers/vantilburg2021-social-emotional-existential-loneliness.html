Social, Emotional, and Existential Lonelinessterm·dom: A Test of the Multidimensional Concept Abstract Since the 1980s, most researchers have agreed on the concept of social and emotional lonelinessterm·dom as an unacceptable and negatively experienced discrepancy between realized and desired interpersonal relationships. For other researchers, existential lonelinessterm·dom stems from the realization that a human being is fundamentally alone, with the accompanying emptiness, sadness, and longing. This article examines whether instruments to measure these conceptualizations indicate a multidimensional concept.

The 2019 observation of the Longitudinal Aging Study Amsterdam ( N = 1,316; aged 61–101 years; 52% women) included five direct questionsterm·key about lonelinessterm·dom, the 11-item de Jong Gierveld social and emotional lonelinessterm·dom scale, and 14 items from the translated Existential Loneliness Questionnaireterm·mth. Confirmatory factor analysisterm·key was conducted in Mplus.

Five factors were observed: direct questionsterm·key, social and emotional lonelinessterm·dom, and lonelinessterm·dom in relationships and meaninglessness in lifeterm·dom. The intercorrelations among all five factors were positive. Emotional lonelinessterm·dom correlated most strongly with direct questionsterm·key.

Lonelinessterm·dom is multifaceted and means that one is not embedded in a personal network, misses closeness and intimacy, and lacks meaning in life. The emotional lonelinessterm·dom items most closely represent what people mean when they report lonelinessterm·dom.

Perlman and Peplau (1981 , p. 31) defined lonelinessterm·dom as “the unpleasant experience that occurs when a person’s network of social relations is deficient in some important way, either quantitatively or qualitatively.” Other researchers have defined existential lonelinessterm·dom “as an intolerable emptiness, sadness, and longing, that results from the awareness of one’s fundamental separateness as a human being” ( Ettema et al., 2010 , p. 142). The two definitions and, as will be shown, their measuring instruments not only use similar terms, but also highlight a lack of social connection, albeit in a very different way. This article examines whether the instruments for measuring these conceptualizations indicate a multidimensional concept.

The Concepts of Social, Emotional, and Existential Lonelinessterm·dom Lonelinessterm·dom is a negative feeling related to loss and disappointment. It is the outcome of a process in which a person weighs up his or her existing personal relationships against his or her own wishes and social expectations with regard to relationships. If the social network is too small or the relationships are of insufficient quality, the person often feels lonely ( de Jong Gierveld, 1978 ; Perlman & Peplau, 1981 ). This has been the most widely used conceptualization of lonelinessterm·dom in research. Two basic types of lonelinessterm·dom are social and emotional lonelinessterm·dom ( Weiss, 1973 ). Social lonelinessterm·dom originates from the absence of a broader group of contacts or an engaging social network. Emotional lonelinessterm·dom originates from the absence of an intimate figure or a close emotional attachment. Social and emotional lonelinessterm·dom often occur due to reduced social activities ( Aartsen & Jylhä, 2011 ) or loss of the partner ( Utz et al., 2014 ), among other situations.

In a literature review, Bolmsjö et al. (2019) distinguished several key aspects of existential lonelinessterm·dom: not connecting with others and the world outside, alienation, feelings of isolation, emptiness, and abandonment. Additionally, mortality-related fears were identified to be associated with this type of lonelinessterm·dom, including the fear of disappearing from the earth, the fear of being forgotten, and the fear of dying. A further examination of this concept leads to two considerations.

First, the concept of existential lonelinessterm·dom needs to be sharpened and more clearly distinguished from social and emotional lonelinessterm·dom. For example, a negative evaluation of not connecting with others overlaps with social lonelinessterm·dom. Feelings of isolation, emptiness, and abandonment are aspects of emotional lonelinessterm·dom.

Second, social and emotional lonelinessterm·dom happen to somebody; people do not seek lonelinessterm·dom voluntarily. These types of lonelinessterm·dom are felt as social pain ( Cacioppo et al., 2006 ). In contrast, some scholars see existential lonelinessterm·dom as something that can be appreciated positively; one finds strength in being solitary, although it takes effort to realize this situation. For Moustakas (1961) , existential lonelinessterm·dom is inherent in human existence, such that everyone can identify as a solitary individual. People retreat from the social world to create a self-identity and to live a genuine life. Thus, in life’s most intimate and important moments, someone is an individual and is always alone. Lonelinessterm·dom can be a creative force among people who create music or inventions on their own. Ellison (1978) has described the separation by which humans give meaning to life and to their relationship with God. For Ettema et al. (2010) , existential lonelinessterm·dom consists of negative feelings of nothingness that alternate and amalgamate with being in a process of inner growth. In this article, we focus on existential lonelinessterm·dom as a negative experience, and we aim to use the concept of solitudeterm·dom for when people want to be alone with positive purposes ( Lay et al., 2020 ).

Existential lonelinessterm·dom is often studied in patients with severe illness ( Tarbi & Meghani, 2019 ) and end-of-life situations ( Boston et al., 2011 ). Casey and Holmes (1995) studied older people residing in nursing homes. After living a fulfilling and often busy life, these people are left without meaningful roles ( Hupkens et al., 2018 ). Rosedale (2007) studied people with severe illnesses who realize their own mortality. Cherry and Smith (1993) examined lonelinessterm·dom in AIDS patients and observed that patients experienced inescapable separateness from others.

Existential lonelinessterm·dom differs from social and emotional lonelinessterm·dom in two ways. First, social and emotional lonelinessterm·dom are associated with a lack of meaningful social relationships and a lack of social companionship. Existential lonelinessterm·dom is the result of a broader separation related to the nature of existence and, in particular, a lack of meaning in life. An individual may be in the company of others but experience existential lonelinessterm·dom ( Larsson et al., 2019 ). Second, social and emotional lonelinessterm·dom can be overcome by improving the quality of the network of relationships or by adjusting the level of aspiration ( Rook & Peplau, 1982 ). Existential lonelinessterm·dom, on the other hand, has no permanent remedy according to the phenomenological approach ( Mayers & Svartberg, 2001 ).

The Measurement of Lonelinessterm·dom Lonelinessterm·dom is often measured with a single, direct questionterm·key. Such a question, for example, “Do you feel lonely?,” is simple to use, appears to be acceptable to respondents, reflects lonelinessterm·dom as understood by the respondent, and provides an easy way to assess the prevalence of lonelinessterm·dom ( Jylhä & Saarenheimo, 2010 ; van TilburgpersName & de Jong Gierveld, 1999 ; Victor et al., 2005 ). However, the use of a direct questionterm·key presupposes that the respondents have a common understanding of the term “lonelinessterm·dom” and that their understanding encompasses the whole theoretical concept. A single item does not provide information on the relevance of social, emotional, and existential aspects of lonelinessterm·dom. Because of the social stigma of lonelinessterm·dom ( Lau & Gruen, 1992 ), people who are not seen as lonely by others may find it difficult to admit their lonelinessterm·dom as an answer to a direct questionterm·key. Finally, the psychometric quality of a single question cannot be determined.

Alternatively, lonelinessterm·dom can be measured with a scale including statements that relate to lonelinessterm·dom but avoid the term “lonelinessterm·dom” or similar wording. The UCLA scale ( Russell, 1996 ) and the de Jong Gierveld (DJG) scale ( de Jong Gierveld & Kamphuis, 1985 ) are based on a conceptual framework of lonelinessterm·dom, in which different relational aspects and emotions relevant to the experience of lonelinessterm·dom are distinguished. Solano (1980) concluded that the UCLA scale appeared to identify a subjective lack of social companionship and is less sensitive to philosophically determined types of lonelinessterm·dom, such as existential lonelinessterm·dom. McWhirter (1990) and Hawkley et al. (2005) found various dimensions in the UCLA scale, but it is often considered a unidimensional measure. The DJG scale was developed as a unidimensional lonelinessterm·dom scale with both the social and emotional aspects of lonelinessterm·dom in mind. The homogeneityterm·mth of the unidimensional scale proved to be modest at best. When searching for more homogeneous subscales, social and emotional lonelinessterm·dom factors emerged ( de Jong Gierveld & van TilburgpersName, 1992 ; van Baarsen et al., 2001 ). The UCLA and DJG scales have rarely been studied in one sample. In a Dutch study, de Jong Gierveld and van TilburgpersName (1992) observed that the measurement of social lonelinessterm·dom was similar to that of the core dimension of the UCLA scale, which includes seven items. Another study ( Penning et al., 2014 ) indicated that both scales were multidimensional, but the correlation between the scale scores was not reported.

While scales for social and emotional lonelinessterm·dom are widely used, there has been limited research into existential lonelinessterm·dom. Mayers et al. (2002) developed the Existential Loneliness Questionnaireterm·mth (ELQterm·mth). This is the only scale we found that focuses on existential lonelinessterm·dom in surveyterm·mth research, with the exception of one studied among students in the 1970s ( Belcher, 1973 ). Three items were specific to HIV, three were formulated conditionally, and two included a direct reference to lonelinessterm·dom. Gökdemir-Bulut and Bozo (2018) conducted two studies on the ELQterm·mth. They found three factors, that is, lonelinessterm·dom in social ties, lonelinessterm·dom in close relationships, and finding meaning in life; it appears that the first and second factors relate to social and emotional lonelinessterm·dom.

The Current Study As far as we know, no research has examined whether existential lonelinessterm·dom should be distinguished from social and emotional lonelinessterm·dom. The current research analyzes data from older respondents who answered items from the DJG and ELQterm·mth scales, along with direct lonelinessterm·dom questions. It tests whether social, emotional, and existential lonelinessterm·dom are related but distinctive dimensions of lonelinessterm·dom that are associated with direct measurements of lonelinessterm·dom. We also aim to determine whether existential lonelinessterm·dom is indeed a negative feeling.

To support the validity of the lonelinessterm·dom scales, we look for the association of various characteristics with the three types of lonelinessterm·dom. We expect to find that men and women have similar levels of lonelinessterm·dom scales scores ( Maes et al., 2019 ) but that women will report more lonelinessterm·dom on the direct questionsterm·key ( Borys & Perlman, 1985 ). We also expect that age will correlate positively with all instruments ( Luhmann & Hawkley, 2016 ), as will being without a partner, having a smaller network, not having daily network contact, and having poor health because unhealthy people have little social participation ( de Jong Gierveld et al., 2018 ). Religiosity is linked to lower levels of lonelinessterm·dom ( Lauder et al., 2006 ). Religious people are more involved than others in church-based social contacts and organizations in the community ( Rote et al., 2013 ). In the current study, validity testing can go both ways. When there is a strong core in the concept of lonelinessterm·dom, we can establish that measurements of social, emotional, and existential lonelinessterm·dom are similarly associated with a number of antecedents. When multiple dimensions in lonelinessterm·dom need to be distinguished, we can establish that the measurements are differently associated with the antecedents.

Design and Methods Respondents Data were taken from the Longitudinal Aging Study Amsterdam ( Huisman et al., 2011 ). This program employed samples of men and women born between 1908 and 1957 taken from the population registers of the cities of Amsterdam, Zwolle, and Oss, and six surrounding small municipalities in 1992, with additions in 2002 and 2012. The response rate at baseline was 63%. Follow-up interviews were conducted every 3 or 4 years. For each follow-up, an average of 82% of respondents were reinterviewed.

Data collection on 1,701 men and women was completed in 2019. We excluded 110 participants for whom data were collected from an interview with a proxy and 198 respondents interviewed by phone with a short questionnaire including only six social and emotional lonelinessterm·dom items. The DJG scale and three direct questionsterm·key were part of the first, general face-to-face interview. One respondent was unable to participate in a full interview due to incapacity and did not answer the lonelinessterm·dom questions. The ELQterm·mth was part of a subsequent medical interview, which was conducted by another interviewer an average of 32 days after the initial interview. A total of 76 respondents from the first interview did not participate in the second interview. Thus, data from 1,316 respondents were analyzed. Their age varied between 61 and 101 years ( M = 73.0), and 52% were women. Migrants were underrepresented. Most respondents were of Dutch origin; 3% were born in another Western country, and 2% were born in a non-Western country. Almost all respondents lived independently; 1% lived in a nursing home.

Two ELQterm·mth items that asked respondents whether they feel “lonely” and “alone” were also treated as direct questionsterm·key.

The subscales reflect social lonelinessterm·dom and emotional lonelinessterm·dom, respectively.

Existential lonelinessterm·dom was measured by 14 ELQterm·mth items. We excluded three HIV items and three conditionally formulated items. We performed a translation from English into Dutch, followed by back translation, using two translators in both steps ( Beaton et al., 2000 ).

The frequency of church attendance was assessed on a six-point scale from “never” to “once a week or more.”

Gökdemir-Bulut and Bozo (2018) tested the ELQterm·mth and assumed three factors. However, this ELQterm·mth dimension was only modestly related to social lonelinessterm·dom. This modest association may be due to addressing different aspects of lonelinessterm·dom in the other items and due to different wordings and mode effects, such as the use of two different interviews and different numbers of response options. The congruence of “existential lonelinessterm·dom in relationships” and social and emotional lonelinessterm·dom needs to be further investigated. We conclude that the ELQterm·mth subscale on relationships does not contribute sufficiently to the conceptualization of lonelinessterm·dom. In addition, the subscale is weakly homogeneous and insufficiently reliable. The third factor distinguished by Gökdemir-Bulut and Bozo relates to a differential conceptualization that we have labeled “meaninglessness in lifeterm·dom.” The positive correlations among existential lonelinessterm·dom scale scores, the direct questionterm·key scores, and social and emotional lonelinessterm·dom show that existential lonelinessterm·dom as measured with the ELQterm·mth is not a positive and rewarding experience. However, we did not address the fact that individuals sometimes seek solitudeterm·dom ( Lay et al., 2020 ) or think they are better off alone ( Birditt et al., 2019 ), and we did not measure the “desire for aloneness” ( Leung, 2015 ).

The lack of fit of the unidimensional, second-order, and bifactor models and the modest correlations between the five factors, especially between social and emotional lonelinessterm·dom on the one hand and existential lonelinessterm·dom on the other, suggest that the various instruments address many different aspects. We can see the collection of these aspects as representative for one broad multidimensional theoretical concept, or, alternatively, these aspects do not have enough in common to be seen as dimensions of one sharply defined concept of lonelinessterm·dom and represent two concepts, namely, “lonelinessterm·dom” and “meaning in life” ( Brandstätter et al., 2012 ), each with their own definition. However, unlike Steptoe et al.’s findings, we did not find clearly distinct antecedents associated with the lonelinessterm·dom dimensions. This may indicate a commonality: social, emotional, and existential aspects are all negative lonelinessterm·dom feelings pointing to a lack of connectedness with people and life, which are associated in the same way to a number of lonelinessterm·dom antecedents. On the basis of the antecedents, the three dimensions cannot therefore be sufficiently distinguished from each other to be treated as a standalone phenomenon. In future research, we propose using 18 questions that measure three types of lonelinessterm·dom. The existential scale could be improved.

It is now widely recognized that lonelinessterm·dom is related to public health, for example, due to increased premature mortality among lonely people ( Holt-Lunstad et al., 2015 ). We want to draw attention to some design issues in addition to those addressed previously. First, respondents participated in two long interviews. The sample does not include many older people who are in a serious situation, such as severe illness or the end of life, which is the focus of a number of studies on existential lonelinessterm·dom. Second, there was a time between the two interviews, which contributed to participants avoiding response sets and other ways in which offering many questions on one topic can affect the outcome of the research. At the same time, the respondent’s situation may have changed between the two interviews, which may have contributed to the relatively low correlation between the DJG scores (assessed in the first interview) and the ELQterm·mth scores (assessed in the second interview). Finally, we did not look in detail at the quality of the scales and did not investigate whether removing items leads to more homogeneous scales.

To conclude, we explored the dimensions of lonelinessterm·dom and found that social, emotional, and existential aspects were relevant.
