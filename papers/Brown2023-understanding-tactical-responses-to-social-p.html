phenomenonchoice between cause-focused and consequence-focused responses to social problems
claim-typeprediction
mechanismFeatures of a social problem contract or expand regulatory scope, and the width of scope then determines whether the solution pursued targets consequences or causes — “We propose that focusing on different features contracts or expands scope, which directs the pursuit of solutions to address either the consequences or causes of a problem (Fig. 1).”
mechanismWhere an emotion is aimed (at affected individuals versus at the system) narrows or widens scope, which in turn channels action towards relief or towards structural change — “These prior findings might be explained by a regulatory scope mechanism: emotions directed at those affected by a social problem might contract scope and promote the pursuit of consequence-focused actions to help those affected directly, whereas emotions directed at the social system might expand scope and promote the pursuit of cause-focused action to interrupt the broader system.”
relation — “In this Perspective, we draw on regulatory scope theory10 and construal level theory11,12 to explain when and why people pursue different solutions to address social problems.”

limitationThe proposed model as a whole has not been empirically tested; only some of its paths have prior support. — “Although prior research provides support for some of the predictions outlined here, future research is needed to empirically test the full proposed model.”
limitationThe intergroup studies cited as support did not actually measure expanded scope as the mediating mechanism. — “However, these studies did not test expanded scope as a mechanism.”
limitationRegulatory scope is only one of several possible mechanisms driving social action. — “Importantly, although regulatory scope is a useful lens for understanding the solutions people pursue to reduce social problems, this is just one possible mechanism and engagement in social actions is not exclusively guided by scope.”
limitationThe five features analysed are illustrative rather than an exhaustive set of scope-modulating features. — “Although this list of features is not exhaustive and additional features certainly influence the pursuit of solutions (for example, the diversity of groups affected by the issue or social familiarity), we focus on these five features as initial illustrations.”
limitationThe feasibility-versus-desirability prediction is offered as plausible reasoning that still requires empirical test. — “Although logically sensible, this notion needs to be empirically tested to fully understand how feasibility and desirability influence responses to social problems.”
limitationThe framework sorts the same actions by a different principle from the frameworks it replaces. — “Importantly, existing frameworks primarily focus on categorizing the actions themselves rather than their underlying aims. For example, in response to police violence, people could donate directly to the family of someone who was harmed42 or donate to organizations seeking to restructure local and state police budgets43. Although these examples involve taking the same action — donating money — to address the same social problem, allocating donations towards different funds might reflect different underlying aims. For example, donating to the family of someone harmed might stem from an aim to help that individual family in the present moment. By contrast, donating to organizations seeking to restructure police budgets might stem from an aim to aid the broader group of Black Americans who might be affected by police violence by curbing opportunities for police violence to occur in future. Thus, the difference between these two responses is not the action itself (donating) but the focus and aim of the action (that is, the scope of concerns the action seeks to address). We propose an alternative framework within which to understand people’s engagement in social action, focusing on the aims of the action and therefore on how people understand the problem that they are attempting to solve”
limitationTwo adjacent constructs are distinguished in one sentence, and the theory depends on keeping them apart. — “Importantly, whereas psychological distance refers to the distance between a person and a mental object, scope refers to the span and breadth of possibilities that one considers”
limitationThe paper is a Perspective that draws two existing theories together rather than reporting a study. — “In this Perspective, we draw on regulatory scope theory10 and construal level theory11,12 to explain when and why people pursue different solutions to address social problems”

content-observations: finding · quantity · variable · definition · key-term — grounded in the full text
findingThe paper's central proposal is that a proximal or lower-level focus pushes people towards solutions that relieve suffering, while a distal or higher-level focus pushes them towards solutions that attack the problem's causes. — “Specifically, we propose that people pursue solutions that alleviate the suffering of those affected by the problem (consequence-focused solutions) when they focus on lower-level or more psychologically proximal features and that they pursue solutions that address the underlying causes of the problem (cause-focused solutions) when they focus on higher-level or more psychologically distant features.”
findingThe framework names five specific features of a social problem that are proposed to expand or contract scope and thereby steer which kind of solution is chosen. — “These features include: individual versus group suffering, present versus future considerations, short-term versus long-term rewards, feasibility versus desirability of creating change, and emotions directed towards the individual situation versus the system (Fig. 1).”
findingThe relation between construal features and chosen solutions is claimed to run in both directions: acting on consequences itself pulls attention to the present, while acting on causes pulls attention to the future. — “Importantly, this relationship is bidirectional. Consequence-focused actions operate at a lower level and therefore promote a focus on the present, whereas cause-focused actions operate at a higher level and therefore promote a focus on the future45.”
findingThe authors flag the emotion-target pathway as an untested, novel prediction generated by their framework rather than an established result. — “and where emotions are directed has not been empirically tested, this is a novel prediction derived from our framework.”
findingAn implication drawn is that the widespread preference for feasible, short-term consequence-focused action helps explain why social problems persist. — “First, understanding how people construe a social problem might explain why many problems continue to persist. If people primarily pursue actions that address the consequences of a problem because of the greater appeal of feasibility over desirability or short-term over long-term rewards, the root cause of the problem will probably remain and continue to affect others.”
quantity90 per cent / 10 per cent — Share of surveyed anti-poverty NGO supporters reporting consequence-focused actions (donating, fair-trade purchases) versus cause-focused actions (signing petitions), cited in support of the framework — “For example, about 90 per cent of sampled members of World Vision Australia and the Global Poverty Project (antipoverty NGOs) reported participating in actions such as donating and purchasing fair-trade products to help those affected by poverty; only 10 per cent reported participating in actions such as signing petitions to try to address the causes of poverty8.”
definitionsocial problem — “Police violence against Black Americans is an example of a social problem — an issue generally perceived as an illegitimate, harmful social condition3,4.”
definitionsocial action — “Social action occurs when people seek to remedy or alter a problematic situation or issue13, such as poverty, social inequality and the impacts of natural disasters.”
definitioncollective action — “Research that investigates why people engage in social action often focuses on understanding engagement in collective action, defined as any action that individuals take in support of a group with the goal of social change14”
definitionpsychological distance (construal level theory) — “Construal level theory describes how people think about and orient to objects or events as a function of psychological distance (how far something is from one’s direct experience)11,12.”
definitionlevel of construal — “Seeing something as more concrete or abstract refers to the level of construal.”
definitionregulatory scope theory — “Regulatory scope theory10 expands on construal level theory and describes how people act and make decisions to achieve different goals by changing the breadth or scope of their considerations.”
definitionconsequence-focused vs cause-focused solutions — “We propose that a similar process might unfold when considering social problems: people might pursue solutions that alleviate the downstream consequences (consequence-focused solutions) or address the underlying causes (cause-focused solutions) of a perceived social problem.”
definitionbenevolence actions vs activism actions — “Actions can be classified as benevolence actions, which provide tangible money, goods or services (often deemed prosocial behaviours) or activism actions, which seek to challenge the existing system (such as attending rallies and signing petitions)8,9.”
definitionaffirmation actions vs informed actions — “Affirmation actions refer to behaviours meant to provide interpersonal support and understanding, whereas informed actions involve behaviours that seek to dismantle privilege and confront bias targeting the outgroup.”
definitionfeasibility and desirability — “Feasibility (the ease or difficulty in achieving an end state) and desirability (the extent to which an end state is valued) are not necessarily oppositional but they are often contrasted in the construal and goal literatures when distinguishing between means and ends”
definitiongroup efficacy — “Group efficacy reflects perceptions of whether collective action will achieve its goals and is measured with items such as “I think that together we can change [the social problem]” and “to what extent do you think that this [collective action] will increase the chances of the government changing their plans?”
definitionhope — “Similarly, hope reflects the cognitive appraisal that a desirable goal is possible to achieve in the future121, which involves both desirability and feasibility (that is, what is desired is possible).”
