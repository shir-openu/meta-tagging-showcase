findingThree identified interneurons of the locust auditory pathway show three qualitatively different distributions of adaptation mechanisms: mixed intrinsic/presynaptic in TN1, inhibition-driven in BSN1, and intrinsic spike-triggered in AN2. — “(1) balanced influence of both intrinsic and presynaptic adaptation mechanisms in an interneuron that summates over several receptor afferents (TN1), (2) predominantly inhibiting input as the source for spike frequency adaptation in a cell that transmits both pattern representation and directional information (BSN1), (3) primarily intrinsic, spike-triggered adaptation currents within an interneuron coding exclusively for direction (AN2).”
findingThe time course of adaptation is cell-type specific, differing significantly between the three neuron types. — “The time courses of spike frequency adaptation differed significantly between the cells types.”
findingSpike frequency adaptation is not confined to one station but operates at every level of the sensory pathway. — “Our major finding is that spike frequency adaptation acts at all levels of a sensory pathway.”
findingThe measured adaptation time constants successfully predicted each cell's high-pass cutoff frequency for amplitude-modulated signals. — “Using the adaptation time constants, we were able to predict signal transmission properties for the different cells.”
findingAll three interneurons behave as bandpass filters, with the low-pass edge set by mean firing rate and the high-pass edge by adaptation. — “All three neurons displayed bandpass characteristics ( Fig. 7 B ).”
findingWhich adaptation mechanism a neuron uses tracks the computation it performs, so the origin of adaptation is a function of the cell's processing role. — “We conclude that the adaptation mechanisms differ greatly among interneurons within this sensory pathway and are a function of their role in information processing.”
findingthree distinct distributions of adaptation mechanisms emerged across three cell types of one pathway — “By comparing current and acoustic stimulation, three distinct patterns of the distribution of adaptation mechanisms within the sensory network emerged: (1) balanced influence of both intrinsic and presynaptic adaptation mechanisms in an interneuron that summates over several receptor afferents (TN1), (2) predominantly inhibiting input as the source for spike frequency adaptation in a cell that transmits both pattern representation and directional information (BSN1), (3) primarily intrinsic, spike-triggered adaptation currents within an interneuron coding exclusively for direction (AN2).”
findingAN ARITHMETIC NULL MODEL, NOT A DIRECTION: linear composition predicts the acoustic ratio equals 0.5 times the current ratio, and the data were compared against that line — “If adaptation mechanisms intrinsic to receptors and interneurons added up linearly, the ratio for acoustic stimuli r a ac in an interneuron that is monosynaptically connected to receptors should be the product of the adaptation ratio of the interneuron r a cu and that of the receptor cells. findingin TN1 the magnitude of SFA to acoustic stimuli was qualitatively in agreement with a linear combination of SFA in receptors and adaptation mechanisms within TN1 — “Direct comparison of the adaptation ratios at 70 and 140 Hz ( Fig. 2 B , C ) showed that the magnitude of SFA to acoustic stimuli is qualitatively in agreement with a linear combination of SFA in receptors and adaptation mechanisms within TN1, leading to a multiplication of adaptation ratios.”
findingA PREDICTED CORRELATION THAT FAILED: cells adapting more under current should adapt more under sound, and TN1 showed r = 0.09, p = 0.81 — “Cells exhibiting stronger SFA under current stimulation should also adapt more strongly to acoustic stimuli, yet no positive correlation between the ratios for acoustic and current stimuli was found for TN1 ( r = 0.09, p = 0.81) ( Fig. 2 B , C ). The putative correlation may have been masked by the large errors in the input–response curves that propagated to the adaptation ratios.”
findingBSN1 at 140 Hz adapted far more than the null model allows, implying processes the model does not contain — “At 140 Hz onset response, SFA during acoustic stimulation was much stronger than expected from adaptation mechanisms within receptors and BSN1 alone, suggesting additional processes underlying the SFA at higher acoustic stimulus levels.”
findingAN ANALYSIS DECLINED AND SAID SO: AN2 was not analysed at the higher onset response because only one cell reached it under both modes — “We did not analyze the AN2 at the higher onset response, because only one of the cells under study responded with >140 Hz at both stimulation modes.”

findingTN1 and BSN1 showed very similar adaptation characteristics under current stimulation, this pattern changed dramatically in BSN1 under acoustic stimulation, and in AN2 the two modes gave very similar results — “In summary, TN1 and BSN1 showed very similar adaptation characteristics when stimulated with current and revealed a moderate and linear SFA with time constants ∼50 ms. Keywords: spike frequency adaptation, auditory system, invertebrate, signal transmission, adaptation mechanisms, temporal Introduction Spike frequency adaptation (SFAterm), the decrease in neuronal response strength during prolonged stimulation, is a feature of virtually all sensory systems. SFAterm plays a role in adjusting the neuron's dynamic range to the stimulus range ( Brenner et al., 2000 ; Dean et al., 2005 ; Maravall et al., 2007 ), optimizing energy consumption ( Heitwerth et al., 2005 ; Niven et al., 2007 ), and processing of time varying signals ( French et al., 2001 ; BendapersName et al., 2005 ). The observed SFAterm can result from different mechanisms and sources of adaptation, such as the transduction process in receptors cells ( Hudspeth et al., 2000 ; Gollisch and Herz, 2004 ; Albert et al., 2007 ), spike-dependent adaptation currents ( Brown and Adams, 1980 ; Madison and Nicoll, 1984 ; Fleidervish et al., 1996 ), synaptic depression ( Abbott et al., 1997 ; Chance et al., 1998 ) and inhibitory inputsterm ( Finlayson and Adam, 1997 ; Ingham and McAlpine, 2005 ). The net SFAterm observed in the intact sensory pathway will typically result from the sum of many processes occurring between the stimulus and the recording siteterm, each of which transforms the representation of sensory information, reflecting a general design principle ( Laughlin, 1989 ; Baccus and Meister, 2004 ). Disentangling the respective contributions to SFAterm within a pathway is a difficult task, however. So far, SFAterm has mainly been studied either by using sensory stimulation in vivo or current stimuli in vitro . In the latter case, intrinsic properties of isolated cells or small circuits are isolated, whereas sensory stimulation invokes adaptation mechanisms within the entire pathway. Here we present data from an insect system, the auditory pathway of locusts, in which we investigate SFAterm in vivo in response to both acoustic stimuli and injected currents in the same cell. We surveyed specific identified auditory interneurons in the metathoracic ganglionterm of the locust and identified different characteristic patterns of adaptation mechanisms for different cell types. The auditory pathway of locusts at the level of the metathoracic ganglionterm is a hierarchical network with three layers: receptors and receptorterm-like interneurons, local neurons, and ascending neurons. We selected three exemplary interneurons from each processing level: TN1term receives monosynaptic input from receptorterm cells and exhibits spiking responses that are similar to those of the receptors, BSN1term is a local interneuron that represents an intermediate processing level, and AN2term is an ascending interneuron that transmits information directly to the insect's brain ( Stumpner and Ronacher, 1991 ). On present evidence there is no direct connection between the three cell types presented here. Overall connectivity in the network appears to be low ( Vogel and Ronacher, 2007 ), consisting of feedforward connections between the layers but no connections between neurons of the same layer ( Marquart, 1985 ; Boyan, 1992 , 1999 ; Vogel and Ronacher 2007 ). In the present study, we systematically quantified the magnitude and time course of SFAterm for the three cell types in response to the two stimulation modes. We explore the distribution of adaptation mechanisms along the auditory pathway, discuss its possible consequences for sensory processing, and ask whether SFAterm predicts differences in coding of temporal aspects of signals for those cell types. In AN2term, current and acoustic stimuli led to very similar results with respect to time course and magnitude of SFAterm. These findings provide evidence for strong network effects on SFAterm under acoustic stimulationterm in BSN1term, whereas in AN2term SFAterm can be understood mainly by intrinsic properties. In TN1term, SFAterm observed under acoustic stimulationterm is in good agreement with a linear combination of adaptation mechanisms within receptors and in the interneuron itself. Discussion We examined the origin of spike frequency adaptation in different identified neurons along the auditory pathway of locusts. Between the three cell types, SFAterm differed significantly in strength and time course when stimulated acoustically. We were able to discern the locations of adaptation mechanisms in the sensory pathway underlying SFAterm by an analysis of SFAterm under current injection and an evaluation of changes of the membrane potential after stimulation. Thereby, we found three distinct patterns: a balanced influence of adaptation mechanisms in the afferents and intrinsic to the cells (TN1term), predominately network effects via inhibition (BSN1term), and primarily intrinsic, spike-triggered adaptation currents (AN2term). Different origins SFAterm in the three cell types We found three different patterns on how SFAterm is based on mechanisms resulting from intrinsic properties or the respective peripheral pathway in auditory interneurons of the locust ( Fig. 8 ). First, both network and intrinsic cell properties can have a balanced impact on SFAterm as observed in TN1term. In this cell, adaptation strength under acoustic stimulationterm was only moderately larger than under current stimulationterm ( Fig. 2 B , C ). Adaptation time constants for both stimulation modes ( Fig. 3 C ) were similar to those found in receptorterm cells (10–80 ms) ( BendapersName, 2002 ) in which SFAterm arises mainly from encoder adaptationterm ( Fig. 8 , 2 ) ( BendapersName and Herz, 2003 ; Gollisch and Herz, 2004 ). Thus, there is no evidence for an additional adaptation process other than in the afferent receptorterm cells and the intrinsic mechanisms of TN1term ( Fig. 8 , 2 , 4 ) (cf. Ocker and Hedwig, 1993 ). Second, SFAterm can arise from network effects acting either peripherally or on the cell itself. This pattern was apparent in BSN1term, which adapted quickly with time constants <20 ms ( Fig. 3 C ). The strong SFAterm was accompanied by long-lasting hyperpolarizations ( Fig. 4 A ). Because these were not the result of spiking activity of the BSN1term ( Fig. 4 C ), they probably are imposed by inhibitory inputsterm leading to slow hyperpolarizing currents ( Fig. 8 , 3 ) (see also Weschke and Ronacher, 2008 ). Third, SFAterm can be dominated by intrinsic properties, as observed in AN2term. This cell revealed long-lasting spike-triggered afterhyperpolarizations that accumulate over time and lead to a long-lasting reduction in excitability ( Figs. 4 , 6 ). This finding makes a spike-triggered, hyperpolarizing AHP- or M-type current the most likely mechanism behind SFAterm in AN2term ( Fig. 8 , 4 ) ( Baldissera et al., 1973 ; Baldissera and Gustafsson, 1974 ). Notably, in AN2term SFAterm was stronger but slower than SFAterm seen in receptorterm neurons ( Fig. 3 C ) ( BendapersName, 2002 ). Thus, intrinsic adaptation mechanisms may overwrite the time course of SFAterm in the upstream pathway, provided sufficient adaptation strength. Impact of the stimulation site on the comparability between acoustic and current stimulationterm All three cells are large and show an extended dendritic tree ( Römer and Marquart, 1984 ; Stumpner and Ronacher, 1991 ), and the site of current injection may have an impact on the results and their interpretation. Therefore, we compared onset responses as an indicator for the effective amount of current delivered in the two stimulation modes. In BSN1term and AN2term, the maximal onset response to current and acoustic stimuli were similar (supplemental Fig. 1 B , available at www.jneurosci.org as supplemental material), and pronounced EPSPs suggest a more dendritic position of our electrode ( Fig. 4 A ; supplemental Fig. 1 A , available at www.jneurosci.org as supplemental material). In TN1term, large sound intensities elicit very strong responses not reached by current stimulationterm, probably due to a more axonal position of the electrode in TN1term recordings (supplemental Fig. 1 A , available at www.jneurosci.org as supplemental material). Thus, our conclusions for TN1term are strictly valid only for onset responses up to 150 Hz. However, in TN1term both adaptation strength and dynamics were largely independent of intensity ( Figs. 2 , 3 ; supplemental Fig. 1 B , available at www.jneurosci.org as supplemental material) and there is no evidence for a more complicated scheme at higher intensities [see Gabbiani and Krapp (2006) for similar results in a large visual interneuron]. SFAterm and signal transmission properties Identified auditory interneurons in locusts have different temporal transmission properties ( Stumpner and Ronacher, 1991 ; Prinz and Ronacher, 2002 ; Vogel and Ronacher, 2007 ; Wohlgemuth and Ronacher, 2007 ). Can SFAterm, which is known to be of relevance for signal recognition in grasshoppers ( Ronacher and HennigpersName, 2004 ), be linked to known transfer characteristics of neurons? SFAterm acts mainly as a high-passterm filter at low frequencies ( BendapersName et al., 2005 ) and the time constants of SFAterm predicted the observed differences in the cutoff frequency ( Fig. 7 C ). Weschke and Ronacher (2008) also found a high-passterm characteristic in BSN1term with a cutoff between 10 and 20 Hz, which matches our findings ( Fig. 7 C ). Notably, in AN2term, the high-passterm is shifted to lower frequencies than at earlier processing stages by strong but slower adaptation dynamics. This is possible, because the overall gain is lower than in more peripheral neurons (e.g., TN1term). Whereas the high-passterm characteristics as reported here are difficult to compare with previous studies due to the different methods of quantification, the low-passterm characteristics predicted by mean frequency confirm known cutoff frequencies ( Prinz and Ronacher, 2002 ; Wohlgemuth and Ronacher, 2007 ; Weschke and Ronacher, 2008 ). However, to further link cell type-specific SFAterm to temporal processing in the respective neurons, experiments with long-duration natural stimuli are required. Functional role of the distribution of adaptation mechanisms Our major finding is that spike frequency adaptation acts at all levels of a sensory pathway. Similar results were reported for the locust's visual pathway ( Gabbiani and Krapp, 2006 ) as well as for vertebrate visual ( Sanchez-Vives et al., 2000a , b ; Solomon et al., 2004 ), somatosensory ( Derdikman et al., 2006 , Katz et al., 2006 ) and auditory systems ( McAlpine et al., 2000 ; Ingham and McAlpine, 2004 , 2005 ; Avissar et al., 2007 ). Different computational tasks may call for different distributions of adaptation mechanisms: e.g., (1) summation over many inputs to achieve high fidelity might require distributed mechanisms at several levels ( Baccus and Meister, 2004 ; Baccus, 2006 ), whereas (2) converging inputs from different processing channels might require channel-specific adaptation mechanisms mostly at presynaptic stations ( Abbott et al., 1997 ; Chance et al., 1998 ; Luksch et al., 2004 ). In grasshoppers, the auditory pathway extracts the “what” and “where” information by processing of temporal and directional cues in parallel pathways ( HennigpersName et al., 2004 ; Helversen and Helversen, 1995 ), and most auditory interneurons can be classified accordingly ( Stumpner and Ronacher, 1994 ). Can the different distributions of adaptation mechanisms we present here be related to these functional aspects of auditory processing? TN1term summates over many excitatory inputs from both sides ( Stumpner and Ronacher, 1994 ), a process by which directional information is lost and the representation of temporal aspects enhanced. We find that mechanisms behind SFAterm for TN1term are distributed over several levels of processing ( Fig. 8 ), thus matching the expectations for summation over many inputs (1) as also observed in the visual pathway of vertebrates ( Solomon et al., 2004 ; Dunn et al., 2007 ). BSN1term appears to serve both localization and pattern processing. SFAterm as observed in BSN1term is distinctly input dependent, as expected for the integration of several information streams in a single neuron (2). However, accumulated hyperpolarizations, probably due to inhibitory inputsterm, have a lasting effect. Then, SFAterm to a stimulus via one input affects the response to subsequent stimulation via another input and will lead to a cross-interaction of temporal and directional information. In AN2term, the intrinsic adaptation state depends mainly on the excitation of the AN2term itself and is thus largely invariant to which input triggered excitation. AN2term exhibits a strict directionality that is achieved by a balance between ipsilateral excitation and contralateral inhibition. When an adapting stimulus is presented laterally, mainly the contralateral AN2term but not the afferents to AN2term will reduce excitability, and possibly preserve the functionally important balance between inhibition and excitation. Thus, the AN2term may represent a third functional principle for the distribution of adaptation mechanisms: if the computation performed by a sensory neuron relies on the ratio of incoming inhibition and excitation, the neuron itself should adapt and not the presynaptic input. Our analysis on the effect of SFAterm on coding properties revealed similar results for AN2term and BSN1term ( Fig. 7 ); however, if directional coding is taken into account, BSN1term and AN2term display different responses ( Stumpner and Ronacher, 1994 ) and SFAterm would be expected to act differently on these two neurons. Because we tested stimuli from the side that elicited a larger response for each neuron, our present investigation of SFAterm was restricted to temporal processing. Nevertheless, for BSN1term a lateralized adaptor could mainly decrease excitability of either contralateral inhibition or ipsilateral excitation, thereby changing the balance of these two. In AN2term, excitability is mainly controlled in the postsynaptic neuron but not in its inputs and therefore directional information should be unbiased by localization of a previous stimulus. Our results add further evidence that distributed mechanisms are a widespread feature of sensory pathways and provide an important design principle for such pathways found across taxa and modalities. Furthermore, we were able to show that the distribution of adaptation mechanisms is highly specific for cell types, which in turn posses distinct coding and processing functions. Thus, the origin of SFAterm depends on the computational role of the part of the sensory pathway under study.
