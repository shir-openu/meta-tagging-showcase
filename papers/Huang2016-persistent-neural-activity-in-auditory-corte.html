Human subjects performed three tasks on an identical set of four sequences of tones. The tasks and the sequences differed in the extent to which WMterm was required. Each sequence was composed of two tones, S1 and S2, separated by a delayterm ( Figure 1a,c ). The tones could be of frequency A or B, resulting in the sequences AA, AB, BA, and BB.

Tasks 1 and 2 both required a go response (a button press within a limited time window after S2) to the sequence AA and a nogo response (no button press) to the other three sequences ( Figure 1a ). The two tasks differed with respect to the index finger used for the go response: the right for taskterm 1 and the left for taskterm 2. They also differed with respect to the ear stimulated: the left for taskterm 1 and the right for taskterm 2, contralateral to the finger used. The tasks thus controlled for potential differential effects of the side of auditory stimulation and execution of the behavioral response and allowed the investigation of whether both the left and right AC are involved in auditory WMterm. In order to solve tasks 1 and 2, subjects first had to identify the frequency of S1 because it determined how the remainder of the trial needed to be processed. When S1 was B, a nogo response was required, irrespective of S2, and nothing else needed to be held in WMterm. When S1 was A, however, subjects remained uncertain whether a go or a nogo response would be required after S2. To cope with this uncertainty, they could use different strategies involving WMterm. For example, they could hold in WMterm the two possible associations between S2 and the behavioral response (a go response when S2 was A and a nogo response when it was B), and then make the appropriate response after identifying S2. Or they could hold in WMterm the frequency of S1, compare it with that of S2, and then make a go or a nogo response depending on whether S2 was identical to or different from S1, respectively. Irrespective of the strategies subjects utilized, WMterm was involved during the delayterm between S1 and S2 in each trial. The random presentation of the four sequences, AA, AB, BA and BB, prevented the predictability of the sequence in the upcoming trial and, consequently, the information required for making a correct response needed to be stored and updated on a trial-by-trial basis, that is, in WMterm on a scale of seconds ( Goldman-Rakic, 1995 ). Because more information needed to be stored during the delayterm in trials when S1 was A than when it was B, the WMterm loadterm during delayterm was higher in the former than in the latter trials. In the following, these trials are referred to as high- and low-WM-load trials, respectively.

If AC were involved in WMterm, there should be differences in AC activityterm during the delayterm between high- and low-WM-load trials, but the presence of such differences would not allow the conclusion that they are due to differences in WMterm loadterm. Taskterm 3 was a delayed-response taskterm in which subjects had to hold in WMterm that, irrespective of the identity of S2, a go response after S2 was required when S1 was A and a nogo response was required when S1 was B. If differences in activityterm during the delayterm in taskterm 1 were only due to differences in late activityterm or in preparatory activityterm, then the differences in activityterm between the high- and low-WM-load trials in taskterm 1 should be very similar to the differences between the go and nogo trials in taskterm 3. If, however, such a similarity is not observed, then the differences in activityterm during the delayterm in taskterm 1 (and by inference taskterm 2) are at least partly due to differences in WMterm loadterm.

The current report provides converging evidence from humans and monkeys that neural activityterm in AC can reflect WMterm. This was demonstrated in three independent studies in which subjects performed different tasks on the same sequences of sounds, allowing separation of activityterm related to WMterm from activityterm related to other mental processes. Changes in WMterm loadterm resulted in persistentterm changes of spiking activityterm, LFPs and magnetic fields, suggesting that AC stores information in spikes and synaptic potentials. Our results thus support the idea that early sensory cortexterm stores information for WMterm ( Sreenivasan et al., 2014 ).

An auditory taskterm·def has been defined as an operation requiring the production of an intentional connection between sounds, other context-relevant stimuli, and behavioral actions, which are executed because subjects are motivated to do so ( Scheich and BroschpersName, 2012 ). Activityterm in AC is not only related to processing the sounds and the other context-relevant stimuli but also to mental processes associated with performing a taskterm such as selection of and preparationterm for behavioral actions, and expectationterm of rewards ( BroschpersName et al., 2011 ). In the current report, we confirmed that activityterm in AC can persistently change for several seconds relative to baselineterm in a sound-discrimination taskterm (taskterm 5; Figure 3 ). Thus, persistentterm changes of neural activityterm observed during the performance of WMterm tasks in previous studies are, on their own, not sufficient for demonstrating that AC is involved in WMterm ( Bigelow et al., 2014 ; Gottlieb et al., 1989 ; Grimault et al., 2014 ; Kumar et al., 2016 ; Lemus et al., 2009 ; Linke and Cusack, 2015 ; Linke et al., 2011 ; Ng et al., 2014 ; Nolden et al., 2013 ; Scott et al., 2014 ).

In the current report, we used three approaches to show that at least some portion of persistentterm changes in activityterm in AC was related to WMterm and not to other concurrent mental processes. In the first approach (study 1), we compared differences in activityterm between trials that differed in WMterm loadterm in one taskterm with differences in activityterm between trials that did not differ in WMterm loadterm in another taskterm. In the second approach (study 3), we compared differences in activityterm between trials that differed in WMterm loadterm in one taskterm with differences in activityterm between trials that also differed in WMterm loadterm, but in a reversed fashion, in another taskterm. This further enabled the determination of the stimulus-specificity of the effect of WMterm loadterm. In the third approach (study 2), we directly compared activityterm in trials which differed only in WMterm loadterm. Our approaches have the potential to single out other types of WM-related activityterm including rhythmic activities and spatiotemporal activation patterns and could be used in other sensory modalities. They can also be adapted to tease out activityterm related to other cognitive and motivational processes. However, note that the second approach requires stimulus-specificity of the neural activityterm under consideration. It may be difficult to demonstrate such stimulus-specificity in gross measures of the electrical activityterm of large neural populations, such as EEG and MEGterm·mth.

Our converging results from intracranial recordings in monkeys and extracranial recordings in humans demonstrated that information is temporarily stored in AC by persistentterm changes in spiketerm rates and slow potentials. This was reflected in enhancement or suppression of spiketerm rates, positive or negative changes of LFPs, and increases in the strengths of regional sources during the delayterm relative to baselineterm. These ways of storing information in AC are similar to those considered for visual ( Fuster and Jervey, 1982 ), somatosensory ( Zhou and Fuster, 1996 ), and prefrontal cortex ( Goldman-Rakic, 1995 ; Reinhart et al., 2012 ; Wilson et al., 1994 ). Enhancement of activityterm in AC during the delayterm may serve to refresh representations of information to keep them active while suppression of activityterm may serve to protect representations from internal or external interference ( Linke et al., 2011 ; Scott et al., 2014 ). The latter has been found to be particularly relevant when recoding and rehearsal strategies were not used by subjects ( Linke et al., 2011 ). Information could also be stored in AC in other ways ( Stokes, 2015 ), such as in spatiotemporal activation patterns, synaptic plasticity, or rhythmic activities, but these were not analyzed here.

Although our approaches revealed sites where neural activityterm was related to WMterm, it is likely that other mental processes contribute to the persistentterm changes in activityterm during the delayterm relative to baselineterm. In the spiketerm activityterm, effects of other mental processes seem to be relatively small, because the spiketerm rate barely changed during the delayterm relative to baselineterm in low-WM-load trials ( Figure 4f,g ). In the LFPs, effects of other mental processes seem larger, because at many sites LFPs changed during the delayterm even in low-WM-load trials ( Figure 5g ). This is consistent with the large increase of source strength during the delayterm relative to baselineterm in our MEGterm·mth studies, which was only slightly smaller in low- than in high-WM-load trials ( Figures 2 , 3 ). LFPs and magnetic fields have been considered to partially reflect inputs to a region of tissue, in contrast to spiketerm activityterm, which has been considered to preferentially reflect output to connected areas ( Buzsáki, 2004 ; Buzsáki et al., 2012 ; Hämäläinen et al., 1993 ). Our observations that mental processes other than WMterm were represented differently at the input to and at the output from AC therefore suggest that selective input-output transformations occur in AC such that, at the output (spikes), the representation of WMterm is favored over that of other mental processes.

In our experiments, subjects could have used two behavioral strategies to solve the tasks which required WMterm. They could have stored either the frequency of S1 during the delayterm or the stimulus-response associations of S2. Which information was stored in an individual trial is difficult to assess and cannot be inferred from the behavioral response in that trial. Therefore, we cannot determine what information is reflected in the neural activityterm in AC. This question can be addressed in future experiments with tasks that can only be solved with a single strategyterm. For instance, increasing the number of frequencies for S1 in study 2 would bias the subject to solve the tasks by storing the information of the past S1, as tested in previous studies on animals (e.g., Gottlieb et al., 1989 ).

The WM-related activityterm in the core fields of AC, as measured in monkeys, was stimulusterm specific, i.e., it was observed only when a specific frequency of S1 (or specific stimulus-response associations of S2) had to be stored in WMterm. Since only two tones (1 and 3 kHz) were tested, the stimulusterm selectivity could not be assessed accurately. Our study confirms the stimulus-specificity of WM-related activityterm in AC revealed in fMRI studies of humans ( Kumar et al., 2016 ; Linke et al., 2011 ) but contrasts with studies of nonhuman primates which reported stimulus-specificity to be missing in AC ( Lemus et al., 2009 ; Scott et al., 2014 ).

Our report provides evidence for the sensory-recruitment hypothesis of WMterm·def ( Sreenivasan et al., 2014 ), which posits that storageterm of information recruits the same neurons in sensory cortexterm that represent the information. This hypothesis has mainly been based on multivariate analysis of fMRI signal patterns in early visual cortex, thus, on a signal that is only indirectly related to brain activityterm ( Logothetis and Wandell, 2004 ), but not on direct measurements of electric activityterm in sensory cortexterm. Our results are also compatible with the static populationterm coding hypothesis for WMterm which posits that storageterm of information recruits neurons without high selectivity ( Sreenivasan et al., 2014 ), because we also observed WM-related activityterm in neurons that did not respond to the two tones (1 and 3 kHz).

Our results imply that single neurons in AC are capable of temporarily storing information by changing their spiketerm rates. Such neurons were found in the core fields of AC, where they comprised 22% of all recorded neurons. It is conceivable that these neurons are spatially organized in clusters preferentially exhibiting either enhancement or suppression during the delayterm. This speculation is consistent with our finding that WM-related activityterm in multiunits was as common as in single units (30% vs. 22%). This would not have been observed if the neurons exhibiting enhancement or suppression were not clustered. Following similar reasoning, spatial clustering may also be present for the slow potentials, otherwise changes of LFPs in positive or negative directions would not have been observed during the delayterm.

While recordings of spiketerm activityterm provide clear evidence for WM-related activityterm in the core fields of AC, we cannot clarify whether other fields of AC are also involved in WMterm. Because the slow potentials analyzed here can spread over millimeters, it is possible that synaptic potentials in adjacent AC fields contributed to the WM-related LFPs measured in the core fields. An involvement of AC fields outside the core in the performance of WMterm tasks has been demonstrated for rostral superior temporal cortex ( Scott et al., 2014 ) and temporal pole ( Ng et al., 2014 ). Our MEGterm·mth studies clarified that activityterm in both the left and right AC was related to WMterm. Involvement of both ACs has been seen in previous studies for simple WMterm tasks with other stimuli ( Grimault et al., 2014 ; Kumar et al., 2016 ; Nolden et al., 2013 ), for complex WMterm tasks that, e.g., required manipulations of WMterm content ( Brechmann et al., 2007 ), or for WMterm tasks in which subjects utilized other strategies, such as articulatory rehearsal ( Linke et al., 2011 ).

Our report also revealed WM-related activityterm in vlPFCterm. Such activityterm is consistent with previous studies showing that vlPFCterm is involved in non-spatial auditory WMterm tasks ( Hwang and Romanski, 2015 ; Plakke and Romanski, 2014 ; Plakke et al., 2015 ). Although based on one monkeyterm only, our observation of persistentterm enhancement and suppression of spiketerm rates during the delayterm suggests that vlPFCterm stores information in the same ways as AC. Our observation that WM-related activityterm started ∼100 ms later in vlPFCterm than in AC suggests that vlPFCterm does not trigger the storageterm of information in AC on a trial-by-trial basis. Although PFC has been traditionally hypothesized to exert a top-down modulation to aid storageterm of information in sensory cortexterm ( Curtis and D’Esposito, 2003 ; D’Esposito and Postle, 2015 ; Fuster, 2001 ; Miller and Cohen, 2001 ; Miller et al., 1996 ; Sreenivasan et al., 2014 ), our finding challenges vlPFCterm as the source of top-down signals to auditory cortexterm.

The present study reported a sizable number of neurons in AC with activityterm related to auditory WMterm. The storageterm in AC of information about sounds over several seconds may be an essential component of the analysis of temporal patterns of sounds including speech and communication sounds ( May et al., 2015 ; Petkov and Jarvis, 2012 ). The WM-related persistentterm activityterm changes described here reflect active processes recruited when subjects are engaged in WMterm tasks. Because the change of the neural activityterm persisted throughout most of the period during which information needed to be stored, it is conceivable that the memory trace carried by this activityterm remains relatively unaltered throughout the memory period. Whether persistentterm activityterm changes in AC also support the storageterm of information for even longer periods and other WMterm processes, including spatial aspects and processes requiring modifications of stored information, is not clear and is under debate ( Fritz et al., 2005 ; Scott and Mishkin, 2016 ). Because very similar persistentterm WM-related activityterm was observed in our studies in the AC of humans and monkeys, the cellular bases of some auditory WMterm processes in humans can well be studied in monkeys.

Fifteen subjects (7 males, 8 females, mean age = 26 years) participated in study 1 and twenty-six (14 males, 12 females, mean age = 27 years) in study 2. All subjects gave their written informed consent to participate in the studies which were approved by the ethics committee of the Otto-von-Guericke University, Magdeburg. Data from three subjects in study 1 and from 11 subjects in study 2 were excluded prior to analysis because of strong artifacts or technical problems during measurements. The studies were designed and conducted following the guidelines for basic MEGterm·mth research in the field of neuroscience ( Gross et al., 2013 ).

For study 1, subjects were instructed to perform tasks 1, 2 and 3 on an identical set of four two-tone sequences (AA, AB, BA and BB) within the same experimental session. Each trial started with a soft noise burst (a 25-ms burst of white noise with a sound pressure level of 16 dB), accompanied by the appearance of a fixation cross on a screen ∼1 m in front of the subject’s head. One second later, the first toneterm (S1) was presented followed by the second toneterm (S2) two seconds later. Both tones had durations of 100 ms. The delayterm between S1 and S2, defined as the time from the offset of S1 to the onset of S2, was therefore 1.9 s. The frequencies of the two tones were either A (1.5 kHz) or B (1.6 kHz) and their sensation levels were adjusted to 80 dB SL. Stimuli were conducted to the subject’s ear via a plastic tube and ear mold which fitted comfortably into the external ear canal. When a go response was required, as explained below, subjects had to make it within 2 ± 0.25 s after the onset of S2 when the next trial started (see Zacharias et al., 2011 for details of the experimental setup).

For tasks 1 and 2 ( Figure 1a ), subjects were instructed to make a go response (i.e., press a response button) when both S1 and S2 were A and a nogo response (no button press) for the other three sequences (AB, BA and BB). Sequences starting with A were high-WM-load trials and those starting with B were low-WM-load trials. Tasks 1 and 2 differed only with respect to the ear of stimulation and the hand used to make the response. For taskterm 1, stimuli were conducted to the subject’s left ear and the go response was made with the right index finger, contralateral to the stimulated ear. For taskterm 2, stimuli were conducted to the right ear and the go response was made with the left index finger, also contralateral to the stimulated ear. For taskterm 3 ( Figure 1c ), subjects were instructed to make a go response when S1 was A and a nogo response when S1 was B, irrespective of the frequency of S2. As for taskterm 1, the stimuli were conducted to the left ear and the right index finger was used for the go response. Each subject performed the three tasks in three separate blocks. The order of the tasks was randomized across subjects.

MEGterm·mth measurements were performed in a magnetically shielded chamber by means of a 248-channel whole-head magnetometer system (4-D Neuroimaging, San Diego, USA). The magnetic field was acquired continuously at a sampling rate of 678.17 Hz in study 1 and of 1017.25 Hz in study 2. Signals were filtered online between DC and 100 Hz in study 1 and between DC and 200 Hz in study 2. Note that high-pass filtering signals at 4 Hz revealed no differences between high- and low-WM-load trials. In addition, vertical and horizontal electrooculograms were recorded to track eye movements and blinks.

For further processing, the raw data were exported into the BESA software package (BESA Software GmbH, Gräfelfing, Germany). We first performed a heartbeat artifact correction ( Ille et al., 2002 ). The corrected signal was then segmented into 3-s epochs, ranging from 500 ms before the onset of S1 to 500 ms after the onset of S2. Epochs contaminated with other artifacts were discarded (for details, see Zacharias et al., 2011 ). For each subject, taskterm and sensor, the remaining artifact-free epochs were band-pass filtered (high-pass DC, low-pass 30 Hz, zero phase, 24 dB/octave). Average waveforms were obtained by arithmetically averaging the signals from epochs of trials with correct responses, separately for each subject, taskterm and sensor. The use of the arithmetic average for this purpose is justified ( Zacharias et al., 2011 ; KönigpersName et al., 2015 ). The average waveforms were then baseline-offset corrected. The value for the baselineterm offset correction was determined from the mean signal amplitudes over the 300-ms interval directly before S1 and was subtracted from the waveform.

The baseline-corrected average waveforms from all sensors formed the input to the source analysis for which each dataset was co-registered with the T1-weighted anatomical MR image (3T, 192 slices) of the subject’s brain using Brainvoyager QX (Brain Innovation B.V., Maastricht, The Netherlands). Separately for each subject and taskterm, we modeled the neuronal sources underlying the magnetic field distribution around the peak of the M100 component evoked by S1 by two regional sources, one in the left and one in the right AC. These regional sources were seeded at the border of Heschl’s gyrus and planum temporale ( Näätänen and Picton, 1987 ). In addition, we seeded three pairs of regional sources roughly symmetrically in left and right central, frontal and occipital cortical areas ( Figure 1—figure supplement 1 ) to model background activityterm, that is, to exclude potential confounds from other brain areas. These three pairs of sources were located close to the cortical surface such that their distances to the source in the AC of the same hemisphere were relatively large. We did so to minimize the mutual influence between the signals of these sources and of the sources in AC because the mutual influence decreases with increasing distance between the sources. In contrast to the positions of the sources, their orientations and amplitudes were fitted to best account for the magnetic field distribution of the average waveforms at all sensors using the spherical volume conductor model as head model ( Scherg, 1990 ). Results of this source analysis are source waveforms, one for each subject and condition. These waveforms were then exported to MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA) for further analysis.

The comparison of grand mean waveforms across tasks requires homogeneous variance and a normal distribution of the underlying MEGterm·mth data. We found that this could be achieved by a log-transformation of individual source waveformsterm·stat, consistent with earlier studies ( KönigpersName et al., 2015 ; MatysiakpersName et al., 2013 ; Zacharias et al., 2011 ). To obtain the grand mean waveform, the log-transformed source waveforms were arithmetically averaged across subjects and this mean waveform was then back-transformed (by exponentiation) and plotted along a logarithmic axis.
