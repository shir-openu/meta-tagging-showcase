Abstract

Nearby neurons in mammalian neocortex demonstrate a great diversity of cell types and connectivity patterns. The importance of this diversity for computation is not understood. While extracellular recording studies in visual cortex have provided a particularly rich description of behavioral modulationterm of neural activity, new methods are needed to dissect the contribution of specific circuit elements in guiding visual perception. Here, we describe a method for three-dimensional cellular imagingterm·mth of neural activity in the awaketerm mouseterm visual cortex during active discriminationterm·mth and passive viewingterm·mth of visual stimuli. Head-fixedterm·mth mice demonstrated robust discriminationterm·mth for many hundred trials per day after initial taskterm acquisition. To record from multiple neurons during operant behaviorterm with single-trial resolution and minimal artifacts, we built a sensitive microscope for two-photonterm·mth calciumterm imagingterm·mth, capable of rapid tracking of neurons in three dimensions. We demonstrate stable recordings of cellular calciumterm activity during discriminationterm·mth behaviorterm across hours, days, and weeks, using both synthetic and genetically encoded calciumterm indicators. When combined with molecular and genetic technologies in mice (e.g., cell-type specific transgenic labeling), this approach allows the identification of neuronal classes in vivo . Physiological measurements from distinct classes of neighboring neurons will enrich our understanding of the coordinated roles of diverse elements of cortical microcircuits in guiding sensory perception and perceptual learningterm·dom. Further, our method provides a high-throughput, chronicterm in vivo assay of behavioral influences on cellular activity that is applicable to a wide range of mouseterm models of neurologic disease.

Introduction

Visual cortical responses to identical stimuli in behaving primates demonstrate rapid, task-dependent modulationterm. For instance, responses can be influenced by spatial, temporal and motivational contextterm, arousal, task-difficulty, and selective attention (e.g., Richmond et al., 1983 ; Spitzer and Richmond, 1991 ; Desimone and Duncan, 1995 ; Zipser et al., 1996 ; Parasuraman, 1998 ; Liu and Richmond, 2000 ; O'Connor et al., 2002 ; Boudreau et al., 2006 ; Gilbert and Sigman, 2007 ). Response modulationterm can be observed even at the level of primary visual cortex (V1; McAdams and Maunsell, 1999 ; O'Connor et al., 2002 ; Super, 2002 ; McAdams and ReidpersName, 2005 ; Palmer et al., 2007 ; Chen et al., 2008 ; Herrero et al., 2008 ). A poorly understood feature of most studies, however, is that behavioral modulationterm of responses in nearby neurons is heterogeneousterm, both in magnitude and even in sign.

The diversity of modulatory effects in nearby neurons may be related to differences in each neuronterm's visual response properties (DeAngelis et al., 1999 ; Martinez-Trujillo and Treue, 2004 ), laminar depth (Mehta et al., 2000 ), connectivity (Bravo and Karten, 1992 ; Brown and Hestrin, 2009 ) or cell typeterm (see below). In particular, cortical inhibitoryterm cell classes may be differentially modulated by behavioral contextterm (Buia and Tiesinga, 2008 ), providing a potential mechanism underlying dynamic regulation of local information flow. Although considerable anatomical, neurochemical, and physiological diversity exists in nearby neurons, only a small number of in vivo electrophysiological studies have correlated behavioral modulationterm of neural responses with information about cell typeterm (Swadlow and Weyand, 1987 ; Mitchell et al., 2007 ; Chen et al., 2008 ). These studies have all inferred cellular identityterm from extracellular spike waveforms, which has limited scope and reliability. Intracellular recordings in vivo , while more informative, have extremely low yieldterm, and are not easily amenable to studies in awaketerm and behaving animals (but see Margrie et al., 2002 ; Crochet and Petersen, 2006 ; Poulet and Petersen, 2008 ; Harvey et al., 2009 ).

To study how diverse elements within a local cortical microcircuit are engaged to optimize visual processing across changing taskterm demands, we have developed a robust method for three-dimensional cellular imagingterm·mth of mouseterm visual cortex during operant visual discriminationterm·mth behaviorterm. The ability to visualize tens or hundreds of simultaneously recorded neurons with two-photonterm·mth calciumterm imagingterm·mth provides several advantages over extra-cellular recordings (see Section ‘ Discussion ’). First, this technique offers the possibility of recording from the same volumeterm of neurons across days and months, enabling new directions in the study of sensory perception and perceptual learningterm·dom. Cellular imagingterm·mth also facilitates labeling of anatomical identityterm (cell typeterm, cell connectivity) of recorded neurons, in combination with molecular and genetic techniques. Two-photonterm·mth imagingterm·mth of cellular and neuropil signals has recently been described in awaketerm, head-fixedterm·mth mice and rats (Dombeck et al., 2007 , 2009 ; Verhagen et al., 2007 ; Greenberg et al., 2008 ; Kuhn et al., 2008 ; Mukamel et al., 2009 ; for review, see Grewe and Helmchen, 2009 ). However, no published work has imaged cellular responses in awaketerm, head-fixedterm·mth mice during sensory discriminationterm·mth or during passive viewingterm·mth of visual stimuli.

The mouseterm is the pre-eminent mammalian model system for genetic labeling or manipulation of specific cortical cell classes (O'Connor et al., 2009 ). Cellular imagingterm·mth in mice offers immense potential for understanding cortical function in healthy mice, as well as in mouseterm models of neurologic disease (e.g., Eichhoff et al., 2008 ; Kuchibhotla et al., 2009 ). While the imagingterm·mth and behavioral techniques described here can be used in different cortical regions and using different sensory modalities, we focus on visual behaviors that allow direct comparison to the rich literature on visual behaviorterm and neurophysiology in carnivores and primates. Indeed, electrophysiological studies in anesthetized (Dräger, 1975 ; Mangini and Pearlman, 1980 ; Metin et al., 1988 ; Niell and Stryker, 2008 ; Liu et al., 2009 ) and awaketerm (Frenkel et al., 2006 ; Niell and Stryker, 2009) mice suggest that mouseterm visual cortical neurons demonstrate similar properties to those in carnivores and primates, despite an overall decrease in spatial acuity (Prusky and Douglas, 2004 ; Niell and Stryker, 2008 ). While freely moving mice have been shown to discriminate between oriented gratings (Reuter, 1987 ; Prusky and Douglas, 2004 ; Wong and Brown, 2006 ), no operant behavioral technique currently exists for visual discriminationterm·mth in head-fixedterm·mth mice. Visual discriminationterm·mth tasks using head fixation would significantly facilitate chronicterm, three-dimensional cellular imagingterm·mth during behaviorterm, in combination with accurate monitoring of eyeterm position.

Here, we describe a GO–NOGOterm·mth visual discriminationterm·mth taskterm in head-fixedterm·mth mice, suitable for cellular imagingterm·mth studies of visual perception, perceptual learningterm·dom, taskterm engagementterm, and taskterm difficulty. We show that mice will perform this taskterm (or passively view the same stimuli) for several hundred trials a day, and we demonstrate quantitative monitoring of neural and behavioral responses across hours, days and months. Combining visual behaviorterm and imagingterm·mth techniques with molecular and genetic technologies in mice (for review, see O'Connor et al., 2009 ) will enable new translational approaches to dissecting the cellular mechanisms underlying visual behaviorterm at the level of cortical microcircuits.

Training procedures

Mice were first handled for 3–14 days (>5 min on most days, or until mice began to groom while being held). Waterterm scheduling then began, in which mice were given a total of 25 cc/kg waterterm per day, during the training sessionterm (see below) or handling (via syringe with soft plastic tip). To achieve head restraint, awaketerm, headposted mice were led into a custom semicircular plastic tube containing support for forepaws and a plastic shield to prevent grasping of the lickspout, and both headpost ends were clamped to 1/2″ posts (Thorlabs) using thumbscrews. The cylinder was held in place by beaker clamps (VWR), and adjusted to minimize stress on neck muscles. An accelerometer glued to the cylinder detected body motionterm ( Figure S2 in Supplementary Material). Waterterm was administered using a syringe or a lickspout at the onset of the restraint period, which was gradually extended from 5 min to >1 h over the course of 1–2 weeks. Several mice could be restrained simultaneously on the same table, further minimizing stress and training time. Once mice sat quietly for extended periods of time, automated training began (for additional details on training procedures, see Section Stable Visual Discriminationterm·mth Behaviorterm in Head-Fixedterm·mth Mice and Figure 3 ).

Eyetracking

Eyetracking was performed using an ISCAN eyetracking system (240 Hz) coupled to a macro lens, positioned along the body axis of the mouseterm (Figure 1 A). The eyeterm was illuminated using 940 nm LEDs positioned near the eyeterm and out of the way of the monitor. A 940 nm narrow-band filter (Edmund) was placed in front of the eyetracking camera to block infra-red laser light emanating from the pupil. A second camera collected images of the eyeterm at lower rates (20 Hz) via the ISCAN monitor output. This provided a simple, low-tech solution for detecting large horizontal eyeterm displacements (Figure 2 D), and could be combined with less expensive eyetracking cameras (see also Stahl et al., 2000 ; Sakatani and Isa, 2004 ). We took a collapsed, horizontal cross-section of the eyeterm from the 20 Hz images, located the pupil boundaries in each frame, and took the midpoint as the pupil position (Figures 2 D,E). This provided an offline means of tracking pupil position and rejecting trials with large eyeterm movements. In general, horizontal eyeterm movements ( Figure S1 in Supplementary Material) and, to an even greater extent, vertical eyeterm movements (not shown) were rare in mice during passive viewingterm·mth, similar to eyeterm stability during passive viewingterm·mth in rabbits (Cano et al., 2006 ).

Online ISCAN estimates of eyeterm rotation using pupil position were contaminated by small translational motions (<300 μm) during licking or chewing, which could be removed by online tracking and subtraction of the corneal reflection spot generated by LED illumination ( Figure S2 in Supplementary Material). Pupil displacement estimates using online or offline software were converted into angular displacements by correcting for off-axis camera location and assuming that the center of eyeterm rotation was 1.09 mm behind the pupil (Stahl et al., 2000 ). For synchronization of eyetracking, visual stimulation, behavioral responses, and imagingterm·mth rigs, ISCAN outputs and all other triggers were digitized (2 kHz, National Instruments) and sorted offline (MATLAB, The MathWorks).

Behavioral analyses

Targetterm stimuli were upwards drifting gratings, and non-targetterm stimuli were identical gratings drifting in other directions (see Figures 3 and 4 ). Hit rateterm (HR; fraction of trials with licks following targetterm presentation) and false-alarmterm rates (FA; fraction of trials with licks following non-targets) for each non-targetterm stimulusterm type were calculated for each block of 43 trials. The number of active discriminationterm·mth trials per sessionterm (Table 1 and Figure 4 ) only included trials in blocks with HRs exceeding 0.7. A useful measure of behavioral discriminabilityterm of targets from non-targets is given by d ′ = norminv(HR) − norminv(FA), where norminv() is the inverse of the cumulative normal function (Green and Swets, 1966 ). Values for HR and FA were truncated between 0.01 and 0.99. Higher d ′ values indicate better discriminabilityterm (maximum d ′ = 4.65). To estimate orientationterm thresholdterm on a given day (Figure 4 C), we performed a linear fit to d ′ values (for all non-targets with d ′ < 2.5) and estimated the angular distance from the targetterm predicted to generate a value of d ′ = 1 (see Section ‘ Taskterm Acquisition and Long-term Stability ’).

Surgery and indicatorterm delivery

Expression of genetically encoded calciumterm indicatorterm, YC3.6

Viral expression of the genetically encoded calciumterm indicatorterm (GECIterm) yellow cameleon 3.6 (pAAV-CBA-YC3.6-WPRE, Kuchibhotla et al., 2008 ; original FRET based probe described in Nagai et al., 2004 ) was achieved in the left primary visual cortex, as follows: Mice were anesthetized and the scalp resected using aseptic technique. Approximately 1 μl of YC3.6 was injected over 10 min though a ∼0.5 mm burrhole (2.7 mm lateral, 0.5 mm anterior to lambda), between 200 and 400 μm deep, using a glass pipette (20 μm tip) and a Picospritzer (<5 psi). After leaving the pipette in place for an additional 10 min, the pipette was removed and sutures applied to skin margins.

Headpost and other implants, craniotomy

A titanium headpost (grade 2, 0.05′ thick, 0.95 g; Figure 1 C) was implanted using aseptic technique, as follows: Mice were anesthetized with an initial dose of ketamine/xylazine (100 and 5 mg/kg), maintained on isoflurane (1.5% in 100% O 2 ), and positioned in a stereotactic apparatus on a heating blanket. Atropine was administered (0.2 mg/kg, IM) and eyes were protected with ointment (Vetropolycin). Following resection of skin and clearing of skull fascia, a skull screw (00-96, Plastics One) was cemented in place placed on the anterior/right bone plate. In some mice, subdural electrocorticogram electrodes and a connector (Plastics One) were positioned over the right visual cortex in order to obtain gross measures of brain state (not shown). The skull was then covered with a thin layer of cyanoacrylate (Vetbond) and allowed to dry, followed by cementing of the headpost in an axis approximately parallel with the skull over the left primary visual cortex. White dental cement powder (Lang) mixed 3:1 with black powder paint (Pearl Arts #101463) was applied for structural support and to prevent stray light from the monitor from entering through the skull. O-rings (#13 and #15; McMaster-Carr) were glued to the headpost concentric with the center of the targeted craniotomy, to form a well for imagingterm·mth (Figure 1 C) and to provide light-tight coupling with a #17 O-ring glued to flexible light shielding material (Thorlabs) that could be wrapped around the imagingterm·mth objective. All wound margins adhered to the perimeter of the headpost and dental cement, and were treated with Vetropolycin prior to recovery. Buprenorphine injections (4 × 0.05 mg/kg) and prophylactic injections of antibiotics (cefazolin 500 mg/kg) were administered, and mice were single housed and allowed to recover with freely available waterterm mixed with sulfatrim antibiotic (1:32) for >5 days before waterterm scheduling recommenced.

Following habituation to head restraint and training in the visual discriminationterm·mth taskterm (Figure 1 A), a 3-mm diameter craniotomy was performed over primary visual cortex. Procedures were similar to headpost surgery except that dexamethasone (8 mg/kg, IM) was also applied. Care was taken to minimize dural tears or bleeding. Either sterile ACSF (HEPES-based, glucose-free), saline, or 1.5 or 3% agarose (type III-A, Sigma-Aldrich) was applied and a 5-mm glass coverslip (WPI) was sealed over the craniotomy using dental cement. The mouseterm was allowed to recover for at least 1–2 days before imagingterm·mth.

In some experiments (Figure 1 A), we performed a bolus loading of the calciumterm indicatorterm Oregon Green BAPTA-1 AM (OGB1-AMterm; Invitrogen) with 10% DMSO and Pluronic F-127 (Stosiek et al., 2003 ; Histed et al., 2009 ), 200–300 μm below the cortical surface, under light isoflurane anesthesia. Passive viewingterm·mth and/or behaviorterm sessions began 1–2 h after recovery from surgery in the home cage, and lasted 2–5 h. Re-injection on subsequent days was achieved by removing the coverslip and penetrating the pre-existing agarose during dye injection, which minimized additional dural bleeding.

Two-photonterm·mth calciumterm imagingterm·mth

Imagingterm·mth hardware and software

The system for two-photonterm·mth imagingterm·mth in behaving mice is shown in Figure 1 B. In vivo calciumterm imagingterm·mth was conducted with a homebuilt two-photonterm·mth microscope. For optimal detection sensitivity, we used a two-inch optical path (2′ lenses and hardware for Thorlabs, 2″ filters and dichroics from Semrock and Chroma), and gallium arsenide photomultiplier tubes (PMTs, 2× increased sensitivity; Hamamatsu #H7422P-40MOD) coupled to electronic shutters (Uniblitz) to prevent PMT damage. The microscope permitted rapid alternation between blue/green and green/red dichroic cubes, in order to isolate signals from CFP or GFP (blue), YFP or OGB1-AMterm (green), or sulforhodamine (red; FF01-457_50, FF01-542_50, and FF01-629_53, respectively; Semrock). We achieved high excitation efficiency using an Olympus 25× (NA 1.05) or Nikon 16× (NA 0.8) objective together with a Mai Tai laser (830 nm for YC3.6, 800 nm or 920 nm for OGB 1-AM) with group delay dispersion compensation (Deep See ‘pre-chirp’ module, Newport). Scanning galvanometers (Cambridge Technology) provided a frame rate up to 64 Hz (32 × 32 pixels/frame, pixel dwell time: 16 μs), which required passive cooling of mirrors and active cooling of mirror drivers (using a chiller, WAtronix).

To achieve complete three-dimensional imagingterm·mth of small volumes, we scanned the objective in a trapezoidal pattern using a piezo-scanner (Physik Instrumente, P-721.LLQ; 48 μm displacement, 3.5 μm/planeterm; Figure 1 B) controlled by a PC computer running custom-written Labview software, synchronized to output triggers sent from the two-photonterm·mth acquisition computer. Image acquisition was controlled by a modified version of ScanImage (Pologruto et al., 2003 ) and MATLAB (The MathWorks). To maintain constant fluorescenceterm excitation across multiple depths and to avoid passing laser power during the two ‘flyback’ frames, we often modulated laser power with a Pockels cell (e.g., between 16 and 13 mW in Figure 6 ), which was controlled by the MATLAB program.

Imagingterm·mth acquisition and analysis

All image analyses were performed in MATLAB. Imagingterm·mth during behaviorterm typically involved three to four runs, each lasting 25 min (∼200 trials/run, 100000 frames/run). Three-dimensional volumes (14 planes) were acquired at 4 Hz. Stable visual discriminationterm·mth behaviorterm in head-fixedterm·mth mice

Behavioral setup

Head-fixedterm·mth mice could be trained to discriminate between gratings drifting in different directions, using a ‘GO–NOGOterm·mth’ operant discriminationterm·mth taskterm (see Section ‘ Materials and Methods ’). The taskterm structure is described in Figure 3 A. Water-scheduled mice were trained to lickterm following presentation of an upward drifting ‘targetterm’ gratingterm, and to suppress licking following ‘non-targetterm’ gratings drifting in other directions. Correct detection of a targetterm (‘hit’) was indicated with a pure tone (2 kHz) and rewarded with a drop of waterterm (5–6 μl), while responses to non-targetterm stimuli (‘false-alarms’) were indicated using white noise.

For initial training sessions, we presented only targetterm stimuli (at 0.5 Hz), and delivered a drop of waterterm automatically following each presentation. The inter-stimulus interval was then gradually extended by several seconds across sessions, while maintaining high response rates (>80%). At this point, we switched to an operant discriminationterm·mth taskterm, by (1) making reward presentation contingent on a lickterm response, and (2) by presenting non-targetterm stimuli as well as targetterm stimuli. For several sessions early in training, a mild airpuff stimulusterm and an additional time-out period (∼2 s) were also used to discourage responses to non-targetterm stimuli. Early lickterm responses following stimulusterm presentation, which could diminish accuracy and hasten lick-related brain motionterm (see below) and eyeterm motionterm ( Figure S2 in Supplementary Material), were discouraged during training by aborting trials in which licking occurred in the first several hundred milliseconds (Figures 3 A,B). We maintained 7–8 s between trials to allow for previous lickterm responses to abate (and to permit previous evoked calciumterm signals to fully decay back to baseline). While we did not penalize licking prior to stimulusterm onset, most mice typically did not lickterm for several seconds prior to stimulusterm onset (e.g., Figure 3 B; but see Figure 6 A for an example of more sustained licking between trials).

About half of the mice tested (13/25) learned to perform the visual discriminationterm·mth taskterm, but with variable learningterm rates. Training proceeded as follows: First, mice were rewarded for licking in response to drifting targetterm stimuli, using an inter-stimulus interval that was gradually increased from 2 to 5–8 s. Subsequent training involved gradually increasing the similarity of target and non-target stimuliterm·def in 5–15 sessions by presenting non-targetterm stimuli that were (1) orthogonal to targetterm and stationary, (2) orthogonal and of increasingly similar speed to targetterm, (3) of increasingly similar orientationterm to targetterm gratings and of identical speed.

The latter part of this learningterm curve is shown for four mice, in sessions employing easy non-targets that were orthogonal to targetterm stimuli and of equal speed, followed by sessions in which non-targets were presented at five increasingly difficult orientations (solid lines). In all mice, d ′ values improved over days using easy (orthogonal) non-targetterm stimuli (Figure 4 A, dashed lines). Discriminationterm·mth rates in sessions involving more difficult non-targets (including 45° non-targets) began at an intermediate baseline performance and improved, suggesting partial transference of initial learningterm (Figure 4 A, solid lines). In a separate group of nine mice, we found that mice could perform the discriminationterm·mth taskterm routinely for several months to over a year, and typically performed on the order of 300 trials/day (Figure 4 B, Table 1 and Section ‘ Materials and Methods ’; over 1000 trials or 2 h in some sessions). These data suggest that head-fixedterm·mth visual discriminationterm·mth learningterm and steady-state performance can be reliably measured across many sessions – a key factor enabling chronicterm calciumterm imagingterm·mth studies of neural correlates of perception and perceptual learningterm·dom in the same neurons (see below), in both wild-type mice and in mouseterm models of disease and aging.

Variation of behavioral contextterm

The above taskterm can be readily modified to study context-dependent modulationterm of responses by varying taskterm difficulty (proportional of ‘difficult’ vs. ‘easy’ non-targetterm trials), or taskterm engagementterm (presence/absence of reward). We varied taskterm engagementterm using interleaved blocks of 43 trials in which mice either actively discriminated target and non-target stimuliterm·def, or passively viewed these same stimuli (Figure 3 A). During passive viewingterm·mth, a solenoid removed the lickspout from reach (Figure 1 B), and mice quickly learned to stop licking in response to targetterm stimuli until the following ‘engaged’ block began, suggesting that the learned stimulusterm association is not purely reflexive (Figure 3 C). Three-dimensional motionterm correction

A major obstacle in our early efforts developing two-dimensional calciumterm imagingterm·mth during behaviorterm involved systematic, lickterm response-induced brain motionterm artifacts. Subsequently, each planeterm (acquired at 64 Hz) could be individually translated in 3Dterm, allowing correction for motions occurring faster than 4 Hz (see Section ‘ Materials and Methods ’).

The improvements in recording stability during behaviorterm gained by incorporating three-dimensional imagingterm·mth are illustrated in Figure 5 . A local volumeterm was loaded with a green synthetic calciumterm indicatorterm (OGB1-AMterm), and astrocytes were co-loaded with sulforhodamine (SR101term; Nimmerjahn et al., 2004 ), a red, calcium-insensitive fluorescent dye that generates a constant average fluorescenceterm level in the absence of brain motionterm (Figure 5 A). Discussion

We have described a method for achieving robust, chronicterm two-photonterm·mth calciumterm responses from layer II/III cortical neurons in head-fixedterm·mth mice performing a visual discriminationterm·mth taskterm. This technique offers many new opportunities for using molecular and genetic techniques to map and manipulate identified classes of neurons during well-controlled operant behaviorterm. Below, we consider the benefits, shortcomings and possible improvements in the choice of (1) species (mice), (2) behavioral paradigm (head-fixedterm·mth, visual GO–NOGOterm·mth), and (3) recording modality (3Dterm two-photonterm·mth calciumterm imagingterm·mth) to study circuit mechanisms underlying sensory perception, perceptual learningterm·dom, and perceptual dysfunction.

Benefits of studying perceptual mechanisms using cellular imagingterm·mth in mice

Why use cellular imagingterm·mth in mice to study mechanisms underlying sensory perception? The major advantage lies in the unique accessibility to genetically modified animals. For example, information on cell typeterm (e.g., GABAergicterm neurons and subtypes, Figure 2 ; O'Connor et al., 2009 ) and potentially inter-areal connectivity (Brown and Hestrin, 2009 ) of recorded neurons can be directly visualized in vivo in transgenic mice. Incorporating information on cell identityterm may help to explain the variability in context-dependent responses across nearby neurons. Two-photonterm·mth imagingterm·mth offers ease of access to most neurons in layer II/III across the lissencephalic cortex. Over nine retinotopically organized visual areas exist in mice, allowing comparative studies across multiple cortical areas. The physiological properties of secondary visual areas are largely unexplored (Wang and Burkhalter, 2007 ), and may demonstrate increased multisensory responses (Brady et al., 2009 ) and variations in context-dependent modulationterm (cf. O'Connor et al., 2002 ). As imagingterm·mth techniques continue to improve (cf. Figure 1 B), the small extent (∼0.5–2 mm 2 ) and relatively thin laminae of mouseterm visual cortical areas could conceivably allow near-complete, simultaneous imagingterm·mth of every neuronterm within layer II/III of a given area during behaviorterm. Our methods could also lend a new dimension to many of the genetic mouseterm models of neurologic disease that currently exist (e.g., Eichhoff et al., 2008 ; Kuchibhotla et al., 2008 ), providing a high-throughput, chronicterm assay of perceptual influences on single neurons throughout the course of disease progression or therapeutic treatment.

Several factors must be considered when selecting particular mice and mouseterm strains for head-fixedterm·mth visual discriminationterm·mth (Deacon, 2006 ; Wong and Brown, 2006 ). Inbred mice, while demonstrating more uniform behaviorterm, may have retinal deficits or diminished capacity for learningterm compared to more outbred strains (e.g., Deacon, 2006 ). Thus, when choosing a strainterm, and when pre-selecting individual mice for training, a compromise must be reached between ease of handling and acclimating to head restraint, and how quickly the animal learns a taskterm (Van Dam et al., 2006 ).

Benefits of using GO–NOGOterm·mth visual discriminationterm·mth in head-fixedterm·mth mice

The visual discriminationterm·mth taskterm developed here for head-fixedterm·mth mice recapitulates many of the advantages of head-fixedterm·mth behaviors in primates. First, we found that mice will perform hundreds of trials per sessionterm (up to ∼1000 per sessionterm) for several months to a year, allowing the averaging of visual responses across sessions that may be necessary to reveal more subtle behavioral effects. Note that the motivation of mice to perform the taskterm was strongly dependent on waterterm scheduling, and increasing sessionterm duration must be balanced with the potential for greater false-alarmterm rates. Although we are unaware of any protocol for training a mouseterm to visually fixate, we found that eyeterm movements were small relative to the spatial frequencies of the presented stimuli, and any trials containing occasional large eyeterm movements could be rejected offline (see Section ‘ Materials and Methods ’). Our use of sine wave stimuli (vs. square-wave) during recordings further decreased the possibility of strong, local luminance transients induced by eye-movements. Thus, our technique provides sufficiently well-controlled recordings of visual responses in awaketerm/behaving animals to enable some comparisons with the wealth of primate visual studies that are most often carried out in non-anesthetized animals. While the GO–NOGOterm·mth taskterm employed here has disadvantages compared to two-alternative forced-choice (2-AFCterm·mth) tasks commonly used in primates and freely moving mice, our taskterm is relatively easy for mice to learn. Many of the strategies developed in the course of our work could be applied to the development of 2-AFCterm·mth visual behaviors in head-fixedterm·mth mice using, for example, a track-ball and virtual reality environment (Harvey et al., 2009 ) or multiple lickterm response ports (P. Reinagel et al., personal communication). The use of response indicators other than licking (e.g., heading motionterm, left/right lever press using forepaws) may also help mitigate challenges associated with lick-induced brain motionterm. Complementary methods allowing two-photonterm·mth calciumterm imagingterm·mth of visual responses and other calciumterm activity in freely moving rats and mice have recently been published (Engelbrecht et al., 2008 ; Flusberg et al., 2008 ; Sawinski et al., 2009 ). Imagingterm·mth in freely moving animals may allow for easier implementation of naturalistic behaviors in the future, albeit with additional limitations in image quality, visual stimulusterm control, and number of trials per sessionterm as compared to head-fixedterm·mth operant behaviors.

Mice demonstrated improved visual thresholds and taskterm performance over the course of many days, which will prove useful in future studies of perceptual learningterm·dom. The learningterm rates were slower than for a similar olfactory taskterm in head-fixedterm·mth mice (Komiyama et al., 2007 ). These differences could be due to the greater behavioral salienceterm of different odors as compared to initial visual stimuli employed in our taskterm. While differences in stimulusterm speed (Section ‘ Taskterm Acquisition and Long-term Stability ’) were useful for attaining initial discriminationterm·mth of stimuli, varying of other stimulusterm parameters such as luminance, or behavioral factors such as running (Niell and Stryker, 2010 ), may further increase visual salienceterm and shorten acquisition time. In addition, the behavioral techniques described in this paper can likely be automated, allowing high-throughput training of large numbers of mice in the home cage (Meier et al., 2009 ). Future experiments could conceivably decrease mouse-to-mouse variability and user-intensive training/surgical procedures by imagingterm·mth a larger number of (locally or commercially acquired) mice that arrive pre-trained and already expressing calciumterm indicators. Transgenic mice with cell-type specific expression of sensitive GECIs are expected to become commercially available soon, and may replace more labor-intensive approaches (virus injection or in utero electroporation).

Head-fixedterm·mth visual studies may offer improved stimulusterm control compared to other head-fixedterm·mth preparations in rodents, such as tactile studies during active whisking (e.g., Harvey et al., 2001 ; O'Connor et al., 2010 ), allowing enhanced differentiation of internally vs. externally mediated changes in neural activity in different behavioral contexts (but see Ferezou et al., 2006 ), and more direct comparison with behavioral neurophysiology experiments in higher mammals. Although mice are nocturnal and have less acute vision than carnivores or primates (Prusky and Douglas, 2004 ; Wong and Brown, 2006 ), we and others have demonstrated reasonably fine neural and behavioral discriminationterm·mth of specific stimulusterm features such as orientationterm (e.g., Dräger, 1975 ; Mangini and Pearlman, 1980 ; Niell and Stryker, 2008 ; Liu et al., 2009 ).

Benefits of two-photonterm·mth calciumterm imagingterm·mth using various indicators

We found that robust calciumterm responses from visualized neurons can be recorded across hours and days during visual discriminationterm·mth, using both synthetic calciumterm dyes and GECIs. The choice of whether to use synthetic dyes or GECIs depends on the research question at hand. For example, neurons bulk-loaded with OGB1-AMterm dye are typically brighter than neurons expressing GECIs, and thus can be imaged with less laser power and potentially less cell damage. Further, OGB1-AMterm loads every cell in a local volumeterm (∼300 × 300 × 300 μm 3 ), thus providing greater yieldterm than GECIterm experiments where only a subset of neurons are been labeled using viral or electroporation techniques. Additionally, we found that clear, stable visual responses could be obtained from the dendrites of GECI-expressing neurons in awaketerm mice ( Movie S1 in Supplementary Material). As GECIs designed to report activity at individual synapses become available (e.g., Dreosti et al., 2009 ), cellular integration of behaviorally modulated synaptic input could be explored using the head-fixedterm·mth imagingterm·mth approach described here.

Many GECIs such as TN-XXL (Mank et al., 2008 ) and YC3.6 (Figure 7 ; Nagai et al., 2004 ) are ratiometric, which is helpful in canceling out residual motionterm artifacts. In our experience, however, three-dimensional imagingterm·mth during behaviorterm was still necessary for ruling out motion-related artifacts systematically linked to lickterm responses. Non-ratiometric GECIs and synthetic dyes may have greater utility in experiments involving operant behaviorterm when co-expressed with calcium-insensitive fluorescent proteins in order to normalize signals during rapid cell motionterm and slow drift (Knopfel et al., 2006 ). The onset of a bout of licking appeared to generate an apparent sinking of the recorded neuronterm out of the planeterm of imagingterm·mth (Figure 5 ), implying that changes in jaw position may be the cause of the motionterm artifactterm (see also Figure S2B in Supplementary Material). Imagingterm·mth at high rates, as well as maintaining a pressurized cranial window, are all important in obtaining clean spontaneous and evoked signals before and during onset of lickterm responses. The 3Dterm volumeterm imagingterm·mth method described in this paper differs from a previous 3Dterm technique that selectively recorded neural activity at pre-determined cellular spots (Göbel et al., 2007 ). The latter technique, while providing higher yieldterm (large number of imaged neurons), would not be effective during operant behaviorterm involving licking, unless it was combined with a predictive model of cell motionterm (Fee, 2000 ; Dombeck et al., 2007 ).

The observed improvement in behavioral orientationterm thresholds across sessions, combined with the ability to image calciumterm indicatorterm signals across sessions in behaving animals, should open the possibility of studying neural correlates of visual task-related plasticity and learningterm. However, calciumterm indicators also act as calciumterm buffers that, in some cases, can perturb native calciumterm dynamics (Holthoff et al., 2002 ; Pologruto et al., 2004 ; but see Schmidt et al., 2003 for an example of negligible perturbation using OGB-1). These exogenous calciumterm buffers may also enhance or suppress synaptic plasticity (Tonkikh et al., 2006 ). Plasticity experiments would benefit from in situ estimation of the indicatorterm concentration (Stosiek et al., 2003 ) and calciumterm affinity (Pologruto et al., 2004 ; Hendel et al., 2008 ). While these potential changes in native calciumterm dynamics using certain indicators could also modify neural activity, initial electrophysiological experiments in vivo have shown little effect of bulk loading of calciumterm indicators on basic cortical response properties (Ohki et al., 2005 ; Sato et al., 2007 ). For example, Ohki and colleagues observed typical orientationterm tuning curves in single-unit recordings from cat visual cortical neurons loaded with OGB-1 AM (Ohki et al., 2005 ).

Finally, while calciumterm signals do increase monotonically with spike rate, dye and calciumterm kinetics are slow compared to spike recordings (∼10 s of ms onset, 100–1000 s of ms decay).
