phenomenonnonauditory effects of noise in laboratory animals
claim-typeexistence
mechanismNoise engages the sympathetic branch of the autonomic nervous system, producing a generic stress response like that of other stressors — “Noise activates the sympathetic division of the autonomic nervous system, producing a stress response with physiological characteristics similar to those triggered by other sensory and psychosocial stimuli (35, 46, 163, 164).”
mechanismChronic noise keeps the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis activated, so circulating stress hormones stay abnormally high and cause downstream organ-system problems — “The almost universal finding that noise increases stress hormone levels suggests that chronic noise-induced activation of the HPA axis might cause a variety of problems because of abnormally high levels of circulating stress hormones.”
mechanismSensory input sets the organism's arousal level, and noise reaches many organ systems through that arousal pathway — “A key function of sensory systems input is to maintain an ethologically appropriate level of arousal (123), and noise affects a number of organ systems and can impact nearly every area of biomedical and behavioral research via this mechanism.”
mechanismEven quiet background sound masks conspecific communication signals, impairing the animal's ability to communicate — “Under this scenario, even a relatively quiet acoustic signal might impair an organism's ability to communicate, perhaps explaining some of the adverse effects of noise (37).”
relation — “We echo this sentiment as it relates to laboratory animals.”
differs innoise → strain — WHETHER THE VARIABLE IS A NUISANCE OR AN INSTRUMENT, and the paper offers both readings in one sentence: recognise noise 'in order to either MINIMIZE noise as a confounding variable or, in some cases, TO USE NOISE EXPERIMENTALLY as a stress manipulation'. The same environmental fact is a threat to one design and the manipulation of another — “Whatever the area of study, from immune response to social behavior, the potential impact of environmental noise on the process should be recognized in order to either minimize noise as a confounding variable or, in some cases, to use noise experimentally as a stress manipulation”
replicationreplicated
limitationThe review is explicitly selective rather than exhaustive on the auditory-system literature. — “This overview is not an exhaustive summary of the literature concerning the effects of noise on the auditory system.”
limitationMuch of the nonauditory evidence comes from human subjects, and its generalisability to animals is not established. — “The degree to which some of these observations can be generalized to animals remains to be determined.”
limitationThe acoustic environment produced by different caging systems has not been compared, so cage-level exposure is not known. — “Further research is needed to compare the acoustic environment as a function of different caging conditions, especially given the increased use of isolator caging.”
limitationThe evidence base is not yet strong enough to support precise noise guidelines. — “However, before more precise guidelines can be developed, more systematic research on noise affects is necessary.”
limitationStrains used as standard laboratory animals go progressively deaf for genetic reasons, with secondary effects reaching brain neurochemistry and social behaviour. — “For example, several inbred mouse strains commonly used in biomedical research (e.g., C57BL/6, DBA/2, and BALB/c) experience a genetically determined, progressive hearing loss that can lead to secondary changes in systems ranging from brain neurochemistry to social behavior.”
limitationThe review's recommendation is precautionary rather than evidential: assume the exposure could cause any of the effects described. — “researchers and animal care workers should assume that noise exposure could cause any or all of the changes described, potentially impacting a wide variety of biomedical research areas.”
limitationThe review defines its central term as broadly as possible, by stipulation. — “For the purpose of this review, noise can be defined broadly as any sound in the environment.”

findingThe authors' own recording inside a rat shoebox cage in a normal housing room put baseline background noise at about 42 dB SPL across the spectrum. — “Baseline levels of noise across the spectrum appear to be around 42 dB SPL.”
findingA routine husbandry action, snapping a wire-top lid onto a shoebox cage, generates a near-100 dB SPL low-frequency impulse with roughly 80 dB SPL of energy extending into the ultrasonic range up to 40 kHz. — “For example, the common practice of simply snapping a wire-top cage lid to a plastic shoebox cage can produce an intense low-frequency noise impulse of nearly 100 dB SPL, with energy of about 80 dB SPL at frequencies up to 40 kHz (Fig. 2).”
findingMost facility noise traces to two sources: staff activity itself, and animals vocalising or banging cages in reaction to people being present. — “These findings suggest that the majority of noise in animal facilities is due to two leading sources: the direct activity of personnel using the facility and the increased activity (vocalization. cage rattling or banging) of animals in response to the presence and actions of people.”
findingDBA/2 mice begin losing hearing at about two weeks of age and are profoundly deaf by three to four months, so strain choice determines what the animal can hear. — “For example, relative to other strains, DBA/2 mice demonstrate substantial hearing loss beginning at the onset of hearing (around 2 weeks of age) and progressing rapidly until they are profoundly deaf by around 3 to 4 months of age (51, 152, 200).”
findingTwo common rat strains differ in opposite directions by roughly 20 dB: F344 hear low frequencies better and FBN hybrids hear high frequencies better. — “F344s show approximately 20 dB better hearing at low frequencies (4 kHz) whereas FBNs show approximately 20 dB better hearing at higher frequencies (32 kHz; 187).”
findingNonauditory effects appear well below the 85 dB guideline, with chronic exposure to as little as 65 dB(A) sufficient to induce them. — “Ising and Kruppa (94) indicated that chronic exposure to as little as 65 dB(A) of noise can induce a variety of nonauditory effects.”
findingRearing animals in the constant white-noise masking background recommended by some facilities may itself harm auditory development by masking vocalisations and other natural input. — “The suggestion from this work is that rearing animals in a constant white noise, masking background (which is being recommended in some animal facilities) might have negative consequences for normal development of the auditory system by effectively masking out the normal input to the ear from vocalizations and other sources.”
quantity42 dB SPL — Baseline background noise level measured inside a rat cage in a standard animal housing room — “Baseline levels of noise across the spectrum appear to be around 42 dB SPL.”
quantitynearly 100 dB SPL — Peak low-frequency impulse produced by attaching a wire-top lid to a shoebox cage — “For example, the common practice of simply snapping a wire-top cage lid to a plastic shoebox cage can produce an intense low-frequency noise impulse of nearly 100 dB SPL, with energy of about 80 dB SPL at frequencies up to 40 kHz (Fig. 2).”
quantity≥ 85 dB — Sound pressure level above which prolonged chronic exposure is recommended to be treated as capable of causing permanent cochlear hearing loss — “As a result of human and laboratory animal studies, current recommendations are that chronic exposure to noise levels at ≥ 85 dB sound pressure level (SPL) for any prolonged period of time can cause permanent hearing loss through cochlear damage (131).”
quantity75% — Proportion of acoustically primed CBA mice that seized and died on re-exposure to the 123-dB noise — “For example, if CBA mice (which hear normally and are not susceptible to audiogenic seizures) are exposed to a 123-dB noise for 2 min when they are 24 to 42 days old, that same exposure repeated 5 days later leads to seizures and death in 75% of the animals (80).”
quantitymore than 90% — Share of mammals used in research that are mice, whose hearing extends into the ultrasonic range beyond human hearing — “The more commonly used laboratory animals, such as mice, which make up more than 90% of mammals used in research (120), have hearing with frequency sensitivity in the ultrasonic range (≥ 80,000 Hz), far beyond the range of the human ear (20 to 20.000 Hz).”
quantity60 episodes/0.5 h at ≥ 90 dB — Rate of loud noise episodes recorded in animal facilities in the morning, rising above 100 dB in the evening — “Pfaff and Stacker (148) recorded noise levels in animal facilities and found approximately 60 episodes/0.5 h at ≥ 90 dB in the morning and > 60 episodes/0.5 h at ≥100 dB in the evening.”

variablespecies and strain of the exposed animal (moderator) — “These changes are determined partly by species and strain; partly by noise intensity level, duration, predictability, and other characteristics of the sound; and partly by animal history and exposure context.”
