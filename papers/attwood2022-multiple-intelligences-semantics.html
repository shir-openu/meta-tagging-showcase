Introduction Gardner’s (1983 , 2006) theory of multiple intelligencesterm [hereafter referred to as multiple intelligencesterm (MIterm) theory] has had substantial influence on K-12 curriculum design and implementation. This influence has been promoted, at times, through professional development for in-service teachers and in teacher educationterm·key programs for preservice teachersterm·key (see, for example, “Project Zero” from the Harvard Graduate School of Education; Multiple Intelligenceterm Schools, 2022 ). The seven and then eight intelligences that comprise the theory that Gardner (1983 , 2006) posited have influenced teachers, students, and teacher educators across the world ( Brualdi Timmins, 1996 ; Rousseau, 2021 ). This influence has generated a long-standing debate among educational psychologists about the efficacy and validity of MI theoryterm. While there has been substantial discussion of the varying points of view of educational psychologists and teachers on MI theoryterm, there is not nearly as much discussion of this issue for teacher educators who address MI theoryterm in what may be the only educational psychology course that preservice teachersterm·key take during their teacher educationterm·key program. This paper reevaluates the debate about the efficacy and validity of MI theoryterm, and makes recommendations for how this debate can be introduced and discussed in a survey course on educational psychology for preservice teachersterm·key.

Rationale Most preservice teachersterm·key seem to have a favorable opinion of Gardner’s MI theoryterm ( Rousseau, 2021 ). This is despite the long-standing critical debate on the theory’s efficacy and validity from the technical perspectives of educational psychologists ( Schulte et al., 2004 ; Bordelon and Banbury, 2005 ; Visser et al., 2006 ; Waterhouse, 2006 ; McGreal, 2013 ; Rogowsky et al., 2015 ; Willingham et al., 2015 ; Rousseau, 2021 ). Additionally, a study by Luo and Huang (2019) of English as a second language (ESL) teachers’ self-perception of MI theoryterm and the uses of the defined multiple intelligencesterm found either ambiguity or no significant correlation between MI theoryterm and its instructional strategies, further supporting the critics of MI theoryterm based on it not having statistical validity. These findings prompt the question of why? Why do preservice teachersterm·key tend to have an overall positive opinion of MI theoryterm? This paper addresses these questions as a conceptual issue in semanticsterm·key.

Part of the answer for preservice teacherterm·key perceptionterm of MI theoryterm may be in the perspectives they developed in either their teacher educationterm·key program or in their school placement ( Rousseau, 2021 ). This may present as having heard about anecdotal success in applying MI theoryterm, and then assuming it is beneficial in and of itself without critical evaluation of whether theories such as MI theoryterm are supported in psychological science. The other part of the answer is in the educational psychological research that supports MI theoryterm in qualitative principle but not in the technical aspects of statistical validity. These can become conflated when preservice teachersterm·key go into their field placements and hear the words differentiationterm and learning stylesterm·key and semantically linking these to preferences in an unquestioning assumption because of perceived popularity despite technical differences among these concepts in psychological science, especially in reference to the word intelligence in context of MI theoryterm. This second reason reinforces the first because preservice teachersterm·key’ perceptionterm of the effectiveness of learning stylesterm·key and related theories, especially MI theoryterm, tends to be influenced by anecdotal experienceterm ( Menz et al., 2021 ).

The issue, again, seems to be predicated on the semanticsterm·key of MI theoryterm in its use of the word intelligences. Role of Anecdotal Evidence Psychology is sometimes viewed as a helping profession as it is often applied across human services fields ( Sternberg and Dennis, 1997 ). Teacher educationterm·key is historically rooted in the field of psychology. As a social science, psychology’s use of validity tests of pedagogical strategies is important. That being stated, teaching—as a helping profession—is also as much about the teacher’s intuition informed by their teaching experience and their anecdotal observations of a lesson’s effects historically and in the present on student learning. Anecdotes matter because teaching can differ from textbook theories, even if those psychological theories have validity. As such, historical study and psychological study are linked, and this can often include individual stories ( Vaughn-Blount et al., 2009 ). Anecdote should not replace psychological science, but neither should quantitative studies be used to the exclusion of all anecdotal experienceterm as an important source of information about teaching practice. These are different constructs that each hold a place in educational psychology. The stories of anecdotal observation by Stock (1993) and Weber (1993) , for example, are examples of the importance of anecdotes in assessing teaching and learning. Similarly, the importance of the anecdote and anecdotal method has been established for its helpful role in teacher professional knowledge construction ( Doecke et al., 2000 ; AttwoodpersName, 2021 ).

Although the effect of anecdotal evidence can be substantially influential, the quality of anecdotes can be difficult for individuals to discern. Hornikx (2018) noted in their study testing the effect on participants’ opinions using “high-quality” and “low-quality” anecdotal evidence that “contrary to theoretical expectations, readers were not found to be sensitive to the quality of anecdotal evidence: The similar and dissimilar variants were equally persuasive” (p. 333). Such a finding might offer further basis for why MI theoryterm has been found to be popular with preservice teachersterm·key when considering the findings of Rousseau (2021) . It also might underscore some of the concerns of critics of MI theoryterm who suggest that MI theoryterm lacks sufficient scientific basis for its claims regarding multiple intelligencesterm ( Waterhouse, 2006 ; Willingham et al., 2015 ). The critique of validity in strategies such as MI theoryterm is important and should be emphasized in educational psychology, but the qualitative and anecdotal observations of teachers who are daily in their classrooms seeing favorable results from differentiated practice is at least as important and should likewise have a place in educational psychology curriculum. Sometimes, the semanticsterm·key of the framework becomes an obstacle and, instead, the observed results in the classroom might matter more. It is in that perspective, then, that MI theoryterm continues to influence some educators in presenting MI theoryterm with what appears to be an overall favorable impression of the theory’s possibilities.

Although there has been a substantial phase of popularity in the critique of “neuromyths” prompted, in part, by critical studies of MI theoryterm during the 2000s and 2010s, this appears to be waning and perhaps partially reversing, at least temporarily ( Gardner, 2020 ; Rousseau, 2021 ). An example of the popularity of critique has been in the widely assigned textbook Educational Psychology: Developing Learners by Ormrod et al. (2020) , which by 2020 was in its tenth edition. This textbook for undergraduate preservice K-12 teachers and psychology students features a critique of “neuromyths” in the first chapter. Among the critiques is one focused on learning stylesterm·key theories and Gardner’s (1983 , 2006) MI theoryterm. There was some ambivalence in the critique, but it was more in the negative.

Considering how widely used this textbook is in survey courses in educational psychology in the United States, this raises the earlier question, again: Why do preservice teachersterm·key still seem to have an overall positive opinion of MI theoryterm, as Rousseau (2021) discussed in a review of several international studies. Part of the reason is what may be an emphasis on qualitative interpretation rather than quantitative inquiry in educational psychology courses for preservice K-12 teachers because they will be teaching in one school and informed by their own individual teaching experience and the experience of their supervising teacher to adapt to the conditions of their local classrooms in consultation with colleagues. In this scenario, the individual experience in full-time student teaching might take precedence, at least in the near term, over the studies that critiqued MI theoryterm that they may have read previously.

Achieving this connection in their educational psychology course is important for encouraging preservice teachersterm·key to emphasize evidence-based pedagogical frameworks (e.g., UbDterm·key and UDLterm) in their lesson planning and practice as classroom teachers. This connection should be established so preservice teachersterm·key may contextualize anecdotal experienceterm with studies in psychological science to foster understanding of evidence-based frameworks, they might be less likely to continue to have misconceptions about MI theoryterm and learning styles theoryterm ( Menz et al., 2021 ; Rousseau, 2021 ).

When teacher educators teach the importance of both qualitative and quantitative inquiry in educational psychology, preservice teachersterm·key can decide what will work for them in the classroom. Contextualizing technical scientific validity matters for how to translate those studies to what seems to work and can most efficiently be communicated for and in the K-12 classroom. Preservice teachersterm·key should be taught frameworks for implementing effective instruction and classroom management, and this can involve teaching about MI theoryterm to get preservice teachersterm·key thinking about intentional, inclusive curriculum design. Thus, teacher educators should probably emphasize what MI theoryterm has to offer practically to encourage effective differentiationterm of instruction.

There are increasingly more examples of demonstrating connections between research that tests MI theoryterm and presents favorable findings of MI theoryterm. A study of secondary school economics teachers found that there was a statistically significant difference using a multiple analysis of variance in teachers’ use of a bodily-kinesthetic approach to teaching economics in secondary school based on the teacher’s teaching experience ( Yidana et al., 2022 ). In a study of English as a foreign language (EFL) teachers, the researchers found that “only teachers of logical-mathematical type were influenced by their dominant intelligence type and other intelligence types did not exert a significant influence on the types of activities being implemented in the classes” ( Dolati and Tahriri, 2017 , p. 1). It was also notable that the majority of the teachers in that study “did not have any education at the university level about the MI theoryterm” ( Dolati and Tahriri, 2017 , p. 7). This indicates further support for the logical-mathematical intelligence construct in MI theoryterm because the teachers in that study were not taught about MI theoryterm in their teacher educationterm·key program suggesting little to no prior influence on their opinion of the theory. In a study comparing EFL students internationally and applying MI theoryterm in a cross-cultural analysis, Wu and Alrabah (2009) applied MI theoryterm to identify students’ learning preferences to develop a differentiated teaching approach. These studies further suggest the popularity of MI theoryterm for its pedagogical potential in successfully implementing a differentiated curriculum. The question, then, is prompted again to what the extent of overlap between MI theoryterm’s concept of plural intelligences is and whether it is being used in practice as nearly synonymous with preferences . This is especially relevant when considering MI theoryterm’s relationship to—and operationalization of—learning styles theoryterm ( Silver et al., 1997 ).

Precision in Word Choice of Intelligences or Preferences at Center of MI Theoryterm Debate If the word intelligences were replaced with the word preferences in MI theoryterm, there might be a shift in the debate of MI theoryterm’s efficacy. The semanticsterm·key of MI theoryterm’s context in teacher educationterm·key may be part of the reason why many preservice teachersterm·key may tend to have a favorable perceptionterm of MI theoryterm because the concept of intelligences seems to be used synonymously with preferences. Nevertheless, a student’s preference in modality (i.e., auditory) may still not necessarily be the best approach for their learning depending on the topic, skill, and activity being taught, though there is some conflicting evidence ( Rogowsky et al., 2015 ). Approaching MI theoryterm from a classroom practitioner perspective is generally how preservice teachersterm·key will interact with the concept of MI theoryterm. With this understanding, it makes sense that technical validity studies would be of less importance if the classroom observations of MI theoryterm’s implementation appear to support its use for K-12 classroom instruction.

Differentiationterm is, in part, about aligning instruction to student preferences as much as anything else for the purpose of maintaining or increasing student engagement. This has been one of the benefits of MI theoryterm in teacher educationterm·key. The debate about MI theoryterm has perhaps, ultimately, been more about the semanticsterm·key of the word intelligences and observations of anecdotal benefits of its implementation. The technical aspects of quantitative validity are important, but not always to a level that supersedes the qualitative effectiveness of the theory’s application if the discussion is focused on preservice teachersterm·key’ observations of its use in classrooms. If students appear engaged in the content when using MI theoryterm, then perhaps that makes more of a positive difference for in-service teachers. Even when validation tests do not meet or exceed the 95 percent confidence interval, there will still be anecdotal cases of apparent success in the theory’s implementation (e.g., Furnham, 2009 ).

Semanticsterm·key of MI Theoryterm Affect Assessment Conceptualization Student engagement is often part of formative assessment in K-12 classrooms, hence part of the reason that MI theoryterm became popular in the late twentieth century as a potential way to address differentiated engagement. This is where more recent discussion in the MI theoryterm debate has trended. When teachers assess students based on MI theoryterm or related, though different, learning styles theoryterm in their classroom, Papadatou-Pastou et al. (2018) observed that there can be a dissonance in the preferences being assessed by the teacher and the students’ self-assessment of their own preferences on the given task. If using the MI theoryterm as a lens, preferences can become conflated with the definition of intelligence used in MI theoryterm. In a broader discussion of self-assessment, Coutinho et al. (2021) addressed the related issue of the Dunning-Kruger effect in the debate on learning stylesterm·key theories when student preferences are emphasized to the point that they could potentially over-rely on self-assessment.

If the word intelligence is changed to preference, it seems that the issue of validity may not be as relevant, and self-assessments could be checked against the teacher’s assessment of the given task or assignment so that the unit grade is not based on the student’s self-assessment. The word change would also likely address Willingham et al. (2015) in their critique of learning stylesterm·key theories and the inferred influence of Gardner’s (1983 , 2006) MI theoryterm. To avoid this semanticterm·key issue, preservice teachersterm·key would benefit more from being taught UDLterm which addresses student preferences within an evidence-based framework so that their learning is personalized to what will more likely generate effective learning instead of relying solely on a student’s self-reported preference which may not be accurate or best for their achievement of the learning goals.

When testing MI theoryterm to ascertain if it holds validity in assessing instructional strategies aligned to its concept of multiple intelligencesterm, many studies have found no widespread statistical correlation or validity in MI theoryterm’s concepts of multiple intelligencesterm and instructional strategies aligned to those purported intelligences (e.g., Waterhouse, 2006 ; Willingham et al., 2015 ; Luo and Huang, 2019 ). However, some case studies have found statistical significance for some of the MIterm domains (e.g., Dolati and Tahriri, 2017 ; Yidana et al., 2022 ), or partial significance for one but not the other domains (e.g., Rogowsky et al., 2015 ). While divergent findings further the debate of MI theoryterm, this again seems to suggest the issue of the semanticsterm·key of this theory and its overlap with learning styles theoryterm, as the use of the word “intelligence” in MI theoryterm can cause confusion from a technical standpoint in psychological science where the word preferences may be a better fit for Gardner’s theory, especially in context of its overlapping application with learning styles theoryterm.
