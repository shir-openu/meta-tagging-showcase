phenomenonpopulation-level psychological distress during an epidemic, before any intervention has been designed for it— “The aim of this study was to survey the general public in China to better understand their levels of psychological impact, anxiety, depression, and stress during the initial stage of the COVID-19 outbreak.”
claim-typeexistence— “In total, 53.8% of respondents rated the psychological impact of the outbreak as moderate or severe; 16.5% reported moderate to severe depressive symptoms; 28.8% reported moderate to severe anxiety symptoms; and 8.1% reported moderate to severe stress levels.”
method — evidence— “We adopted a cross-sectional survey design to assess the public’s immediate psychological response during the epidemic of COVID-19 by using an anonymous online questionnaire.”

contribution — evidence— “This study included 1210 respondents from 194 cities in China.”
mechanismTHE INSTRUMENT SETS THE CEILING ON THE CLAIM: IES-R measures a stress reaction to a named event and DASS-21 measures self-reported state, so what the study can report is how people scored themselves during three days, not what their mental health was— “Psychological impact was assessed by the Impact of Event Scale-Revised (IES-R), and mental health status was assessed by the Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21).”
mechanismSNOWBALL SAMPLING FROM STUDENTS PROPAGATES THROUGH ONE NETWORK: the survey was seeded with university students and passed onward, so the sample inherits the structure of that network - which is why the authors report oversampling of peers and reduced generalisability to less educated people— “A snowball sampling strategy, focused on recruiting the general public living in mainland China during the epidemic of COVID-19, was utilized. The online survey was first disseminated to university students and they were encouraged to pass it on to others.”
mechanismANONYMITY FORECLOSED THE FOLLOW-UP: ethical requirements on anonymity and confidentiality prevented collecting contact details, so the prospective study the authors say would be ideal was not merely unfunded but structurally impossible after the fact— “Furthermore, it would be ideal to conduct a prospective study on the same group of participants after a period. Due to ethical requirements on anonymity and confidentiality, we were not allowed to collect contact details and personal information from the respondents.”
builds-on previous surveys of the psychological impact of SARS and influenza outbreaks, which the authors reviewed and extended with COVID-19-specific questions rather than replacing— “Previous surveys on the psychological impacts of SARS and influenza outbreaks were reviewed \ [18, 21, 24\]. Authors included additional questions related to the COVID-19 outbreak.”
sensepsychological impact - IN THIS PAPER A SCORE ON THE IMPACT OF EVENT SCALE-REVISED, an instrument built for post-traumatic stress reactions to a specific event, and NOT a clinical assessment. The authors say so themselves: self-reported levels may not align with assessment by mental health professionals— “Another limitation is that self-reported levels of psychological impact, anxiety, depression and stress may not always be aligned with assessment by mental health professionals.”
senseassociated with - THE PAPER'S OWN CEILING AND IT KEEPS TO IT EVERYWHERE. The design is cross-sectional and the analysis is UNIVARIATE linear regression, so nothing here is adjusted for anything else and no temporal order is observed. `Female gender ... associated with a greater psychological impact` is a difference between groups measured on one day— “We used linear regressions to calculate the univariate associations between sociodemographic characteristics, physical symptom and health service utilization variables, contact history variables, knowledge and concern variables, precautionary measure variables, additional health information variables, and the IES-S score as well as the…
sensethe general public - RECRUITED FROM UNIVERSITY STUDENTS OUTWARD, which the authors name as oversampling one network of peers. The sample is 1,210 people across 194 cities and it is explicitly not a picture of the general population— “There was an oversampling of a particular network of peers (e.g., students), leading to selection bias. As a result, the conclusion was less generalizable to the entire population, particularly less educated people.”
