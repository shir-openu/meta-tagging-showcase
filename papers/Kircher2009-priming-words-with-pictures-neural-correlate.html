phenomenoncross-modal semantic priming (picture-word)
claim-typeexistence
mechanismPresentation of a prime activates its concept, and that activation spreads automatically along associative pathways in the semantic network to related concepts, so the related target is already pre-activated when it appears. — “The priming effect is explained by an automatic “spread of activation” between related word representations within the semantic network that occurs at short stimulus onset asynchrony [SOA < 400 ms; see Neely, 1991 for a review].”
mechanismThe haemodynamic decrease for related pairs arises because pre-activation lowers the demand on semantic memory retrieval and selection, so less neural work is needed than for unrelated pairs. — “Hence, the response suppression is caused by reduced demands for the semantic memory retrieval or is the result of selection caused by the automatic spread of activation [Matsumoto et al., 2005 ; Tivarus et al., 2006 ].”
mechanismThe shared right fronto-temporal deactivation is attributed to the easier semantic processing of associatively related concepts, an ease that does not depend on which modality delivered the prime. — “we assume that the deactivation of the right fronto‐temporal regions for related pairs reflects the ease of semantic processing for associatively related concepts that is independent of modality”
relation — “Thus, our data support an amodal semantic system based on the hypothesis of Caramazza et al. [ 1990 ] rather than the hypothesis of multiple semantic systems [e.g., Paivio, 1991 ].”
relation — “Thus, our data support an amodal semantic system based on the hypothesis of Caramazza et al. [ 1990 ] rather than the hypothesis of multiple semantic systems [e.g., Paivio, 1991 ].”
relation — “First of all, similar to other studies we found no difference between cross‐modal and unimodal priming on a behavioral or neural level [Caramazza et al., 1990 ; Vandenberghe et al., 1996 ].”
differs incross‐modal → unimodal — WHERE IN THE BRAIN, NOT HOW MUCH - both produce a behavioural priming effect and the comparison of the two revealed NO behavioural difference, while the neural suppression sits in bilateral fronto-parietal regions for unimodal and in the right inferior frontal gyrus for cross-modal — “a priming effect for cross‐modal and unimodal word‐pairs”
replicationreplicated
limitationThe sample was restricted to male subjects, so gender effects on the reported lateralisation and language processing are untested here. — “We restricted our sample to male subjects due to the ongoing debate about gender specific effects for almost all types of cognitive demands, including language processing and lateralization [Baxter et al., 2003 ; Bermeitinger et al., 2008 ; Laws, 1999 ; Lloyd‐Jones and Humphreys, 1997 ].”
limitationOnly artifact (man-made object) concepts were used, so the findings may not extend to natural/living concepts or to manipulable versus non-manipulable relations. — “In the current study, we used only artifacts to investigate the influence of different visual modalities on the processing of semantic associations.”

findingA significant behavioural priming effect was obtained in both the picture-word and the word-word conditions. — “In both modalities, subjects were faster in the related compared to the unrelated condition (cross‐modal condition: t (15) = 2.92, P < 0.01; unimodal condition: t (15) = 2.66, P < 0.05).”
findingThe size of the cross-modal priming effect was statistically indistinguishable from the unimodal one. — “A further t ‐test of priming effect sizes in cross‐modal and unimodal conditions revealed that the picture–word priming effect did not differ from the word–word priming effect ( t (15) = −0.45, P = 0.66”
findingUnimodal word-word priming produced response suppression across bilateral fronto-parietal cortex. — “Subtraction of the unimodal related from the unimodal unrelated trials (unrelated > related; excl. masked with unimodal word‐nonword) revealed bilateral fronto‐parietal clusters of activation”
findingCross-modal picture-word priming produced response suppression confined to the right inferior frontal gyrus. — “The contrast cross‐modal unrelated > cross‐modal related (excl. masked with cross‐modal picture‐nonword) revealed activation in the right IFG.”
findingThe direct contrast between the two modalities yielded no significant modality-specific clusters. — “The comparison of both modalities revealed no differences between uni‐ and cross‐modal semantic priming.”
findingBecause the two modalities shared a common bilateral fronto-tempo-parietal deactivation network and differed nowhere, the data favour a single amodal semantic system over multiple modality-specific semantic systems. — “Thus, our data support an amodal semantic system based on the hypothesis of Caramazza et al. [ 1990 ] rather than the hypothesis of multiple semantic systems [e.g., Paivio, 1991 ].”
