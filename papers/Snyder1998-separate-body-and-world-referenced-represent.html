limitationAn efference-copy contribution during the passive body-under-head rotation cannot be formally excluded. — “Equal contributions from efference copy signals under both conditions cannot be ruled out, because it is possible (although unlikely) that the animal actively assisted during the body-underhead movement.”

content-observations: finding · quantity · variable · definition · key-term — grounded in the full text
findingVisual gain-field modulation in the two adjacent parietal areas is referenced to different frames: LIP gain fields are body-referenced and 7a gain fields are world-referenced. — “Here we report that modulations of visual signals (gain fields) in two adjacent cortical fields, LIP and 7a, are referenced to the body and to the world, respectively.”
findingIn LIP, 22 of 59 cells were significantly modulated and almost all of them (20) were body-referenced, with only one world-referenced and one mixed. — “In cortical area LIP, 22 of 59 cells showed significant (P , 0:05) modulation for at least one of the two rotations: 20 body-referenced, one worldreferenced, and only one mixed bodyand world-referenced.”
findingIn area 7a the pattern reversed: of 11 significantly modulated cells, nine were world-referenced, one body-referenced and one mixed. — “In area 7a, 11 of 31 cells showed significant modulation: nine worldreferenced, one body-referenced, and one mixed.”
findingThe two reference frames are carried by separate cell populations rather than coexisting on the same cells, since only two of the 90 cells tested showed significant modulation in both frames. — “Out of 31 7a and 59 LIP cells tested, only two cells showed significant effects in both reference frames.”
findingWorld-referenced modulation was both more frequent (40% vs 18% of cells) and about 50% stronger in 7a than in LIP. — “In a separate series of experiments in which only head-plus-body rotation was performed (see Table 1), world-referenced modulation was more than twice as frequent in 7a than in LIP (40% versus 18% of cells, respectively)”
findingIn 7a, world-referenced signals explained only part of the gain-field modulation produced by active head rotation, unlike in LIP where body-referenced signals accounted for it entirely. — “Worldreferenced signals account for only part of the effect during active head rotation (Fig. 3).”
findingEighteen of the 57 modulated 7a cells were modulated after active head rotation but not after passive body-plus-head rotation, pointing to an additional proprioceptive or efference-copy contribution. — “Of these 57 cells, 18 showed significant modulation after active head but not after passive body-plus-head rotation, suggesting an influence of proprioception or efference copy.”
findingWorld-referenced gain fields in 7a appeared only after rotations fast enough to exceed the vestibular threshold, in six of seven cells tested, identifying the signal source as vestibular. — “In six of seven cells, gain fields appeared after rotation above but not below threshold, strongly suggesting a vestibular input.”
findingThe authors conclude that posterior parietal cortex segregates spatial information according to the behavioural function it will serve (gaze control versus navigation) rather than by sensory modality. — “We propose that, in posterior parietal cortex, information is segregated based on the specific functional role it will serve.”
