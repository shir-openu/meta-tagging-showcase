phenomenonlegislative voting defection— “Section III proposes a theory of legislative voting in the European Parliament that predicts the circumstances under which an MEP will defect from her parliamentary party or her national party.”
claim-typemechanism— “The results reveal that national parties are more able to enforce their wishes on their MEPs in systems containing electoral institutions that provide these principals with strong controls—specifically, in systems with closed-list proportional representation, small districts, or centralized candidate selection.”
method — evidence— “To explain these MEP defection decisions and to test the predictions of the theoretical model, we can estimate the following fixed-effects logit model:”
mechanismCentralized candidate selection gives the national leadership a credible power to punish, so the MEP anticipates the cost and complies with national instructions— “Following the previous discussion, we can assume that the more centralized the system of candidate selection in the hands of the national party leadership, the more likely it is that the national party will be able to punish that MEP, regardless of the type of electoral system or the size of the MEP's electoral district.”
mechanismThe MEP weighs the cost of defecting from each principal and votes for whichever side leaves her better off, an expected-utility calculation— “Accordingly, each MEP makes a vote decision based on the overall expected utility of her action.”
mechanismWhere individual candidate votes decide electoral fortunes, reelection-seeking legislators cultivate a personal following independent of the party, which loosens party discipline— “In systems in which votes cast for individual candidates—as opposed to votes cast for a party list—significantly influence each candidate's electoral fortunes, legislators seeking reelection have strategic incentives to cultivate personal support among the electorate.”
builds-on Gabel and Hix (2002), "The European Parliament and Executive Politics in the EU"— “This builds on the model in Matthew Gabel and Simon Hix,”

findingMEPs elected under institutions that weaken their national party's grip — a candidate-centered electoral system, decentralized candidate selection, and large district magnitude — are less likely to vote against their European party group.— “The less control a national party can exert over its MEPs as a result of the electoral institutions—if there is a candidate-centered electoral system, decentralized candidate selection, and a large district magnitude— the less likely it is that an MEP from this party will vote against the majority position of her European party group.”
findingThe same three institutions push in the opposite direction for the other principal: candidate-centered voting rules, decentralized candidate selection, or a large district each make an MEP more likely to vote against her own national party.— “In all models, if an MEP is elected under candidate-centered voting rules, with decentralized candidate-selection rules, or in a district with a large…
findingFor defection from the national party the two institutions do not add up independently: only the combination of a candidate-centered system with decentralized selection raises defection, since either a party-centered system or centralized selection alone is enough to suppress it.
findingLonger service in the European Parliament lowers defection from the European party group but raises defection from the national party.
findingDomestic constitutional design — semipresidentialism in France and federalism in Germany — does not carry over into MEP voting behaviour, since MEPs from both countries defect less than average from their national parties.
findingDefection is rare overall: MEPs almost always voted with the majorities of both their European party group and their national party.
