Introduction In all sensory systems, damage to peripheral organs leads to compensatory cortical reorganization and increased cortical sensitivity to the non-damaged (spared) sensory input 1 – 9 . The computational modelterm generates testable hypotheses for SOMterm and PVterm plasticityterm after NIHLterm Our results showing increased gainterm and recovered response thresholdterm·def of PNs after peripheral damage are consistent with previous studies 10 , 11 , 14 , 16 , 17 , 20 , 21 . However, the central goal of our study is to highlight the plasticityterm of the different cortical IN subtypes and how it may contribute to the observed PNterm recoveryterm from peripheral damage. Thus, we investigated the possible role of inhibitory circuitry on the recoveryterm of A1term L2/3 PNs after NIHLterm. To this end, we first used a computational modelterm to investigate the possible changes in inhibitionterm that can achieve PNterm high gainterm. Past modeling work has shown that a decrease in the recurrent inhibitionterm in a recurrently coupled cortical modelterm results in higher PNterm gainterm 60 , 61 , thus, an NIHL-induced reduction in inhibitionterm could be a candidate mechanism. However, strong recurrent PNterm connections can yield unstable, runaway behavior if a recurrently coupled inhibitory population is unable to dynamically track and cancel the recurrent excitatory activity 53 , 60 , 62 . As such, the stabilization role for inhibitionterm poses constraints on how reduced inhibitionterm can safely modulate cortical gainterm·def because a global and non-specific inhibitory reduction could lead to instability and pathology, such as epileptic-like activity 54 . Thus, a simplified two-population modelterm, consisting of generic excitatory and inhibitory neurons, would likely fall short of capturing the experimental results presented thus far 60 (Fig. 2 ). As a result, we started our investigation by considering a computational network of leaky integrate-and-fire neuron models (“Methods”) of three subpopulations of neurons (PNs, PVs, and SOMs) (Fig. 3a ). PNs and PVs received a feedforward presynaptic drive, and we modeled sound level by increasing the firing rate of the feedforward inputs. We considered four sound levels: none (no sound), low, medium, and high. The control (pre-exposure) spiking behavior of the network lies in an asynchronous (stable) regime, with the firing rate of all three populations increasing monotonically with sound level (Fig. 3b, c ). Because peripheral damage reduces the intensity of peripheral sensory drive from the cochlea to the AN and the brain, noise-induced damage in our modelterm is implemented by decreasing the feedforward (evoked) and background (spontaneous) firing rates. We modeled recoveryterm after NIHLterm, as observed 10 days after NIHLterm (Fig. 2 ), either as a static depolarization or hyperpolarization of individual cortical neurons. The underlying cause behind these inputs could be due to the intrinsicterm or synaptic mechanisms that restore neuronal threshold post-NIHL. Consistent with our prediction on the constraints on how reduced inhibitionterm can safely modulate cortical gainterm·def, we found that depending on the magnitude and sign of these currents to each subpopulation, the network spiking behavior varied drastically, from oscillatory and unstable, to asynchronous and stable with high gainterm (Fig. 3d ). Discussion Cell-type-specific plasticityterm of cortical IN subtypes after NIHLterm: strengths and limitations of our approach Extensive evidence supports divergence, complementarity, and division-of-labor between the cortical IN subtypes in terms of their tuning properties 41 , 73 – 75 and their role in contextual and adaptive cortical sound processing 43 , 46 , 76 , 77 . However, despite the established role of reduced GABAergic signaling in A1term plasticityterm after cochlearterm damage 11 , 21 , 27 , 28 , 78 , 79 , the plasticityterm of different IN subtypes in cortical recoveryterm remained unknown. Our approach with computational modeling, in vivo imaging, and in vitro electrophysiology provides a comprehensive account of the distinct plasticityterm of the different cortical IN subtypes during cortical recoveryterm. This distinct, cell-type-specific plasticityterm supports the notion that PVs, SOMs, and VIPs play distinct roles in cortical recoveryterm after peripheral damage. Namely, after noise trauma, we found enhanced sound-evoked activityterm·var in PNs (Fig. 2 ), PVs (Fig. 4 ), and VIPs (Fig. 8 ) but reduced sound-evoked activityterm·var in SOMs (Fig. 5 ). Based on the known sequentially organized inhibitory cortical network 35 , where VIPs neurons inhibit SOMs, SOMs inhibit PVs and PNs, and PVs inhibit other PVs and PNs, our results are consistent with the notion that the underlying SOMterm → PVterm → PNterm and SOMterm → PNterm circuits support a cell-type-specific plasticityterm mechanism in which, robust PVterm activity provides network stabilityterm by balancing PNterm activity. Moreover, the vastly decreased SOMterm activity allows for increased PVterm and PNterm gainterm, which supports stabilityterm and high gainterm. The VIPterm → SOMterm → PNterm disinhibitory pathway completes the task, whereby robust VIPterm activity enables reduced SOMterm activity. Although the causality or even the contribution of the proposed roles of PVs, SOMs, and VIPs and recovered A1term sound processing were not tested here, they will be evaluated in future work. Moreover, we are aware that the recoveryterm of cortical sound processing after noise trauma is influenced by subcortical plasticityterm mechanisms 10 , 32 , which are also outside the scope of this study. In sum, our study is the first to identify the cell-type-specific plasticityterm of the different cortical IN subtypes in cortical recoveryterm after noise trauma; and it suggests distinct roles for these interneurons in this process. As such, these results highlight a strategic, cooperative, and cell-type-specific plasticityterm program that may contribute to restoring cortical sound processing after cochlearterm damage and, thus, may provide novel cellular targets that may also aid in the development of pharmacotherapeutic or rehabilitative treatment options for impaired hearing after NIHLterm. Several key cortical circuit features are consistent with our proposed hypothesis regarding the proposed roles of PVs as stabilizers and SOMs as modulators of A1term plasticityterm after NIHLterm. PNs and PVs are embedded into very similar synaptic environments. Both receive the excitatory drive from upstream areas 35 , 36 , and both receive strong recurrent excitation, as well as PV- and SOM-mediated inhibitionterm 66 . This symmetry places PVs in a strategic position for monitoring and stabilizing PNterm activity 60 . On the other hand, SOMs are in a better position to modulate the cortical inhibitionterm and excitation, such that a higher gainterm state of PNs can be achieved without compromising the stabilityterm of the network. For example, the increased PNterm gainterm via reduced SOMterm activity would also lead to increased PVterm firing because of the strong excitatory feedback from PNs to PVs. In turn, these hyperactive PVs would then stabilize the recurrently activated PNs. Further, consistent with our modelterm predictions (Fig. 3e ), suppression of SOMs enhances cortical plasticityterm without compromising the stabilityterm of the network 54 , 80 , 81 , whereas suppression of PVs can result in uncontrolled network activity, evidenced by unstable ictal-like events in most 54 , but not all cases 82 . Although we do not present a measure of network stabilityterm, future studies could utilize optogenetic perturbations to further investigate our proposed roles of PVs, SOMs, and VIPs while also evaluating how network stabilityterm changes after NIHLterm. Namely, different light intensities could be utilized to suppress a varying fraction of PVs, SOMs, and VIPs. Runaway excitation would arise while targeting the subpopulation responsible for stabilizing the network. Moreover, the light intensity at which runaway activity occurs can be used as a proxy for network stabilityterm, with higher light intensities indicating greater stabilityterm. To assess how the network stabilityterm changes during recoveryterm, the same experiment can be repeated before and at different times after NIHLterm. Taken together, our observations are consistent with the notion, albeit not tested here, that PVs act as the stabilizers, whereas SOMs act as the modulators of A1term plasticityterm. Similarly, our proposed role of VIPs as the enablers of A1term plasticityterm after NIHLterm is consistent with previous reports showing that VIPs enable cortical plasticityterm across the sensory cortices 68 , 77 , 80 , 81 , 83 – 85 . In the visual cortex, synaptic transmission from VIPs to SOMs is necessary and sufficient for the increased cortical responses in PNs after monocular deprivation in adult mice 80 . In the somatosensory cortex, increased VIPterm activity facilitates increased PNterm activity in a mouse modelterm of neuropathic pain 86 . Here, we found the enhanced activity of A1term L2/3 VIPs after NIHLterm (Fig. 8 ), suggesting that VIPs may contribute to the enhanced PNterm activity (Fig. 2 ) via the disinhibitory pathway VIPterm → SOMterm → PNterm. Together, our results provide the first comprehensive cell-type- and circuit-plasticity mechanism that may explain, at least partially, how cortex rebuilds itself after peripheral damage. Maladaptive aspects of cortical plasticityterm Compensatory A1term plasticityterm after peripheral damage likely supports the recoveryterm of the perceptual sound-detection threshold but does not support sound processing encoded by precise spike timingterm, such as modulated noise or speech, and restricts hearing in a noisy environment 10 , 11 , 15 , 87 , 88 . Interestingly, A1term SOMs, which are critically important for sound processing encoded by precise spike timingterm of neuronal firing 38 , 46 , 89 , 90 , showed reduced sound-evoked activityterm·var after NIHLterm (Fig. 5 ). Based on these results and although not tested here, we propose that the reduced SOMterm activity after cochlearterm damage may contribute to the hearing problems after peripheral damage, such as difficulty in understanding speech and trouble hearing in noisy environments. Another maladaptive aspect of increased AC gainterm after peripheral damage is the development of tinnitus, the perception of phantom sounds 15 , and hyperacusis, the painful sensitivity to everyday sounds 16 . Both these disorders have many similarities with neuropathic pain and phantom limb syndrome 91 . Namely, these neurological disorders are developed after damage to the peripheral organs and manifest increased PNterm activity in the respective sensory cortices, suggesting a common underlying cortical circuit mechanism. In the allodynia mouse modelterm of neuropathic pain 86 , where sensory touch that does not normally provoke pain becomes painful in the spared nerve injury modelterm, SOMterm activity was drastically reduced in the somatosensory cortex. This is consistent with the notion that reduced SOMterm activity disinhibits PNs and leads to increased activity of PNs. Interestingly, selective SOMterm activation in the somatosensory cortex after nerve injury was sufficient to prevent the increased PNterm activity and mitigate the development of neuropathic pain 86 . Together, these results suggest that the reduced cortical SOMterm activity after peripheral organ damage may be a common mechanism across sensory cortices that permits the increased PNterm gainterm. Moreover, these results suggest that the modulation of A1term SOMterm activity after noise trauma could be a potential target for mitigating noise-induced tinnitus and hyperacusisterm·def. PVs and cortical plasticityterm after peripheral trauma Our results suggest that the increased PVterm sound-evoked activityterm·var 10 days after NIHLterm (Fig. 4d, k , cyan) may stabilize the increased PNterm activity (Fig. 2 ). However, 1 day after NIHLterm, we observed reduced sound-evoked PVterm activity (Fig. 4d, k , red). Since PVs initiate cortical plasticityterm in juvenile and adult brain 83 an initial reduction in PVterm activity after NIHLterm may initiate cortical plasticityterm. Consistent with this notion, a rapid drop in PV-mediated PNterm inhibitionterm as early as 1 day after cochlearterm denervation precedes the recoveryterm of cortical sound processing 10 . Moreover, recent results show reduced intrinsicterm excitability of AC L2/3 PVs 1 day after noise-trauma because of increased KCNQ potassium channel activity 31 , suggesting that this mechanism might contribute to decreased PVterm responses to sound 1 day after noise exposure. Similarly, in the visual cortex, PVs show reduced firing rates 1 day after monocular deprivation 83 , and in the somatosensory cortex, PVs also show a reduction in their intrinsicterm excitability 1 day after whisker plugging 92 . These results suggest that a rapid reduction in PV-mediated PNterm inhibitionterm may be a common feature of sensory cortices plasticityterm that plays a critical role in initiating cortical recoveryterm after sensory organ damage. A recent study 11 reported that the PVterm sound-evoked activityterm·var was reduced after cochlearterm denervation and remained reduced for two weeks. However, unlike our modelterm of noise-induced cochlearterm damage, cochlearterm denervation was induced with bilateral cochlearterm application of ouabain, which eliminates ~95% of the type 1 SGNs. Since the type and the severity of peripheral organ damage may result in heterogeneous cortical plasticityterm 7 , 10 , 16 , 21 , 30 , 93 – 99 , noise- and ouabain-induced damage to the cochlea may trigger different trajectories of plasticityterm in A1term neuronal subtypes. Another explanation for the observed differences could arise from differences in the experimental design, such as the sound-stimuli used (broadband vs. 12 kHz pure tones 11 ) and the number of PVs tracked (82 vs. 29 11 ). Overall, the observed differences point to the need for additional rigorous investigations on the role of distinct INs in A1term plasticityterm in different types and degrees of hearing loss, including unilateral vs. bilateral and noise vs. ototoxic compounds-induced hearing loss. Nonetheless, our results provide a comprehensive modelterm of cortical rehabilitation after noise trauma whereby the precise and well-timed division-of-labor and cooperativity among cortical INs may secure high gainterm and stabilityterm. Strengths and limitations of our wide-fieldterm and 2Pterm imaging studies We employed A1term wide-fieldterm transcranial imaging and A1term longitudinal 2Pterm imaging of L2/3 somata to identify the plasticityterm in the different neuronal cell types of A1term after NIHLterm. Although we found overall similar plasticityterm rules via 2Pterm and wide-fieldterm imaging after NIHLterm, wide-fieldterm imaging showed larger shifts and reduced recoveryterm in the response thresholds compared to the 2Pterm imaging. The main reason that likely explains these differences is that wide-fieldterm imaging assesses responses from different neuronal compartments (somata and dendrites) and different cortical layers, whereas 2Pterm imaging assesses only L2/ 3 somatic responses 56 . As such, wide-fieldterm imaging results might provide insights into the cell-type-specific plasticityterm of the entire A1term after NIHLterm. For example, a robust shift and lower recoveryterm in the wide-fieldterm response thresholds after NIHLterm is consistent with the notion that A1term dendrites and/or neurons residing in the cortical layers other than L2/3 may undergo more robust and longer-lasting changes than changes occurring in L2/3 somata after NIHLterm. In this context, one limitation of our study is that we studied cellular mechanisms based on distinguishing PNs, PVs, SOMs, and VIPs; however, these neuronal subtypes are not a homogeneous population 35 , 36 . Moreover, our 2Pterm studies were only limited to L2/3. Therefore, our future studies will expand to investigate different cortical neuronal subtypes in terms of their gene expression profile, projection targets, and AC layer localization. Modelterm assumptions and limitations In this work, we leveraged a computational modelterm to assist the exploration of cortical mechanisms responsible for the A1term PNterm recoveryterm following NIHLterm. At the neuronal level, we modeled recoveryterm as either depolarization or hyperpolarization of the resting membrane potentialterm induced by current injection. After peripheral injuries, such as in NIHLterm, it is possible that intrinsicterm or synaptic mechanisms of homeostatic plasticityterm could lead to such changes in the membrane potentialterm. However, our results did not show a change in resting membrane potentialterm after NIHLterm. Nonetheless, our modeling studies provided testable hypotheses that facilitated the experimental design. The computational modelterm assumes that after NIHLterm, the network must balance recoveryterm and stabilization. The assumption that cortical inhibitionterm is needed to prevent pathologic activity places the modelterm in the inhibition-stabilized network (ISNterm) regime 46 , 53 , 60 , 62 . While we have already mentioned the hypothesis that PVs are the best IN subtype to stabilize the network, after NIHLterm, synaptic plasticityterm, and other mechanisms may divert this role to alternative INs or shift the entire circuit out of the ISNterm regime. In that case, the region of viable parameter setsterm·def for the computational modelterm would grow and, as a result, would suggest alternative recoveryterm pathways (e.g., hyperpolarizing PVs). Yet, the absence of such pathways in our experimental results implies that the dynamics observed in the ISNterm regime constrain the mechanisms utilized in recoveryterm. However, it remains an open question whether the cortex lies in the ISNterm regime with inhibitionterm actively preventing unstable dynamics by balancing excitation or is simply wired to resist instability. The answer likely resides in the dynamics and relative strengths of synaptic connections. As a result, future models should account for possible heterogeneities of synaptic strengths across and within populations, as well as the diversity of short-term plasticityterm rules found across different cortical synaptic connections 72 . Therefore, combinations of computational modeling with ongoing and future ex vivo and in vivo studies in our laboratories are expected to address the synaptic mechanisms underlying cortical rehabilitation after noise trauma. However, it is also clear that in addition to synaptic mechanisms, the cell-type-specific intrinsicterm plasticityterm found in PVs 31 and VIPs (Fig. 9 ), but not in either PNs 31 or SOMs (Fig. 5 ), are consistent with contributions of intrinsicterm plasticityterm mechanisms in cortical recoveryterm. In conclusion, our results create a comprehensive framework for understanding the cellular and circuit mechanisms underlying AC plasticityterm after peripheral trauma and hold the promise to advance understanding of the cortical mechanisms underlying disorders associated with maladaptive cortical plasticityterm after peripheral damage, such as tinnitus 14 , 15 , hyperacusis 16 , 100 , visual hallucinations, and phantom limb pain 13 , 101 . Fluorescence values were extracted from each ROI for each frame, and the mean for each cell was computed. FISSA gave us two vectors of fluorescence values for the somatic and the neuropil. We weighted the neuropil vector by 0.8, as described previously 57 , 114 , 115 . The weighted neuropil vector was subtracted from the somatic vector to produce a corrected vector of fluorescence values. This FISSA-corrected fluorescence ( F ) values from each sound trial were then converted to Δ F / F values by using baseline fluorescence measured 1 s before each sound onset. We then averaged the Δ F / F values from each sound trail (5–8 trials) to get the mean Δ F / F from each neuron. To identify the sound-responsive neurons, we used a tone sensitivity index, d-prime (d’), from preexposure sessions as described previously 57 , 58 , 110 . Briefly, we presented trains of pure tones at interstimuli intervals of 3 s in pseudo-random order that spanned in the range of 4–40 kHz frequencies (500-ms long) in 0.20-octave increments at 30–80 dB SPL in 10 dB SPL steps. For each neuron, we calculated the average response amplitude from responses at and immediately adjacent to the frequency/level combination eliciting the maximum response (preferred frequency, the average of 5 values if the maximum response is observed at dB < 80, 4 values if the maximum response is observed at 80 dB). We then averaged the same number of values selected at random frequency/level locations of the frequency response area (FRA). We took the difference between these averages and iterated this process 1000 times. The tone sensitivity index, d-prime (d’), was calculated as the average of the iterated differences, and the neurons with d’ ≥ 0 only were analyzed further. We then used these sound-responsive cells to assess sound-evoked activities, such as response thresholdterm·def, amplitude, and gainterm. The sound-evoked responses were measured for 1 s after the sound onset and were defined as significant responses if the sound-evoked changes in Δ F / F were larger than the mean + 2 standard deviations (SDs) of the baseline fluorescence measured before the sound onset. Peak fluorescence signals during the 1-s period after the sound presentation were quantified as the sound-evoked response amplitude. The response gainterm was defined as the slope of response amplitudes against the sound levels and calculated as the average change in the fluorescence signals (Δ F / F %) per 5 dB SPL step starting from response thresholdterm·def 10 , 11 , 20 . Best frequency (BF)term·def was defined as the sound frequency with the maximum average sound-evoked response across all sound levels (30–80 dB SPL) 59 . For electrophysiological recordings, we used a MultiClamp- 700B amplifier equipped with a Digidata-1440A A/D converter and Clampex (Molecular Devices). Pipette capacitance was compensated, and series resistance for recordings was lower than 15 M Ω . Series resistance ( R series ) was determined by giving a −5-mV voltage step for 50 ms in voltage–clamp mode (command potential set either at −70 mV or at 0 mV) and was monitored throughout the experiments. R series was calculated by dividing the −5 mV voltage step by the peak current value generated immediately after the step in the command potential. Recordings were excluded from further analysis if the series resistance changed by more than 15% throughout the experiment. Input resistance ( R input ) was calculated by giving a −5-mV step in voltage–clamp mode (command potential set either at −70 mV or at 0 mV), which resulted in transient current responses. The difference between baseline and steady-state hyperpolarized current (Δ I ) was used to calculate R input using the following formula: R input = (−5 mV/Δ I ) − R series . The average resting membrane potentialterm ( V m ) was calculated by holding the neuron in voltage-follower mode (current clamp, at I = 0) immediately after breaking in and averaging the membrane potentialterm over the next 20 s. In the current clamp, depolarizing current pulses (0–450 pA in 50 pA increments of 1-s duration) were used to examine each neuron’s basic suprathreshold electrophysiological properties (baseline V m was maintained at −70 mV). Action potential (AP) width was calculated as the full width at the half-maximum amplitude of the first resulting AP at rheobase. The AP threshold was measured in the phase plane as the membrane potentialterm at which the depolarization slope exhibited the first abrupt change (Δslope > 10 V/s). The adaptation ratio was calculated by dividing the instantaneous frequency between the ninth and tenth AP by the instantaneous frequency between the second and third AP (f9/f2).
