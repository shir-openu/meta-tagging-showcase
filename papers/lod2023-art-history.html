Introduction The scientific discourse in object-focused and historical disciplines follows certain methods and builds a narration with objects, events, evidence and conclusions.

In Digital Humanities (DH) research this process and its specific structure has to be represented on a data level. Linked Open Dataterm·dom, Semantic Web ontologiesterm·mth and, in particular, reference modelsterm·def like CIDOC CRMterm·dom (2022) offer a precise structural vocabulary to describe cultural heritageterm·key objects and their relations. These frameworks can support GLAM institutions to make the data on their objects findable, accessible and interoperable.

However, in research projects the course of developing arguments and describing findings is crucial. We use Art History research, in particular, the project “Restaging Fashion - Digital contextualization of vestimentary sources” (Refa), as one example to show how emerging discourses within the research process can be mapped in a knowledge organization systemterm·dom and which aspects may not be formally represented. A recurring question is: What should be modeled as linked open dataterm·dom (LODterm·def) and what should be entered as free-form textterm·mth? Despite the proven utility of data standards, vocabulariesterm·key, and authority filesterm·key providing for accuracy, context, and unambiguity, there is also a need for an open space to formulate and present tentative observations. With this contribution we present how we bridge LODterm·def and full text descriptions through information visualizationterm·mth.

Semantic data modelingterm·key in Museums Semantic data modelingterm·key of a collection during an early stage of the research prompts a trade-off to be made between data standardization and semantic accuracyterm·key.Sophisticated ontologiesterm·mth like CIDOC CRMterm·dom encourage the detailed description of an object through an event-driven structure, which makes it a very powerful tool for the cultural heritageterm·key sector by providing for the possibility of adding meaning to the original data.

Since 2006, when the term Linked Data was coined (see Berners-Lee 2006), according to Daquinoet al."only a few pioneers have abandoned legacy cataloguing and archiving systems to fully embrace the Semantic Web paradigm and manage their catalogues through LOD-native management systems" (2022).

When considering the LODterm·def datasets published by The British Museum and the Rijksmuseum, who also create tools such as ResearchSpace (Oldman / Tanase 2018) and dedicated platforms to engage with their collections, it appears that these museums only share relatively shallow data models for their artworks on the web. The Rijksmuseum provides basic metadataterm·mth on objects structured in DC , EDM and LIDO schemas. Only one example is modeled deeply: Linked Art van Gogh object metadataterm·mth download . But the creators clearly state that: “both the implementation of the model as well as the use of identifiers will be subject to change” (RijksData 2022). The British Museum, on the other hand, structured its entire collection using CIDOC CRMterm·dom, but was unable to preserve its database in a consistent and accessible manner (Lincoln, 2015). Data that should be linked together is no longer accessible, making the research of scholars no longer available ( see Programminghistorian 2017).

The potential of these applications is obvious: LODterm·def allows data to be reused and redistributed, revealing relationships with other data on the Web (Berners-Lee 2006). The resources involved, however, are somewhat excessive both from a technological perspective (see De Decker 2015)and regarding the cataloging of information. Manual tagging can be performed by domain experts in a very precise manner, but it cannot be performed manually to large volumes of already existing content due to the lack of resources (Simou et al. 2017) or may not be conducted automatically due to inconsistencies and/or the lack of standardization in the datasets. This leads institutions to publish only a subset of the available information, exposing minimal metadataterm·mth (Doerr et al. 2010), which might prevent researchers from using the data and constrains them to conventional research methods.

Data-driven art historical discourse Art historical research on and with collections is deeply concerned with the history of objects and their discourse such as provenance history, history of interpretation, dating or attribution based on comparable works. This can be expressed with the event structure of CIDOC CRMterm·dom including descriptive information on the objects like dimensions, inscriptions, and alternative titles also linked to relevant vocabulariesterm·key like ICONCLASS and AAT . But a crucial part of humanities research consists of annotating that basic formal information, by elaborating and contextualizing the history of the artworks and their interpretation, often concluding in the form of a paper or monograph. Undertaking this part of research in the form of LODterm·def is very intricate and laborious. Complex ontologiesterm·mth will never be completely exhaustive since no cultural artifact or experience could be fully and finally contextualized and vocabulariesterm·key are not equally accurate and detailed for every subtopic. In addition, contexts and meanings shift over time, even during the timeframe of a research project. According to Galloway "there are some things that are unrepresentableterm·def. And the computer is our guide into that realm" (2011: 91). In terms of representing the research process it is not worth making this part machine -readable as a basis for formal queries. It is more important to present it in a way that is accessible and understandable to other researchers, which does not require this high level of formalization, because it would end up in overcomplicated queries.

Visualizing context One way to equally accommodate formal LODterm·def structures as well as full text descriptions is through custom interfaces. In particular, information visualizationterm·mth can be used for the contextualization of images, sources, texts and their relations.

Fig. 1: Draft of Refa interface As part of the Restaging Fashion project, we devised different experimental prototypes, linked specific parts of the textual description to artworks, to classification entries, to archival sources as well as to other cultural heritageterm·key objects (see Fig. 1.) with the aim to establish an overview of the context.

Instead of hiding the full text in unstructured note fields, we approach this as a starting point for the semantic contextualization of the research process.

In conclusion, certain aspects of humanities research can be properly expressed by semantic web structures. In particular quantitative information, for instance, on objects and their relations, which can be queried and expressed statistically, are worth to be modeled in triple structuresterm·mth. Other, more elaborated, reflexive, or contextualized information may require different forms of representations like visual interfaces.
