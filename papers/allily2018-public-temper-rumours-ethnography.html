Understanding the public temperterm·dom through an evaluation of rumoursterm·key: an ethnographical method using educational technologyterm·key Abstract The power of rumoursterm·key is that they can be broadly exchanged, generating a ‘public temperterm·dom’ (which is everybody’s temper without being anybody’s temper in particular). This article, therefore, describes an approach to measuring the public temperterm·dom, examining particularly the public temperterm·dom of an Arab society, namely Saudi Arabiaterm·key. It addresses the following research question: is it possible to analyse existing (scholarly) rumoursterm·key to see if they can be used as informants of the public temperterm·dom of the culture in which they exist? This question is answered ethnographically by analysing 579 Arabic online rumoursterm·key collected by students as part of their critical engagement with educational technologyterm·key. Having analysed the data, four categories emerged: the concerns , interests , attitudes and values of Saudi Arabiaterm·key. According to the literature, these four categories, taken together, constitute the emotional domain (i.e., the public temperterm·dom) of a society. Thus, a theoretical proposition (and contribution to the existing literature regarding sociology) is that rumoursterm·key mirror the public temperterm·dom of a culture, reflecting a range of emotions from simple to complex (from concerns, interests and attitudes to values). Simpler emotions (e.g., concerns) appear to be more easily affected by rumoursterm·key than more complex emotions (e.g., values). An implication of this study is that rumoursterm·key have ‘biographies’, which detail public tempersterm·dom across space and time. Rumoursterm·key are ‘records’ of public tempersterm·dom that should be read in the same way archaeologists read landscapes and remains. Although rumoursterm·key entail ill-defined information, it is feasible to well define society through such ill-defined information, meaning that something can come out of its opposite. This study offers ethnographers a new method of understanding public tempersterm·dom through rumoursterm·key, alongside conventional meaning-making symbols (e.g., poems).

Subject terms: Sociology, Social anthropology Introduction Rumoursterm·key can be widely exchanged, thus producing a ‘public temperterm·dom’, which is ‘everybody’s without being anybody’s in particular’ (Durkheim, 1947 : 102). This article, therefore, describes an approach to measuring public tempersterm·dom. Social media are a fertile ground for the dissemination of rumoursterm·key (Tufekci, 2008 ). Through social media, rumoursterm·key have experienced a high level of diffusion and have become more influential than in the past, thereby becoming worthy of serious academic research and scholarly attention (Mintz, 2002 ). Rumoursterm·key can sometimes be limited and weak but, at other times, they can hit hard, initiating battles and bringing down political systems (cf. Caplow, 1947 ; Lotan et al. 2011 ). The effect of rumoursterm·key can be destructive (Fine and Heath, 2009 ), and yet it arguably takes time for this destruction to unfold. A concern is that, if a rumourterm·dom changes public opinion, and then the rumourterm·dom is discredited, the changed public opinion can remain the same, despite being based initially on false information. Rumoursterm·key stick easily in the mind and hence people will mentally and subconsciously go back to them. Put simply, ‘Once we’ve heard an untrue claim, we can’t simply unhear it’ (Harford, Financial Times , 9 March 2017 ). Echoing these arguments, this article examines how rumoursterm·key can help us understand the public temperterm·dom of a society. It first establishes a conceptual framework for the study, explaining the key existing terms and concepts concerning rumoursterm·key and presenting the existing kinds of writing on rumoursterm·key. It then talks about the methodological aspects of this study, setting out the data collection and analysis methods. After that, it presents the key findings and engages in interpretation of these findings. It then discusses these findings and links them back to the literature. Finally, it offers concluding remarks, which outline the research in plain language, show the limitations and suggest further research paths.

Conceptual framework The term ‘rumourterm·dom’ refers to ‘information or a story that is passed from one person to another and which may or may not be true’ ( Longman Dictionary of Contemporary English , emphasis added). Rumoursterm·key may be ‘the oldest medium of mass communication of information or ideas’ (Kapferer, 1990 : 1). The literature addresses rumoursterm·key in relation to three types of information: misinformationterm·dom, disinformationterm·dom and, indeed, accurate information (Rosnow and Fine, 1976 ; Lee, 2014 ). While misinformationterm·dom refers to unintentionally made false information, disinformationterm·dom signifies deliberately made false information. In other words, misinformationterm·dom comes out of limitations in human communication, while disinformationterm·dom comes out of the human predilection for shaping one’s surroundings in one’s own interests. Disinformationterm·dom, unlike misinformationterm·dom, has the subjective intention to deceive and direct (Jowett and O’Donnell, 2006 ). It is the outcome of deliberate lies that seek to mislead. Misinformationterm·dom is the result of honest mistakes. However, the current research is not interested in unearthing the reasons behind inaccurate information. Rather, it looks into what rumoursterm·key tell us about the nature of the society where they have existed.

Studies have looked into the linguistic aspects of rumourterm·dom and have outlined a number of terms that people tend to confuse with the term ‘rumourterm·dom’. One such term is ‘humourterm·dom’. Humourterm·dom is intended to make one laugh, but rumourterm·dom is meant to make one believe (Knapp, 1944 ; Allport and Lepkin, 1945 ). Another confusing term is ‘gossipterm·dom’. Gossipterm·dom is concerned with private, trivial, pieces of information, whereas rumourterm·dom is associated with public matters (DiFonzo and Bordia, 2007 ). That said, there is overlap between these two terms and this overlap is increased by such modern ideas as ‘infotainment’ and ‘tabloidisation’. Infotainment refers to information that aims to both inform and entertain (Thussu, 2008 ). In tabloidisation, journalism does not focus only on politics and foreign affairs but also on entertainment and celebrities (Turner, 1999 ). Considering these linguistic clarifications, the current study will look into rumourterm·dom (not humourterm·dom); into what seeks to be believed (not what provokes laughter). It concentrates more on rumoursterm·key (not gossipterm·dom) that relate to public, social matters (not the private and personal).

There are certain terms associated with rumoursterm·key. One is ‘propaganda’, which is subjective information intended to trigger emotional (instead of rational) responses so as to encourage particular syntheses, influence audiences, further agendas, promote political causes and alter attitudes towards specific positions (Ross, 2002 ). Another term is ‘spin’, which is a type of propaganda associated with the psychological mechanisms of using exceedingly deceitful, misleading and controlling strategies, providing highly biased interpretations of events and/or campaigning to persuade public opinion in accord with or opposition to certain organisations or public figures (Branigan, The Guardian , 12 June 2007 ). An additional term is ‘fallacy’ or ‘fallacious reasoning’, which is the application of deficient, untrue or flawed reasoning when building up arguments (Bustamente and Dahlman, 2015 ). ‘Circular reportingterm·def’ refers to a condition wherein a piece of information seems to originate from multiple independent sources despite actually originating from one single source. ‘Doublespeak’ is a term that means intentionally reversing, masking, covering up and twisting the meaning of terms (Kehl and Livingston, 1999 ). ‘Hoaxes’ are deliberately fabricated false statements made to masquerade as the truth (MacDougall, 1958 ). ‘Fabrication’ is used in scholarly enquiry to refer to the deliberate misrepresentation of research findings by making up data. ‘Gaslighting’ is a type of manipulation that attempts to spread self-doubt in individuals or communities, with the intention of making them rethink their own memory, insight and understanding (Dorpat, 1994 ). This article is about rumoursterm·key, not ‘contemporary legends’, which have been written about by sociologists, such as Fine ( 1980 ) and Best ( 1997 ), and indeed by folklorists, historians and psychologists.

There are three types of research into rumoursterm·key. Type 1 is politically oriented research, looking into political issues surrounding rumoursterm·key and into the political use of rumoursterm·key (Briant, 2015 ). For example, Coast and Fox ( 2015 ) have shown the key role of rumoursterm·key in politics, proving that the act of spreading negative rumoursterm·key about rivals tends to be more successful than the act of promoting positive messages about one’s own party. Type 2 is psychologically driven research, which studies cognitive matters related to rumoursterm·key. By way of illustration, Bordia and DiFonzo ( 2004 ) have argued that rumourterm·dom transmission is reflective of a collective explanation process and that rumoursterm·key are ‘sense-making’ statements and attempts at solving problems. Type 3 is a lawfully oriented investigation, examining legal topics in relation to rumoursterm·key. For instance, Sunstein ( 2009 ) has discussed rumoursterm·key in line with their risk to personal reputations and according to libel and privacy laws. What is missing among these approaches, however, is a culturally driven type that examines the cultural dimension of rumoursterm·key. The present research aims to fill this gap, by looking into the relationship between rumoursterm·key and the culture wherein they exist. Previous studies have focused on the meanings and uses of rumoursterm·key, whereas the current study looks into the implications of rumoursterm·key, at what rumoursterm·key culturally imply . Hence, the research examines whether it is possible to analyse rumoursterm·key to see if they can be informants of the culture in which they exist.

Methodology The current study addresses the following research question: Is it possible to analyse existing (scholarly) rumoursterm·key to see if they can be used as informants of the public temperterm·dom of the culture in which they exist? It concentrates on what could be called ‘scholarly rumoursterm·dom’, which are defined in the current article in two ways. First, they are rumoursterm·key that present causality, in that they show relationships between causes and effects. Second, they are rumoursterm·key falsely based on research and experimentation. Scholarly rumoursterm·dom are, arguably, influential, considering that society tends to trust scholarliness. This social trust remains because scholarliness has long been an agent for change, having the power to initiate changes in wider society and push or break down cultural boundaries (Corbyn, Times Higher Education , 5 November 2009 ). Besides, scholarliness is associated with expertise and has been proved to contribute to the ‘progress’ of human society and the development of standards of living. Another reason for concentrating on scholarly rumoursterm·dom is that they, unlike non-scholarly rumoursterm·key, have a tendency to sound more ‘sophisticated’, to last for a longer time, and even be transmitted from one generation to another.

Scholarly rumoursterm·dom do not come from nowhere, nor are they without political intentions (Lasswell, 1938 ). They do not happen in a vacuum but are influenced by social norms, cultural values and political agendas. This implies that scholarly rumoursterm·dom can be reflections and, moreover, reinforcers of social realities. The norms and values of a culture can be drivers and subjects of scholarly rumoursterm·dom. Scholarliness can, hence, be misused as a tool in daily social and political games. The scholarly language can be spoken to gain social sympathy. A concern is that, when scholarly rumoursterm·dom are well incorporated into the surroundings, they then become ‘naturalised’ and hidden in the landscape (Bernardi et al. 2012 ), resulting in neglect of the politics exercised by them. Scholarly rumoursterm·dom can be implicitly objectified in, and ‘wrapped’ into, social media, penetrating the social fabric and moving across generations. If scholarly rumoursterm·dom are documented and written down, they act as archives and therefore spaces in which certain kinds of power are codified and justified. With these observations in mind, it would be politically naïve to attempt to conceptualise scholarly rumoursterm·dom in isolation from the political intentions behind them. To interpret and discuss scholarly rumoursterm·dom as isolated elements is to risk treating them as being outside the social fabric. It is therefore well worth considering both the ‘culturalisation’ of scholarly rumoursterm·dom and the ‘rumourisation’ of cultures.

The research question is addressed within an Arab context (Saudi Arabiaterm·key) that has seen the founding of ‘anti-rumour’ associations, campaigns, hashtags, workshops and studies (Al Qarnaf, 2014 ; Al LilypersName et al. 2017 ). Many members of Saudi Arabian society enjoy ‘journalistic curiosity’, i.e., they care about knowing what others do. Saudi Arabian society is a collective and communicative community where everyone cares about everyone, which makes it easier for rumoursterm·key to spread. In other words, rumoursterm·key are used in Saudi Arabian society to aid social bonding, solidarity and community (cf. Miller, 1992 ; Dunbar, 2004 ). Many Saudi Arabs seemingly have both a desire to communicate and an unwillingness to read. So, when they encounter lengthy messages, they combine their desire to communicate with their unwillingness to read by forwarding these messages without reading them deeply or checking their accuracy.

For some Saudi Arabs, the act of passing around information (regardless of its trustworthiness and credibility) is a cultural virtue, making the distribution of rumoursterm·key a socially well-received act. Many Saudi Arabs forward any information (regardless of accuracy) that displays warnings against something, because of their over-concern and over-care about family and friends. Spreading rumoursterm·key is seen as a means of enhancing communication among family members and friends, given that such communication has social and cultural value (and is even an obligation). Many Saudi Arabs may not realise the implications and ramifications of rumoursterm·key, and therefore they exchange and spread them merely for fun and to kill time, socialise and overcome boredom. Many Saudi Arabs like rumoursterm·key because they see them as ‘fast food’—i.e., like easy-to-reach and consume ‘ready meals’. Because information is censored and, therefore, limited, it is less available and this, therefore, encourages rumoursterm·key. Moreover, the relationship between Saudi individuals and managers is not harmonious, and hence some individuals use rumoursterm·key as an indirect tool to criticise or challenge managers. In Saudi Arabian organisations, transparency tends to be limited, encouraging members to come up with rumoursterm·key through which they place pressure on managers to reveal truths and be more transparent. Studying rumoursterm·key in a collective society is different, and the concern is that some readers of this article (who come from an individualistic culture) may default to individualist modes of thinking.

This article seeks to study how male Saudi Arabian undergraduates perceive a broader trans-national Arabic-writing audience on the Internet. In 2017, five groups of 20 Saudi Arabian undergraduates (a total of 100 students) in a Saudi university participated in the study. Every effort was made to follow the best ethical practices, such as seeking to ensure that there was no harm to participants, no lack of informed consent, no invasion of privacy and no deception involved. Informed consent was obtained from candidates after explicitly describing the relevant features of the research and officially asking for their consent. The participants were then asked to collect rumoursterm·key as part of their critical engagement with educational technologyterm·key. They were social-science students, aged between 18 and 22 years, Saudi Arabian, male, single (except for two), undergraduates, unemployed (except seven part-time employees) and came from lower-middle-class or working-class families. There is, therefore, a bias towards the interests of Saudi Arabian undergraduates.

Saudi Arabian undergraduates were chosen for various culturally specific reasons. The researchers saw them as appropriate for the research because they were more exposed to the outside world than high-school teenagers and yet were less serious and conservative than graduates (Abdullah, 1986 ; Al Doori, 2008 ). For example, undergraduates tend to talk more freely about gossipterm·dom, compared with Saudi graduates, who perceive their academic degree as making them ‘culturally responsible’ and ‘social examples’. To illustrate, asking Saudi Arabian graduates for input into any culturally ‘unethical’ topic (here, ‘gossipterm·dom’) would be regarded by many such graduates as ‘insulting’ and ‘offensive’, because they would think that the researchers were assuming they were tolerant of unethical behaviour. Asking for one’s input into any culturally unethical topic would be seen by graduates as pointless, given that this topic is unnegotiable and culturally ‘settled’ to be negative. Saudi Arabian undergraduates, on the other hand, would be less likely to take offence.

The students were all ‘Arabs’—i.e., ‘members of a Semitic people, originally from the Arabian Peninsula and neighbouring territories’ ( Oxford Dictionaries ). Arabic was the language of instruction, and the students were all native speakers of Arabic. They were asked to use mobile phones during class-time (1 h and 40 min) to collect rumoursterm·key via social networks (as part of their online curricular activities). Once one student found a rumourterm·dom, he said it aloud immediately so as to reserve this rumourterm·dom for himself. That is, one could not reserve any rumourterm·dom that one’s peers had already reserved. A point was won for every rumourterm·dom reserved. It was easy for Group 1 to collect rumoursterm·key (and therefore collect points). Group 2, however, found it difficult to find rumoursterm·key that had not already been found and therefore reserved by their peers in Group 1. Group 3 faced even more difficulty since their peers in Groups 1 and 2 had reserved so many rumoursterm·key. Groups 4 and 5 reported finding it ‘impossible’ (in their words) to find rumoursterm·key that were not already found and reserved by their peers in Groups 1, 2 and 3. Members of Groups 4 and 5 were unable to reserve more than two rumoursterm·key, and therefore were unable to win more than two points. This is when the authors decided to stop the process of having more groups and collecting more rumoursterm·key, since this perceived ‘impossibility’ of collecting more rumoursterm·key was seen as an indication of having managed to collect common rumoursterm·key during that particular period.

The students managed to collect 579 rumoursterm·key in total. A key limitation in this research is, of course, that some of the participating students perceived some pieces of information to be accurate and therefore did not collect them, even though these pieces were actually rumoursterm·key. The task of checking the accuracy of rumoursterm·key is beyond the scope of this research. This research collects merely what appear to be rumoursterm·key and what are socially perceived as rumoursterm·key; that is, what the students thought of as rumoursterm·key. So, the current study is associated with what is thought to be a rumourterm·dom and is not intended to examine the accuracy of rumoursterm·key. The authors designed the study and created the methodology in line with the work of Goodwin and Goodwin ( 2016 ), who discuss in-class research. It should be clarified that the research was not on students but rather through students—i.e., employing students as ‘data-collectors’ (Al LilypersName, 2013 ). While it is debatable whether it is ethical to employ one’s students for one’s research (Denzin and Lincoln, 1994 ), this exercise was seen as part of the students’ practical learning about research. There are various advantages of in-class data collection by student teams, including familiarity with the wider culture, a budget-friendly workforce and accessibility to diverse resources.

This is a sociology paper, showing how male Saudi undergraduates define rumoursterm·key. As a methodology paper, it describes an approach to measuring the public temperterm·dom among a group during a 100-min class. The collected rumoursterm·key were analysed to see what they could tell us about Saudi Arabian culture. These rumoursterm·key were first coded. The code for each rumourterm·dom is basically the keyword of the rumourterm·dom. Similar codes were then grouped to generate sub-categories. After that, similar sub-categories were assembled to generate categories. These categories, collectively, detailed the subject of the research (i.e., a theoretical proposition). Table 1 displays the whole analytical process. This table shows merely a sample or ‘taste’ of the rumoursterm·key, codes and sub-categories. It shows only a selection of codes, given that there are a large number of codes. Some codes can belong to more than one sub-category. To enhance the quality of this table, three Arab and three non-Arab academics were asked to critically challenge and problematise them. The main advantage of having Arab academics review this table is that they are familiar with the social context under investigation. A key benefit of having non-Arab academics involved as well is that they are ‘outsiders’ in relation to the researched context, and hence they are more likely not to take things for granted or be biased.

Analysis of the raw data Findings and interpretations This research investigates whether the public temperterm·dom of a community can be recognised through its rumoursterm·key. Having analysed hundreds of Arabic online rumoursterm·key, four categories emerged: the concerns , interests , attitudes and values of an Arab society. These categories are displayed in Table 1 . According to the literature, these four categories, taken together, form the emotional nature (i.e., the public temperterm·dom) of any society (Bloom, 1956 ; Krathwohl, 1964 ). Therefore, a theoretical proposition could be that rumoursterm·key mirror the emotional sphere of any culture, reflecting a range of emotions from simple to complex (from concerns, interests and attitudes to values). Other researchers from non-psychological backgrounds might find non-emotional categories. For this reason, further research should be conducted by non-psychologists to re-analyse the raw data and the subject matter.

This first category shows that rumoursterm·key do not arise in a vacuum, but rather they are a reflection of various concerns in a community (see Table 1 ). For example, the rumourterm·dom that ‘ Eating Lay’s potato chips causes cancer’ can be seen as a sign of a medical concern with cancer in the community. In other words, people may have resorted to rumoursterm·key because of the human inability to cure certain diseases (e.g., cancer). Likewise, the rumourterm·dom that ‘ A higher level of forgetting is a sign of having a higher level of intelligence ’ indicates a psychological preoccupation with forgetting. Put in general words, rumoursterm·key have been exploited here to ‘cheer up’ individuals’ psychological concerns over human limitations (here, forgetting). In this case, rumoursterm·key have been used to improve self-confidence and/or make people feel good about themselves—that is, feel good about their congenital flaw of forgetting. In this case, rumoursterm·key have been used to fill gaps in ‘human perfection’ and to overcome various aspects of human shortage (here, forgetting). There seem also to be environmental concerns, which can be seen in such rumoursterm·key as ‘ NASA has announced that there will soon be storms that prevent sunlight from reaching Earth for six days’ . In addition, the social concern over gender can be noticed in various rumoursterm·key, such as the rumourterm·dom that ‘ If the stomach of a pregnant woman is up, the baby is a boy. If it is down, the baby is a girl’ . In this rumourterm·dom, the use of ‘up’ to refer to males could be seen as a reflection of male superiority in an Arab society, whereas the use of ‘down’ to refer to females mirrors socially perceived female inferiority. Hence, men may help spread this rumourterm·dom so as to promote their superiority.

This second category demonstrates that scholarly rumoursterm·dom do not come from nowhere but can hold intentions and interests (see Table 1 ). For example, a political interest that sought to discredit a certain product can be seen in the rumourterm·dom that ‘ Indomie [an instant noodle brand] results in brain damage ’. When one has a strong interest in a particular concept (such as the health risk of sugar and chocolate), one may feel excited about spreading any information (regardless of its accuracy) about this concept, such as the information that ‘ Mars bars contain pieces of plastic’ . At times, a rumourterm·dom shows a positive social interest in something (e.g., music), whereas another rumourterm·dom exhibits a negative social interest in this same thing. For instance, a rumoured positive interest in music is ‘ Cows produce more milk when listening to music’ , and a rumoured negative interest in music is ‘ If one listens to music, a skewer made of fire will be stuck into one’s ear’ . Sometimes, a rumourterm·dom promotes a natural interest in something (e.g., dates), and yet another rumourterm·dom enhances a natural dis interest in the same thing. For example, a promoted natural interest in dates can be seen in the rumourterm·dom that ‘ Dates help to remove air gases from the womb ’, and a promoted natural disinterest can be noticed in the rumourterm·dom ‘ Coronavirus is transmitted via dates .’ Rumoursterm·key can have positive intentions behind them, promoting ethics and morality. For example, the following rumourterm·dom seeks to promote honesty in society: ‘ A Canadian study shows that, every time one lies one loses five hairs from one’s head’ . Here, although the intention or end is ‘good’, the means is ‘bad’.

Rumoursterm·key are found in this third category to be influenced by certain attitudes , be they cultural, political or social (see Table 1 ). The rumourterm·dom that ‘ If one stares into the dark, one will be killed by a ghost who thinks you have seen him ’ shows a negative social attitude towards darkness (and indeed ghosts). People seem to have a certain political attitude towards America and its socially perceived political attempt at domination over the world, which can be seen in the following rumourterm·dom: ‘ WhatsApp conversations and Skype calls are subject to surveillance by America .’ There appear to be positive social attitudes towards tall individuals, with there being a rumourterm·dom that ‘ Eating bananas or drinking milk helps one become taller .’ The feature of having straight hair is socially regarded as a sign of beauty, and for this reason, there is a rumourterm·dom that ‘ Child’s hair becomes straight when the hair is entirely shaved and then grows again .’ The far-reaching influence and power of rumoursterm·key could make companies scared, as any misconduct might cause rumoursterm·key against their products (Dunbar, 1994 ; Schoeman, 1994 ). To illustrate, the following rumourterm·dom arose from the desire to discipline a company: ‘ It is written on Coca Cola cans, “No God, No Mohammed”’ . On the other hand, companies could themselves utilise rumoursterm·key to market and promote their own products, for example, initiating rumoursterm·key such as ‘ Chewing gum helps enhance academic performance ’, thereby promoting the chewing-gum industry. Rumoursterm·key could also be exploited to cultivate a sense of conspiracy and to manipulate certain international relations. The following rumourterm·dom illustrates the point: ‘ Some kinds of juices coming from Iran cause bladder cancer’ .

This fourth category demonstrates that rumoursterm·key can be cultivated via a variety of psychological, social and cultural values (see Table 1 ). One such psychological value is, for example, being mature. The importance of maturity can be seen in the following rumourterm·dom: ‘ Having white hair at an early age is a sign of reaching maturity at an early age ’. Moreover, psychologically oriented rumoursterm·key have been used to make people feel good about themselves—for example, to feel good about their ‘flaw’ of having white hair. A social value is given to colours through such rumoursterm·key as ‘ Black cats are inhabited by ghosts ’. A curse appears to represent a cultural value, around which a number of rumoursterm·key have circulated: ‘ If one makes fun of the Quran, one may be turned into a sheep ’. Another cultural value is the belief in spirits, which can be seen in the following rumourterm·dom: ‘ If one cannot get up whatsoever after waking up, this means that a ghost is holding you tightly down ’. In other cultures, many of these sorts of outlandish sayings would be called ‘old wives’ tales’ rather than rumoursterm·key.

Discussion Rumourterm·dom is not ‘a deviant act’ (Miller, 2006 , p. 505). It is rather the natural human response to uncertain situations and the normal social act of addressing uncertainty (Fine and Heath, 2009 ). It is a cross-cultural phenomenon, being an inevitable ‘congenital’ disorder integral to the constitution of society (Gluckman, 1963 ). There cannot be society without rumourterm·dom (Shibutani, 1966 ). Although rumourterm·dom is essentially a structural ‘error’ in humanity, the current study has examined the possibility of getting truth out of error (Nietzsche 1996 ). That is, it has examined whether it is possible to systematically analyse rumoursterm·key (i.e., errors ) to see if they can inform us of truths about the society wherein they exist. It has empirically shown that, with rumoursterm·key, one can get some idea about the concerns, interests, attitudes and values of a society. Although one may think that a society should be the one to be looked into to understand its rumoursterm·key, the current study reverses this logic by looking into rumoursterm·key in a society to understand the society. That is, this study has checked if rumoursterm·key in a particular society can inform us of this society. So, the investigation has not taken this normal path: society → rumoursterm·key. Instead, the path for the investigation has been: rumoursterm·key → society. Traditionally, the conditions that can ease the initiation and diffusion of rumoursterm·key, innovations and the like are subject to examination, and yet this examination does not look into the rumoursterm·key and innovations after they have been initiated and diffused. In other words, the rumoursterm·key and innovations should be scrutinised after they have been introduced and spread, just as much as they are scrutinised before they are introduced and spread. Hence, the current study looks into rumoursterm·key after they have been introduced and spread.

The current research has found that ‘rumourterm·dom initiators’ use—whether intentionally or spontaneously (see Knopf, 1975 )—certain ‘psychological strategies’ (Prasad, 1935 ) for convincing others of their own rumoursterm·key and for enhancing the social acceptance of their claims (cf. McCandless, 2014 ). Table 2 displays some of these strategies.

Strategies for enhancing the social acceptance of rumoursterm·key The employment of such strategies suggests that rumourterm·dom initiators are not, at least politically and socially, naïve and that they do act strategically.

The research has discovered that many rumoursterm·key are obviously ‘stupid’ (i.e., making limited or no sense, or, at least, being ill-informed, Al LilypersName et al. 2017 ), and yet despite this obvious stupidity, individuals still exchange them with each other. This could be seen as a sign of limited critical thinking, education and, moreover, ‘intelligence’. An example of these ‘nonsense’ rumoursterm·key is ‘ Counting stars causes pimples or tumours ’ – anyone (even those with very little critical-thinking ability) should easily figure out the lack of any correlation whatsoever between ‘counting stars’ and ‘pimples or tumours’. Another example is that ‘ If one lies, one’s nose gets longer ’—again, anyone with even the most limited critical-thinking skills should effortlessly realise how false this rumourterm·dom is, considering that individuals have lied before and yet their noses have actually remained the same. This rumourterm·dom appears to come from the children’s tale ‘Pinocchio’, so it was never meant to be taken literally. Another rumourterm·dom is that ‘ If a child’s tooth falls out, and then he puts it under his bed, it becomes money when he wakes up ’—did anyone not do an experiment on this matter to realise how false this is? Another naïve rumourterm·dom is that ‘ If one sneezes with one’s eyes open, one’s eyes will come out of one’s face ’—how many times have humans sneezed, while their eyes have remained in place? One may wonder if people actually believe in these rumoursterm·key, or whether they simply exchange these rumoursterm·key out of boredom and for entertainment. In their defence, most people automatically close their eyes when they sneeze, making it a hard one to verify.

Interestingly, no ‘political rumoursterm·key’ were found, perhaps because such rumoursterm·key are socially seen as ‘off limits’. There were no instructions given to the students to avoid such rumoursterm·key. Although undergraduates know about politics, they choose not to raise political issues in the context of academia and in Internet searches for rumoursterm·key.

It has been found that it is common for ‘rumourterm·dom forwarders’—or, as Buckner ( 1965 ) calls them, ‘rumourterm·dom transmitters’—to sign their rumoursterm·key with such phrases as ‘ as received’ or ‘ the one to be blamed in case of any possible inaccuracy regarding this piece of information is the one who has sent me this piece ’. These phrases appear to be derived from a widely held belief in Saudi Arabian society that ‘the carrier of bad things is not bad’, implying that the forwarders of ‘bad’ rumoursterm·key are not ‘bad’. Such phrases have created fertile ground for rumoursterm·key to be easily and smoothly transmitted throughout society. Such phrases are used so that rumourterm·dom forwarders can hide behind disclaimers and protect themselves if receivers question or blame them. Rumourterm·dom forwarders may think that the use of such phrases grants them the freedom to forward ‘anything’ without even reading what they forward. In fact, such phrases actually show rumourterm·dom forwarders admitting that they have not critically reflected on the received rumoursterm·key. Besides, thinking themselves protected by such phrases, one person can forward two rumoursterm·key that show conflicting interests in the same thing. An appropriate example is when one person forwards both: 1) a rumourterm·dom promoting honesty (‘If one lies, his nose gets longer’) ; and 2) a rumourterm·dom promoting dishonesty (‘ Lying a lot makes one live longer’) . Such phrases have been used by some rumourterm·dom forwarders to show a sense of ethics, referencing and honesty in communicating and passing around information. They may also suggest that some rumourterm·dom forwarders do not necessarily believe in (and/or, more importantly, understand) the content of the forwarded messages.

Conclusion This article has studied the perceptions of male undergraduates’ views of a broader trans-national Arabic-writing audience on the Internet. It has underpinned the theoretical proposition that ‘rumoursterm·key often serve as a window into a community’ (Kelley, 2004 : v). It has suggested that scholarly rumoursterm·dom (perhaps like non-scholarly rumoursterm·key) mirror the public temperterm·dom of a society, reflecting a range of emotions from simple to complex, from concerns, interests and attitudes to values. Simpler emotions (e.g., concerns) are, perhaps, more easily affected by scholarly rumoursterm·dom than more complex emotions (e.g., values). A society produces scholarly rumoursterm·dom based on its public temperterm·dom, and therefore these rumoursterm·key are a deep, complex reflection of its public temperterm·dom. Hence, scholarly rumoursterm·dom can be effective ‘informants’ of public tempersterm·dom. Scholarly rumoursterm·dom capture the specific and changing emotional aspects of the milieu in which these rumoursterm·key exist. They reveal why habitants are committed to these rumoursterm·key. They form ‘books’ in which the emotional formula of a society is written and may be transmitted to succeeding generations. They can exist as ‘inscribed spaces’ on which a certain society ‘writes’ their temper. They can serve the function of public tempersterm·dom, even though individuals can be drawn to rumoursterm·key without being conscious of any desire to promote a public temperterm·dom (Baumeister et al. 2004 ). Although scholarly rumoursterm·dom are ‘dark spots’, this study has examined whether it is feasible to get ‘light’ out of this ‘darkness’, i.e., to use scholarly rumoursterm·dom (i.e., dark spots) as indirect informants of truth (i.e., light) about society, meaning that something can come out of its opposite. It is not only that components of a given culture can be predicted from its rumoursterm·key, but also that rumourterm·dom (just like language) is a function of a given culture. Lakoff ( 2010 ) contends that linguistic metaphors—just like rumoursterm·key—play an essential role in the functionality of human socio-political life and in the direction of societies (particularly their politics).

This article helps give a sense of what is perceived as rumourterm·dom in a social context within the Arabic-speaking world. It provides the English-speaking world with an example of how an Arab society ‘thinks’. This is, however, not meant to reduce the various Arab cultures into one single culture, to create essentialism, to (re)produce a form of orientalism or to promote a neo-colonial style overgeneralisation about the ‘Arab mentality’. The findings of the study are, at their best, applicable merely to the Saudi Arabian mentalities, which may, or may not, share the same characteristics as other Arabian mentalities. It should be remarked that this article is not about ‘Arabic rumoursterm·key’ since rumoursterm·key cannot be ethnicity-specific (e.g., white people’s rumoursterm·key). It, instead, is concerned with how a certain group (here, Saudi Arabs) deals with rumoursterm·key in their own language (here, Arabic) or translates rumoursterm·key into their language. The current study is purely qualitative, and therefore this area would benefit from further research that quantifies its findings. The current research has shown that the public temperterm·dom of a society can be understood through the nature of its rumoursterm·key, and therefore further research should be carried out to reverse this investigation, by examining whether one, through understanding the public temperterm·dom of a society, can design a model that predicts the kinds of rumoursterm·key that exist and will exist in this society. That is, further research should examine whether the nature of rumoursterm·key can be gleaned through understanding the public temperterm·dom of a society. Further research should look into how certain societies, organisations or individuals may influence and manipulate the public temperterm·dom of other societies, organisations or individuals by means of the political power of rumoursterm·key.

Further research should also consider a political process wherein rumoursterm·key are influenced by themselves. This influence may take two forms. First, the enactment of one rumourterm·dom may hold back or conflict with existing rumoursterm·key, or disrupt the possibility of enacting other rumoursterm·key. Second, rumoursterm·key may feed on themselves, making more rumoursterm·key possible. To illustrate, a rumourterm·dom may require another new rumourterm·dom in order for the two to function effectively. New rumoursterm·key may obligate their forwarders to link them to other rumoursterm·key, establishing an almost independent network of political power. Rumoursterm·key may appear to have a power and a life of their own, with the role of humans becoming less relevant. While rumoursterm·key do not make themselves, and are constituted by humans, humans may not afterwards have full freedom to determine how rumoursterm·key grow and shape the public temperterm·dom. In this case, rumoursterm·key get out of hand and shape themselves, away from the agency of humans. Indeed, it is not only researchers but humans in general that hardly recognise the implicit ability of non-humans (here, rumoursterm·key) to play politics and act as politicising media.

Further studies should be carried out to re-apply the current research to Saudi women’s universities, older people and younger people (e.g., high-school students). Transnational comparisons within the Arab world would be also worthy of academic investigation using the methodology of the current research. Likewise, comparative studies in how rumoursterm·key are evaluated in the public tempersterm·dom of other Muslim areas can be of considerable value, especially considering that the Koran warns against rumour-forwarders: ‘O you who believe! If a Fāsiq (liar–evil person) comes to you with any news, verify it, lest you should harm people in ignorance, and afterwards you become regretful for what you have done’ (The Dwellings Chapter, Verse 6).
