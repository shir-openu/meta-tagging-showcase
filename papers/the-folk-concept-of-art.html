Although further factors, such as emotional expressivity or formal complexity, could be explored in follow-up studies, our findings constitute tentative evidence that the folk concept of art is not an essentialist concept and that it is a cluster concept instead. contribution — evidence — “What is the folk concept of art? Does it track any of the major definitions of art philosophers have proposed? In two preregistered experiments (N = 888) focusing on two types of artworks (paintings and musical works), we manipulate three potential features of artworks: intentional creation, the possession of aesthetic value, and institutional recognition. This allows us to investigate whether the folk concept of art fits an essentialist definition drawing on one or more of the manipulated factors or whether it might be a disjunctive or cluster concept. The results suggest that none of the three manipulated properties alone suffices for an object to be considered art. The folk concept of art might thus well be a cluster concept instead of an essentialist concept.”
mechanismfunction-and-intention categorisation Artifacts are routinely categorised in terms of their inferred function and the intentions ascribed to their creators, a cognitive default that plausibly underlies how the folk classify art. — “Empirical research has shown that artifacts are often categorized in terms of functions and intentions (Boyer & Barrett, 2004). Function-based explanations of artifacts, and even natural objects, are held to be a cognitive default (Kelemen, 1999; Kelemen et al., 2013) inherent in the reasoning of children (DiYanni & Kelemen, 2005) and adults (Kelemen & Rosset, 2009), also in non-Western cultures (Schachner et al., 2017).”
mechanismaesthetic-function ascription On Pignocchi's account, the concept of art is applied when a judge takes an artifact to be intended to fulfil a function - typically inducing an aesthetic experience - that other artworks fulfil. — “Alessandro Pignocchi hypothesized that the concept of art seems intuitively appropriate in cases where people consider an artifact as intended to fulfil a function that other artworks typically fulfil (2014: p. 439). Two factors, he claims, are key: the functions that the judging person (e.g. the study participant) accepts as being normally fulfilled by art, and the intentions that they ascribe to the creator of that artifact. Although Pignocchi mentions several possible functions of art, we will limit ourselves to what Davies claims to be the most typical– that of inducing aesthetic experiences.”
mechanismostensive act Institutional recognition works, on a relevance-theory reading, as an ostensive act: presenting an object as art signals authorial intentions and marks it as worth interpreting. — “McCallum et al. (2019) offer an explanation of how the institutional theory of art might work from a cognitive point of view. They claim that the institutional theory matches the main claims of relevance theory (Sperber & Wilson, 1986). Presentation of an object as a work of art (such as putting it into a gallery) is an ostensive act. It suggests for the viewer two kinds of authorial intentions: not only informative (to communicate some information to the audience), but also communicative (an intention to point out the informative intention to the audience). According to McCallum and colleagues, institutional recognition and presentation “as a candidate for appreciation” is a kind of ostensive act which not only intentionally draws attention to an object, but also gives a reason to think that interpreting the object is worthy and will provide worthwhile cognitive effects (2019: 25). This theory, then, points to both institutional and intentional factors in art status ascriptions.”
mechanismauthorial intent Inferences about a creator's mental states help fix the kind an artifact belongs to, so judgements of intended membership in the art category drive art classification. — “Inferences about the mental states of creators are as important in categorizing works of art as they are for other artifacts. According to Bloom (1996), authorial intent is relevant to determining the kind of an artifact even if it is not directly connected to the object’s appearance or function. Thus, intuitions on whether an object was created with the intention for it to belong to the category of artworks must play an important part in judgments on whether an object should be categorized as art.”
mechanismcuratorial intention Where art is made without a creative intention, intentionality re-enters at the stage of conferring art status: creative intention is replaced by curatorial intention in selecting what is presented as art. — “Although our results suggest that creative intention at the moment of an action that results in a work of art is not necessary, it is hard to imagine intentionality being entirely absent from art. In similar cases, intentionality often becomes relevant during the artwork selection or curation process. One familiar example is the creative process of AI-generated art, where the human user of the system is often presented with multiple outputs and selects the best one. Another example is animal art or human creations made by accident, both of which can be seen as instances of art made without artistic intention. However, the pieces ultimately presented to the audience are not chosen randomly but are often intentionally selected by humans. In the context of art created without a creative intention, intentionality is unlikely to disappear completely, at least not from the process of conferring art status: artistic intention here is replaced by curatorial intention. We leave these interesting, more sophisticated aspects of intentionality to be explored in future research.”

qualifies the philosophical assumption that art must be the product of intentional action and must have an artist — “We have also gathered data on the question under what conditions a creator is deemed an artist. Broadly speaking, whether or not an agent is considered an artist does – expectedly – track whether or not the object they created is deemed art. However, and this is an interesting and surprising finding, in the visual context, we found a divergence: Beautiful, accidentally produced creations (recognized or not) are considered art, though their creators are not considered artists. What this suggests, at least prima facie, is that people are willing to attribute the status of art to objects that were created without a creative intention and not created by artists. senseconcept of art - closed sense (determinate necessary and sufficient conditions) versus open sense (amendable, corrigible conditions of use) — “The definition of art has been one of the central topics in philosophical aesthetics in the last century. However, finding a satisfactory definition of art turned out to be very difficult: traditional definitions of art often include objects that are not artworks or exclude objects that are, in fact, artworks. Thus, a second question has gained more and more attention in the last few decades: Is it possible to define art at all? If so, what kind of definition would it be? Are there any individually necessary and jointly sufficient conditions for an object to be art? Is it rather a disjunction of minimally sufficient conditions that makes something art? Are these conditions (or set of conditions) determinate or indeterminate, i.e., is the concept of art closed, or is it open?”
sensedefinition of art - descriptive sense (how art is actually thought of) versus normative sense (how it should be thought of) — “In contrast to descriptive accounts, there are views that provide a normative, or partially normative, definition of art (e.g. Gaut, 2000: p. 39). Definitions of this sort aim to describe not how art is actually thought of, but how it should be thought of, or what the concept of art really consists of (whether or not the folk agree). In this case, empirical findings may also be important for philosophers seeking to create normative definitions. Empirical findings can provide philosophers with a background against which the normative theories might be created and refined; they uncover problems and inconsistencies found within actual usage (Monseré, 2016: p. 15). Any normative project must start with a descriptive step, as it is impossible to revise a concept without knowing how it actually functions (Meskin et al., 2018). A philosopher offering a normative definition must explain, in a first step, what people’s intuitions are, and only then can they offer an explanation of why said intuitions are mistaken (see also Pignocchi, 2014).”
senseart - essentialist sense (a kind with an essence) versus family-resemblance / cluster sense (overlapping properties, no common essence) — “All of the discussed definitions can be interpreted as essentialist: they invoke necessary and sufficient conditions for an object to be considered an artwork, or at least emphasize one factor as the most important, while others are treated as more peripheral. This view is challenged by anti-essentialists, who object to essentialist definitions with counterexamples that are intended to demonstrate that the respective views are over- or underinclusive. The concept of game thus has no rigid boundaries.”
senseconcept of art - cluster sense (no definitive set of application criteria) versus disjunctive sense (a fixed disjunction of sufficient conditions) — “Our findings suggest that the folk concept of art might be a cluster or a disjunctive concept, but it is not evident which of the two accounts is correct from an empirical point of view. In the literature, cluster and disjunctive definitions are usually quite similar (Davies, 2004a, 2004b; Longworth & Scarantino, 2010). What distinguishes them is that a cluster concept, in contrast to a disjunctive one, is not governed by a definitive set of criteria for application. The fact that we have only tested three criteria is an evident limitation of our study. What is more, if the criteria of application can change in the future, as hypothesized by some, empirical studies of the sort conducted here will always be partially incomplete (Cook, 2013; Weitz, 1956).”

definitionfamily resemblance — “All of the discussed definitions can be interpreted as essentialist: they invoke necessary and sufficient conditions for an object to be considered an artwork, or at least emphasize one factor as the most important, while others are treated as more peripheral. This view is challenged by anti-essentialists, who object to essentialist definitions with counterexamples that are intended to demonstrate that the respective views are over- or underinclusive. We can distinguish between cluster and disjunctive anti-essentialist theories, both of which draw inspiration from Wittgenstein’s idea of ‘family resemblance’ (Wittgenstein PI, 2009: §67, Weitz, 1956; Ziff, 1953; Dean, 2003). There are board games, card games, Olympic games, ball games etc., but, Wittgenstein argues, there is no single property (or set of properties) they all have in common. The concept of game thus has no rigid boundaries.”

Most objects were “hard cases” discussed by philosophers of artterm. Kamber found many discrepancies between the intuitions of artterm professionals and philosophers’ claims about hard cases: for instance, almost half of the artterm professionals in the study categorised a Bugatti car as artterm. He concluded that none of the existing philosophical artterm theories succeed in tracking lay intuitions.

In another study, Kamber and Enoch (2018) presented their participants with the same set of objects and asked them to categorize the objects as artterm or not artterm. In addition, they asked the participants to consider a set of fourteen possible reasons for their judgments. The latter included that the object was made by a conscious agent, intended by its makers to be an object of aesthetic interest or appreciation, intended by its makers to evoke or communicate intention, or that the object invites interpretation, provides an imaginative experience, or is beautiful (Kamber & Enoch, 2018: p. 21). Although intentional justifications were the most popular, the results suggest that none of the tested artterm definitions are fully in accordance with intuitions of artterm professionals. Kamber and Enoch note that Beardsley’s aesthetic definition of artterm is more successful than the others. Based on Kamber’s findings, Winner (2018) has argued that the concept of artterm is open, as proposed in a Wittgensteinian manner by Weitz (1956): there are no necessary properties, just family resemblancesterm, and the conditions for the application of the term might even change over time.

In both studies conducted by Kamber, the majority of the participants were artterm professionals (2018) or half artterm professionals, half “artterm buffs” (both groups consisted mainly of university faculty members) (2011). As such, these studies do not speak to the folk concept of artterm. Moreover, the studies have been described as employing an “informal approach” of testing a large number of definitions using only one or two instances of artworksterm (Winner, 2019: p. 21). Thus, although these results are interesting and important, the actual folk concept of artterm remains largely unexplored.Footnote 5 5 Empirical work relating to the three core factors Let us take a deeper look at three factors– intentional creationterm, aesthetic valueterm, and institutional recognitionterm– and at the cognitive mechanisms that could lie behind the folk concept of artterm. Each of these three factors enjoys some support from the psychological literature and from psychological studies that were conducted with a view to exploring only one or two aspects of the folk concept of artterm. We will survey the literature relevant to each of these factors: aesthetic valueterm (5.1.), institutional recognitionterm (5.2.), and intentional creationterm (5.3.).

5.1 Aesthetic valueterm Alessandro Pignocchi hypothesized that the concept of artterm seems intuitively appropriate in cases where people consider an artifact as intended to fulfil a function that other artworksterm typically fulfil (2014: p. 439). Two factors, he claims, are key: the functions that the judging person (e.g. the study participant) accepts as being normally fulfilled by artterm, and the intentions that they ascribe to the creator of that artifact. Although Pignocchi mentions several possible functions of artterm, we will limit ourselves to what Davies claims to be the most typical– that of inducing aesthetic experiencesterm.

Empirical research has shown that artifacts are often categorized in terms of functions and intentions (Boyer & Barrett, 2004). Function-based explanations of artifacts, and even natural objects, are held to be a cognitive default (Kelemen, 1999; Kelemen et al., 2013) inherent in the reasoning of children (DiYanni & Kelemen, 2005) and adults (Kelemen & Rosset, 2009), also in non-Western cultures (Schachner et al., 2017).

An additional motivation to test the aesthetic definition derives from Kamber and Enoch’s (2018) results, which suggest that Beardsley’s aesthetic definition of artterm is more successful than the others. If the folk concept of artterm is not radically different from that used by artterm professionals, we might expect their intuitions to favour the aesthetic definition. Moreover, research by Pelowski et al. (2017) shows a strong correlation between the classification of objects as artterm and ratings of liking, which suggests that appraisal and classification are interrelated. These results also offer some prima facie support for the aesthetic definition.

5.2 Institutional recognitionterm McCallum et al. (2019) offer an explanation of how the institutional theory of artterm might work from a cognitive point of view. They claim that the institutional theory matches the main claims of relevance theory (Sperber & Wilson, 1986). Presentation of an object as a work of artterm (such as putting it into a gallery) is an ostensive act. It suggests for the viewer two kinds of authorial intentions: not only informative (to communicate some information to the audience), but also communicative (an intention to point out the informative intention to the audience). According to McCallum and colleagues, institutional recognitionterm and presentation “as a candidate for appreciation” is a kind of ostensive act which not only intentionally draws attention to an object, but also gives a reason to think that interpreting the object is worthy and will provide worthwhile cognitive effects (2019: 25). This theory, then, points to both institutional and intentional factors in artterm status ascriptions.

Kamber’s studies suggest that both institutional recognitionterm and aesthetic valueterm might be important in judgments on what is artterm. However, in the 2011 study, more than half of the people were willing to categorize an aesthetically bad painting as artterm, while an even bigger number of participants categorized works as artterm that were institutionally recognized but unlikely to provoke aesthetic experiencesterm. These results suggest that neither the aesthetic nor institutional theory fully tracks folk intuitions on what is artterm on their own.

5.3 Intentional creationterm Inferences about the mental states of creators are as important in categorizing works of artterm as they are for other artifacts. According to Bloom (1996), authorial intent is relevant to determining the kind of an artifact even if it is not directly connected to the object’s appearance or function. Thus, intuitions on whether an object was created with the intention for it to belong to the category of artworksterm must play an important part in judgments on whether an object should be categorized as artterm.

Several empirical studies have suggested that intention is essential for the way we think about artterm. Intentions have been found to be crucial for what people categorize as artterm (Jucker et al., 2014; Newman & Bloom, 2012)– even more important than the appearance of the object (Newman & Smith, 2018). Moreover, in Kamber’s studies, when asked to provide reasons for categorizing an object as artterm, participants mentioned “being made by a conscious agent”, “being intended by its makers to be an object of aesthetic interest”, and “inviting interpretation” more often than other kinds of justifications for their judgments (Kamber, 2018: p. 81). If ordinary folk deploy the same concept of artterm as artterm professionals, intentions must be an important factor. However, in another study on folk intuitions as to whether AI can create artterm, MikalonytėpersName and KneerpersName (2022) found that intentional creationterm is not seen by non-experts as a necessary condition for an object to be considered artterm. These results reveal that the exact role of intentional creationterm in judgments of what counts as artterm is still not fully understood.

Furthermore, we wanted to explore the mental-state requirements as well as their possible interaction with beautyterm and institutional recognitionterm and thus asked participants to what extent the creator desired, believed, and intended to make a work of artterm.

In contrast to Kamber’s studies, our participants are not artterm professionals or amateur-experts, but, rather, ordinary people. 6.4 Discussion Our experiment produced several findings. First, among the three manipulated factors, intentionality and beautyterm manifested a significant and pronounced impact on art judgmentsterm·var, whereas the effect of institutional recognitionterm was significant yet small. Our finding that beautiful accidental creations are artterm, even if they have no artistterm, is very surprising, bearing in mind that there is no artistterm to claim credit for aesthetically valuable artworksterm created in this way.

Our findings suggest that the folk concept of artterm might be a cluster or a disjunctive conceptterm, but it is not evident which of the two accounts is correct from an empirical point of view. In the literature, cluster and disjunctive definitions are usually quite similar (Davies, 2004a, 2004b; Longworth & Scarantino, 2010). What distinguishes them is that a cluster conceptterm, in contrast to a disjunctive one, is not governed by a definitive set of criteria for application. The fact that we have only tested three criteria is an evident limitation of our study. What is more, if the criteria of application can change in the future, as hypothesized by some, empirical studies of the sort conducted here will always be partially incomplete (Cook, 2013; Weitz, 1956).

That said, we can draw some very tentative lessons from the data as regards the two non-essentialist accountsterm under consideration. Consider a contextualist interpretation of cluster conceptsterm, akin to cluster accounts of the concept of knowledge in (see e.g. DeRose, 1992; Cohen, 1999; Stanley, 2004). If the difference between cluster and disjunctive conceptsterm is essentially a question of whether their conditions of application are context-sensitive, then our results show some support for the cluster theory. The application conditions for the concept of artterm change across domains (painting v. music). In our study concerning music, institutional recognitionterm was a sufficient criterion for an object’s being considered artterm, whereas in the study concerning visual artterm, it was not.

As regards the status of being an artistterm: In the context of visual artterm, only intentionality mattered; in the context of music, all three manipulated factors proved significant. Intentionality or beautyterm of the object was sufficient by itself for people to deem the creator an artistterm, and only in the case where the object lacked all three tested properties did people clearly refrain from judging the creator as an artistterm. While it is not surprising that being an artistterm and creating intentionally is tightly related, we found that, in some cases, intentional action is not a necessary condition for the folk to consider someone an artistterm.

8 General discussion 8.1 Overview of findings At the beginning of the paper, we distinguished between essentialist accounts of artterm on the one hand, and two particular non-essentialist accountsterm – the cluster and disjunctive views – on the other. Essentialism requires a set of individually necessary and jointly sufficient conditions for an object to be artterm. According to cluster and disjunctive accountsterm, by contrast, an object can constitute artterm if it manifests one or several individually nonessential properties from a set of features.

Our empirical inquiry has explored (Q1) whether any of the three properties are seen by the folk as necessary and/or sufficient properties for an object’s status of artterm, and, more generally, (Q2) whether the folk concept of artterm is an essentialist concept.

As regards the first question (Q1), we found that none of the three properties proved necessary: Mean artterm ratings exceeded the midpoint of the scale in both the visual artterm and musical context if the object manifested only beautyterm or only intentionality. Correlatively, this suggests that either property was sufficient for an object to be deemed artterm.Footnote 12 Beautyterm exerted a substantial impact on art judgmentsterm·var in both artistic contexts, whereas the impact of intentionality was less pronounced in the musical context. Institutional recognitionterm was clearly the least important feature of artterm: In the visual artterm context, it proved insufficient by itself (though in the musical context, mean artterm ratings for objects which only had the feature of recognition exceeded the midpoint and just about reached the significance threshold). Furthermore, we detected an interesting interaction between beautyterm and recognition: For nonbeautiful objects, the presence of recognition tended to substantially raise the level of art judgmentsterm·var.

Given that none of the three properties that feature most prominently in the literature capture a necessary feature of the folk concept of artterm, the folk concept of artterm might not be essentialist in nature (as stated, of course one could test further potential properties). Instead, it seems to be either a cluster or a disjunctive conceptterm, and all three features tested seem to be part of the cluster of properties that can, by themselves or in conjunction with others, confer the status of artterm. According to our findings, in each context, beautyterm suffices to confer the status of artterm. Intentionality does in the visual context and is trending in the musical context. Recognition is (just about) sufficient by itself in the musical context, though does not suffice when it comes to paintings. However, recognition exerts a significant influence on art judgmentsterm·var when the object lacks beautyterm. Both the context-sensitivity of the factor’s relative impact and the significant beautyterm*recognition interaction provide some support for the hypothesis that the folk concept of artterm is a cluster conceptterm: Consistent with what cluster theories would predict, the impact of each factor can change from situation to situation. The concept of artterm is sensitive both to artistic domainterm·var and the presence or absence of other features. In different contexts, different criteria might be relevant for categorising objects as artterm or not artterm.

We have also gathered data on the question under what conditions a creator is deemed an artistterm. Broadly speaking, whether or not an agent is considered an artistterm does – expectedly – track whether or not the object they created is deemed artterm. However, and this is an interesting and surprising finding, in the visual context, we found a divergence: Beautiful, accidentally produced creations (recognized or not) are considered artterm, though their creators are not considered artiststerm. What this suggests, at least prima facie, is that people are willing to attribute the status of artterm to objects that were created without a creative intention and not created by artiststerm. This is surprising for several reasons: First, most authors in the philosophy of artterm assume that whatever is the real nature of artterm, it must be the result of intentional action (Dutton, 2009; Mag Uidhir, 2013). Artworksterm are almost always described as artifacts, which entails that they must be the product of intentional creationterm. The role of intention for artterm status and artterm interpretation has been emphasized extensively not only by philosophers but also in the psychological and empirical literature (Bloom, 1996; Jucker et al., 2014; Kamber & Enoch, 2018; Newman & Bloom, 2012; Newman & Smith, 2018).

Second, quite obviously, being an artistterm and being the source of art-making intentions is closely related, because any object that is the product of intentional action must have a creator. For instance, according to Mag Uidhir, “if what it is to be an artworkterm is to be the product of a successful art-attempt, then presumably what it is to be the artistterm of a particular artworkterm is to be the source of the intentions directing the actions constitutive of the successful art-attempt of which that particular artworkterm is the product. From this, it follows that an artworkterm must have an artistterm.” (2013: 45). According to the standard view, then, artterm must be intentionally made, and it must have an intentionally acting creator. That people are willing to recognize artworksterm that do not have an artistterm might sound paradoxical at the outset. However, it is less so when we think of artterm created by non-human agents, such as artificial intelligence. Although AI-driven robots are standardly not deemed artiststerm, their creations (in experiments similar to the ones here presented) frequently are considered artterm nonetheless (see MikalonytėpersName & KneerpersName, 2022). One could wonder if people simply ascribe the creative intention to the human creator of AI instead of the AI-driven machine itself and consider the human creator the source of artistic intentions. However, the results of the current study show that people are open to the idea of artterm being created without a creator in a more general sense.

Although our results suggest that creative intention at the moment of an action that results in a work of artterm is not necessary, it is hard to imagine intentionality being entirely absent from artterm. In similar cases, intentionality often becomes relevant during the artworkterm selection or curation process. One familiar example is the creative process of AI-generated artterm, where the human user of the system is often presented with multiple outputs and selects the best one. Another example is animal artterm or human creations made by accident, both of which can be seen as instances of artterm made without artistic intention. However, the pieces ultimately presented to the audience are not chosen randomly but are often intentionally selected by humans. In the context of artterm created without a creative intention, intentionality is unlikely to disappear completely, at least not from the process of conferring artterm status: artistic intention here is replaced by curatorial intentionterm. We leave these interesting, more sophisticated aspects of intentionality to be explored in future research.

8.2 Limitations While we have delivered substantial evidence that the folk concept of artterm is a cluster conceptterm and not an essentialist one, much work is needed to elucidate said concept in more depth.

First, our inquiry is limited to only two artistic contexts (painting and music). Given that even across these two contexts we detected some differences, it stands to reason to explore a wide variety of further artistic domainsterm·var, and ideally employ several distinct scenarios for each to test for potential intra-domain variation. This would also help to ensure that differences found between artistic contexts have not been determined by differences between scenarios or perhaps differences in the plausibility of the stories.

Second, as one reviewer has helpfully pointed out, a full factorial design with three distinct factors (and thus eight individual conditions), could give rise to some combinations of factors that might sound less plausible than others. It might thus be helpful to also test each factor in separate experiments, maximizing plausibility for both the absence and presence condition of the factor at issue.Footnote 13 It might also be helpful to rerun the full factorial design with extensive manipulation checks (ours are limited to intentionality).

Third, future experiments should randomise the order of the three (or more) factors. Since information about intention and beautyterm was always presented earlier than institutional recognitionterm in our scenarios, order effects cannot be ruled out.

Fourth, although we have tested several properties that have prominently featured in both essentialist and disjunctive or cluster accounts of artterm, the net should be cast wider. Dutton, for instance, lists (though does not limit himself to) twelve distinct criteria in his cluster account; (1) direct pleasure, (2) skill and virtuosity, (3) style, (4) novelty and creativity, (5) criticism, (6) representation, (7) special focus, (8) expressive individuality, (9) emotional saturation, (10) intellectual challenge, (11) artterm traditions and institutions, (12) imaginative experience, and, in addition, intentional creationterm as a necessary condition (Dutton, 2009).

Fifth, in future research, it would be useful to make a clearer distinction between the intent to produce something that falls under the concept of artterm and other intentions of the appropriate kind that do not involve having a concept of artterm– for instance, an intention to create a rhythmical sound structure or to sketch something resembling a horse (see Lopes, 2007).

Sixth, empirical research in the social sciences tends to oversample populations from WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic) countries in general, and US Americans in particular (Henrich et al., 2010, see also De Block & Kelly, 2022). Some have argued for a surprising stability of experimental-philosophical findings across populations and cultures (Knobe, 2019, 2021), citing studies that report cross-cultural convergence on topics ranging from the metaphysics of free will (Sarkissian et al., 2010), central intuitions in epistemology (Rose et al., 2019) and intentionality attribution (Lin et al., 2019), norms of assertion (KneerpersName, 2021), to key concepts in philosophy of law (Hannikainen et al., 2021, 2022). Others have countered this contention (Stich & Machery, 2022), pointing to studies where results vary strongly across cultures. In the experimental philosophy of aesthetics, there is but one study to date which reports some, yet limited, divergence concerning intuitions of intersubjective validity in aesthetic judgments across 19 different countries (Cova et al., 2019). A systematic inquiry into the folk concept of artterm thus requires cross-cultural experiments.

Seventh, given potentially diverging reasons for identical or similar responses, quantitative research of the sort reported in this paper might need to be supplemented with qualitative data, asking participants why they responded as they did. Given technological advances, corpus analysis constitutes another possibility (for examples see Baumgartner & KneerpersName, 2025; Sytsma et al., 2019; Sytsma & Fischer, 2023; Reuter & Baumgartner, 2024; for general discussion, see e.g. Andow, 2016; Chartrand, 2022; Fischer & Curtis, 2019; Plou, 2023).

Eighth, novel experiments are needed to better distinguish between cluster and disjunctive definitions. Future experiments can proceed in two directions: (1) further explorations of the context-sensitivity of the concept of artterm, and (2) the possibility of new cases: under what conditions might the current application conditions be reconsidered?

Finally, whereas the vignette-based approach has its strengths as it allows experimenters to tightly control the manipulated factors, one might argue that an essential part of aesthetic evaluation lies in a first-hand aesthetic experienceterm, and the present studies do not involve such an experience. After all, textual vignettes leave out many details which must be filled in by a reader in her imagination. Readers tend to choose different details, which leads to different intuitive verdicts. However, this problem can– at least in the domain of aesthetics – be solved by using visual (or aural) stimuli (Weinberg, 2018: p. 237). It has been demonstrated in experimental philosophy of music, for instance, that using acoustic stimuli in addition to text vignettes (vs. text vignettes only) influences people’s intuitions (Puy, 2022). Hence, it might be helpful to include actual or potential works of artterm among the stimuli to explore the robustness of the findings here presented.

9 Conclusion Whereas it is notoriously difficult to pick out the correct definition of artterm, the broadest question in the literature on artterm theories – and also the question we aimed to investigate in this study – concerns the type of concept the expression “artterm” most plausibly denotes. Can artterm be defined by individually necessary and jointly sufficient conditions or by a disjunction of minimally sufficient conditions? If the latter is true, is the set of conditions determinate or not?

Our initial exploration of the three most widely discussed factors – intentional creationterm, aesthetic valueterm and institutional recognitionterm – showed that none of them is deemed individually necessary. Although further factors, such as emotional expressivity or formal complexity, could be explored in follow-up studies, our findings constitute tentative evidence that the folk concept of artterm is not an essentialist concept and that it is a cluster conceptterm instead. If the correct account of artterm must be consistent with the folk concept of artterm – and there are good reasons to assume that this is the case– the cluster theory of artterm is the most promising one we have at our disposal.
