claim-typenull-result
mechanismTHE TERM IS INTRODUCED IN SCARE QUOTES AND GIVEN A BRIEF DESCRIPTION RATHER THAN A DEFINITION: the COMPUTABLE numbers MAY BE DESCRIBED BRIEFLY as the real numbers whose expressions as a decimal are CALCULABLE BY FINITE MEANS. The quotation marks and the word BRIEFLY both mark that the real definitional work is still to come — “The "computable" numbers may be described briefly as the real numbers whose expressions as a decimal are calculable by finite means.”
mechanismTHE OBJECT OF STUDY IS CHOSEN FOR TECHNICAL CONVENIENCE AND THE AUTHOR SAYS SO: the subject is OSTENSIBLY the computable numbers, several other objects would be ALMOST EQUALLY EASY to treat, THE FUNDAMENTAL PROBLEMS INVOLVED ARE THE SAME IN EACH CASE - and the numbers were chosen FOR EXPLICIT TREATMENT AS INVOLVING THE LEAST CUMBROUS TECHNIQUE. What the paper is about is a presentational decision — “Although the subject of this paper is ostensibly the computable numbers. it is almost equally easy to define and investigate computable functions of an integral variable or a real or computable variable, computable predicates, and so forth. The fundamental problems involved are, however, the same in each case, and I have chosen the computable numbers for explicit treatment as involving the least cumbrous technique.”
mechanismTHE DEFINITION IS OFFERED IN THE FIRST PERSON POSSESSIVE: ACCORDING TO MY DEFINITION, a number is computable IF ITS DECIMAL CAN BE WRITTEN DOWN BY A MACHINE. The criterion is a capability of a device, and the sentence marks that this is one definition among possible others — “According to my definition, a number is computable if its decimal can be written down by a machine.”
mechanismTHE ADEQUACY OF THE DEFINITION TO THE INFORMAL NOTION IS ANNOUNCED AS SOMETHING TO BE ARGUED IN ITS OWN SECTIONS: two sections are given over to ARGUMENTS WITH THE INTENTION OF SHOWING that the computable numbers INCLUDE ALL NUMBERS WHICH COULD NATURALLY BE REGARDED AS COMPUTABLE - so the match between the formal class and the natural one is itself a claim requiring support — “In §§ 9, 10 I give some arguments with the intention of showing that the computable numbers include all numbers which could naturally be regarded as computable.”
mechanismTWO NEIGHBOURING TERMS ARE SEPARATED BY A COUNTEREXAMPLE RATHER THAN BY ARGUMENT: the computable numbers DO NOT include all DEFINABLE numbers, AND AN EXAMPLE IS GIVEN of a definable number which is not computable. The boundary between the two words is established by exhibiting one object that falls on the far side of it — “The computable numbers do not, however, include all definable numbers, and an example is given of a definable number which is not computable.”
mechanismANOTHER AUTHOR'S TERM IS RECORDED AS EQUIVALENT AND VERY DIFFERENTLY DEFINED: Church has introduced an idea of EFFECTIVE CALCULABILITY, WHICH IS EQUIVALENT TO MY COMPUTABILITY, BUT IS VERY DIFFERENTLY DEFINED - and the PROOF of the equivalence is deferred to an appendix. Two vocabularies, one extension, and the identity is a theorem rather than a convention — “In a recent paper Alonzo Church f has introduced an idea of "effective calculability", which is equivalent to my "computability", but is very differently defined.”
mechanismTHE CENTRAL ADMISSION, AND IT IS THIS PROJECT'S PROBLEM STATED IN 1936: NO ATTEMPT HAS YET BEEN MADE to show that the COMPUTABLE numbers include all numbers WHICH WOULD NATURALLY BE REGARDED AS COMPUTABLE, and ALL ARGUMENTS WHICH CAN BE GIVEN ARE BOUND TO BE, FUNDAMENTALLY, APPEALS TO INTUITION, AND FOR THIS REASON RATHER UNSATISFACTORY MATHEMATICALLY. Whether a formalism captures the notion it was built for cannot be proved, only argued - said by the person who built the formalism — “No attempt has yet been made to show that the " computable " numbers include all numbers which would naturally be regarded as computable. Al I arguments which can be given are bound to be, fundamentally, appeals to intuition, and for this reason rather unsatisfactory mathematically.”
mechanismTHE QUESTION BEHIND THE DEFINITION IS NAMED, AND IT IS NOT A MATHEMATICAL QUESTION: THE REAL QUESTION AT ISSUE IS WHAT ARE THE POSSIBLE PROCESSES WHICH CAN BE CARRIED OUT IN COMPUTING A NUMBER. The formal apparatus is answerable to a claim about what can be done, not about what follows from axioms — “The real question at issue is " What are the possible processes which can be carried out in computing a number?"”
mechanismA TAXONOMY OF WAYS TO ARGUE THAT A DEFINITION CAPTURES ITS CONCEPT, WITH A CONDITION ON THE SECOND: (a) A DIRECT APPEAL TO INTUITION; (b) A PROOF OF THE EQUIVALENCE OF TWO DEFINITIONS - IN CASE THE NEW DEFINITION HAS A GREATER INTUITIVE APPEAL; (c) GIVING EXAMPLES OF LARGE CLASSES of numbers which are computable. **An equivalence proof is evidence for adequacy only if one of the two definitions is the more intuitive** - otherwise it moves the problem rather than solving it — “The arguments which I shall use are of three kinds. (a) A direct appeal to intuition. (6) A proof of the equivalence of two definitions (in case the new definition has a greater intuitive appeal). (c) Giving examples of large classes of numbers which are computable.”
mechanismA FEATURE OF THE APPARATUS IS DECLARED INESSENTIAL BY AN APPEAL TO THE READER'S ASSENT: the two-dimensional character of the paper IS SOMETIMES USED in elementary arithmetic, but SUCH A USE IS ALWAYS AVOIDABLE, and - I THINK THAT IT WILL BE AGREED - it is NO ESSENTIAL of computation. The idealisation is argued for rather than assumed, and the argument offered is that the reader will agree — “In elementary arithmetic the two-dimensional character of the paper is sometimes used. But such a use is always avoidable, and I think that it will be agreed that the two-dimensional character of paper is no essential”
mechanismA RESTRICTION IS JUSTIFIED BY THE INDISCRIMINABILITY THAT WOULD FOLLOW WITHOUT IT, AND THEN MINIMISED: if an infinity of symbols were allowed there would be SYMBOLS DIFFERING TO AN ARBITRARILY SMALL EXTENT - and the effect of the restriction IS NOT VERY SERIOUS, because SEQUENCES of symbols can always be used in place of single ones. The bound is defended by what could not be told apart — “I shall also suppose that the number of symbols which may be printed is finite. If we were to allow an infinity of symbols, then there would be symbols differing to an arbitrarily small extent j. The effect of this restriction of the number of symbols is not very serious. It is always possible to use sequences of symbols in the place of single symbols.”
mechanismTHE SAME ARGUMENT IS APPLIED TO THE MIND, AND ITS FORM IS EXPLICITLY REUSED: the number of STATES OF MIND which need be taken into account is finite, THE REASONS FOR THIS ARE OF THE SAME CHARACTER as those restricting the symbols, because an infinity of states of mind would include some ARBITRARILY CLOSE that WILL BE CONFUSED - and again the restriction DOES NOT SERIOUSLY AFFECT COMPUTATION, since complicated states can be traded for more symbols on the tape — “We will also suppose that the number of states of mind which need be taken into account is finite. The reasons for this are of the same character as those which restrict the number of symbols. If we admitted an infinity of states of mind, some of them will be '' arbitrarily close " and will be confused. Again, the restriction is not one which seriously affects computation, since the use of more complicated states of mind can be avoided by writing more symbols on the tape.” [checked against the archived source, not this rendering]
mechanismTHE ATOM OF THE ANALYSIS IS DEFINED BY THE LIMITS OF IMAGINATION: the operations are split into SIMPLE OPERATIONS WHICH ARE SO ELEMENTARY THAT IT IS NOT EASY TO IMAGINE THEM FURTHER DIVIDED. What counts as indivisible is fixed by what cannot readily be pictured as divided, not by a property of the operations — “Let us imagine the operations performed by the computer to be split up into "simple operations" which are so elementary that it is not easy to imagine them further divided.”
mechanismAN EMPIRICAL CLAIM ABOUT READING IS ADMITTED INTO A MATHEMATICAL ARGUMENT AND MARKED AS SUCH: compound symbols, if too lengthy, CANNOT BE OBSERVED AT ONE GLANCE - THIS IS IN ACCORDANCE WITH EXPERIENCE - and the demonstration offered is that WE CANNOT TELL AT A GLANCE whether two long strings of nines are the same. A fact about human perception is doing load-bearing work in a proof about computability — “The differences from our point of view between the single and compound symbols is that the compound symbols, if they are too lengthy, cannot be observed at one glance. This is in accordance with experience. We cannot tell at a glance whether 9999999999999999 and 999999999999999 are the same.”
