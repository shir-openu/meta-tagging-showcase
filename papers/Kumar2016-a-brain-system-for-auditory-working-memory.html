While one study using fMRIterm·mth ( Gaab et al., 2003 ) showed increased activity in auditory cortexterm, the other study using PET ( Zatorre et al., 1994 ) showed decreased regional cerebral blood flow in auditory cortexterm. Furthermore, the paradigm used in those two studies does not permit a differentiation of the encodingterm, maintenanceterm, or retrievalterm phases of WMterm·dom. The current study assessed the fundamental bases for encodingterm, maintenanceterm, and retrievalterm of single tones in AWMterm·dom.

A second question relates to the nature and specificity of representations in AWMterm·dom. Conventionally, sustained activityterm observed in a given brain area during the maintenanceterm period was regarded as a neural basis of WMterm·dom. However, a number of recent studies ( Harrison and Tong, 2009 ; Serences et al., 2009 ; Riggall and Postle, 2012 ) have shown that although a brain region may not show elevated activity during the delay period, patterns of subthreshold activity within the same area may nevertheless contain information about the remembered stimulus. Moreover, sustained activityterm does not imply that the activity is related to WMterm·dom per se (e.g., it may be related to sustained attention). In this study, we assessed whether representations during AWMterm·dom are specific to the maintained tone, both in auditory cortexterm and in higher-order areas, including frontal cortex.

A third question concerns how representations in auditory cortexterm are kept active during the maintenanceterm period. A consistent observation in WMterm·dom studies in other sensory modalities has been sustained activation during maintenanceterm in multiple brain areas, including frontal and parietal regions ( Salazar et al., 2012 ). Interaction between sensory cortex and these areas is thought to support active sensory representations. Although some evidence for this hypothesis for humans is available in the visual domain ( Gazzaley et al., 2004 ), we are not aware of any such evidence in the auditory domain. In this study, we measured functional connectivityterm between auditory cortexterm and other areas to delineate the functional network underlying AWMterm·dom.

Last, we were interested in the controversial role of the hippocampusterm in WMterm·dom suggested by certain visual studies ( Ranganath and D'Esposito, 2001 ; Axmacher et al., 2007 , 2010a , b ), as opposed to its better established role in long-term memory (LTMterm·dom). In this study, in addition to showing robust involvement of hippocampusterm in all phases (encodingterm, maintenanceterm, and retrievalterm) of AWMterm·dom, we go beyond the simple demonstration of hippocampal activity and evaluate the relationship between this and behavior that supports a model of hippocampal involvement in WMterm·dom based on an overlap with LTMterm·dom.

To address these questions, we measured the fMRIterm·mth BOLD response while subjects, after listening to a pair of tones, were then cued to maintain either a low or a high tone for 16 s. Using univariate analysis on the fMRIterm·mth data, we show sustained maintenanceterm activity in the auditory cortexterm, hippocampusterm, and frontal areas, including the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG). Using multivoxel pattern analysis (MVPA)term·def on the fMRIterm·mth data ( Chadwick et al., 2012 ; KumarpersName et al., 2014b ), we show that activity patterns in auditory cortexterm and the left IFG (LIFGterm) are specific to information kept in WMterm·dom. Using functional connectivityterm analysis ( Whitfield-Gabrieli and Nieto-Castanon, 2012 ), we demonstrate long-range connectivityterm between auditory cortexterm and both the hippocampusterm and IFG. The data support a scheme based on content-specific representations in auditory cortexterm during AWMterm·dom that are kept active during the maintenanceterm period by long-range functional connections from the hippocampusterm and IFG.

Materials and Methods

Subjects.

Seventeen healthy adults (8 females; mean age, 29.5 years; age range, 19–52 years) participated in this study after providing written informed consent to undergo procedures approved by the local ethics committee. Participants were paid for their participation and were selected based on the following criteria: normal hearing and no musical training. Data from one subject could not be used because of technical problems in the sound delivery in the MRI scanner.

Stimuli.

Stimuli consisted of pure tones, logarithmically sampled at random either from a low range of 200–300 Hz or a high range of 2500–3000 Hz. A set of three tones was chosen randomly in each range. A different set of frequencies was produced for each session. Auditory stimuli were generated at a sampling rate of 44.1 kHz in Matlab version R2013b (MathWorks) and presented using Cogent ( http://www.vislab.ucl.ac.uk ). Sounds were delivered binaurally through MRI-compatible in-ear headphones [model S14, Sensimetrics ( http://www.sens.com/s14/ )] at an intensity of ∼70 dBA.

Procedure and experimental design.

Outside the scanner, subjects performed a pitch discrimination task (one block of 16 trials) to ensure that their pitch discrimination thresholds were within the normal range. This was followed by a practice block of the WMterm·dom task ( Fig. 1 ), which was then performed inside the scanner.

Figure 1.

Working memory paradigm. After a pair of tones was presented, subjects were cued to maintain one of the two tones in working memory for 16 s. A decision period followed in which subjects compared a probe tone with the tone in working memory.

Inside the scanner, subjects were scanned in four runs each consisting of 24 trials. The start of a given trial of each memory block was indicated by text instructions appearing onscreen (“Sounds to start soon”). This message was presented for 1 s and was followed by the presentation of two pure tones picked randomly from each of the two categories (encodingterm). There was always a low tone and a high tone contained within each set of sounds, and the order in which they appeared was randomized. Therefore, the low tone could be presented at the first or second serial position and vice versa. The stimulus duration was 0.5 s with an interstimulus interval of 1 s. Next, 0.6 s after the second tone, another message appeared on the screen for 2.5 s, indicating which tone subjects had to actively maintain in mind (Cue). Whether tone 1 or tone 2 had to be held in mind was randomized. In each block of 24 trials, half of them required maintenanceterm of a low tone. After subjects knew which tone to maintain, they had to actively keep this particular tone in mind for 16 s, while maintaining gaze on a fixation cross at the center of the screen. Finally, a probe tone was presented for 0.5 s. Participants had to decide whether this tone was the same or different from the one held in mind. The message “Same or different?” appeared onscreen for 1 s followed by a button press response. Subjects were instructed to make a response as quickly as possible without making mistakes within a time window of 3 s. Each trial was followed by a resting period of variable length, jittered at 8–12 s. For trials with a different probe than the test tone, a ±10% change in frequency was made. Equal numbers of trials with and without change were presented in a block.

Functional imaging/MRI data collection.

All imaging data were collected on a Siemens 3 tesla Quattro head-only MRI scanner ( http://www.siemens.com ) at the Wellcome Trust Functional Imaging Centre. MRI images were acquired continuously (TR, 1.1 s; TE, 15.85 ms; flip angle, 15°; 3D sequence; whole brain acquisition; nominal flip angle; isotropic voxel size, 2 mm; matrix size, 96 × 96). A total of 725 volumes were acquired per run. After the fMRIterm·mth runs, a high-resolution (1 × 1 × 1 mm) T1-weighted structural MRI scan was acquired for each subject.

Univariate analysis.

MRI data were analyzed using SPM8 ( http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm ). At the preprocessing stage, images were realigned to the first volume, then normalized to stereotactic space and finally smoothed with a 3D Gaussian kernel with full-width at half-maximum of 6 mm. After preprocessing, a general linear model (GLM)term·stat was used for statistical analysis. The design matrix consisted of boxcar functions encodingterm the onsets and durations of different events convolved with a hemodynamic response function.

The following events were included in the design matrix and modeled as three regressors: (1) an encodingterm period modeled as a single block of 3 s (1 s alert plus 2 s sound); (2) a maintenanceterm period with onset starting from the onset of cue and of duration 18.5 s (2.5 s Cue period plus 16 s); and (3) a retrievalterm period modeled as a block of 3 s after the maintenanceterm period. The design matrix also included physiological and motion regressors of no interest. A high-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 1/128 Hz was applied to remove low-frequency fluctuations in the BOLD signal. Once the GLM for each subject was estimated, the contrasts of parameter estimates for each individual subject were entered into second-level t tests to form statistical parametric maps, and a whole-brain random-effects analysis was implemented. The correlation of brain activity with behavioral performanceterm at group level was performed using regression analysis with age of the subjects as a regressor of no interestterm·stat. Given prior hypotheses about the involvement of auditory cortexterm and hippocampusterm in WMterm·dom, small-volume correction (using anatomically defined volumes)term·stat was used for auditory cortexterm and hippocampusterm.

One concern in WMterm·dom studies using fMRIterm·mth is that the maintenanceterm phase immediately follows the encodingterm phase and, because of the delay intrinsic to the BOLD signal, the regressors for the maintenanceterm and encodingterm phases may be correlated. The activity observed in the maintenanceterm phase may thus be contaminated with activity during the encodingterm phase. In our study, because the maintenanceterm duration was much longer than the encodingterm phase, the correlation coefficient between the encodingterm and maintenanceterm regressors in the design matrix was very small (0.04). Activity during the maintenanceterm phase is not therefore explained by correlation between the regressors in this design. We also repeated the univariate analysis by leaving 4 s on either side of the maintenanceterm phase [i.e., modeling only the middle portion (10.5 s) of the maintenanceterm phase; Zarahn et al., 1997 ] to avoid any possibility of “lagged” blood flow changes during maintenanceterm affecting the maintenanceterm measurements. The results of this analysis were very similar to the one using all of the maintenanceterm period.

Time series were extracted by first dividing the continuous time series into individual trials, with presentation of the visual alert marking the beginning of a trial, and 33 s (30 scans) after the alert cue marking the end of the trial. Trial activity was then referenced with respect to the first scan of that trial before computing the average across all trials. To compute the time series for a given region of interest (ROI), an average across all voxels within that ROI was computed.

Multivoxel pattern analysis.

The principal aim of MVPAterm is to determine whether patterns of activity in a given area can distinguish between two or more experimental conditions ( Haynes and Rees, 2006 ; Norman et al., 2006 ). We used a linear support vector machine (SVM) classifier to determine whether patterns of activity from different brain areas could be decoded when participants held a low or high tone in WMterm·dom. Activity during the maintenanceterm period was summarized by computing β values for each trial on the unsmoothed data. In our experiment, the probe tone presented at the retrievalterm phase was of the same category that was cued for the maintenanceterm phase. It is, therefore, likely that the probe tone could aid in the classification of tones during the maintenanceterm phase (because of the common variance captured by maintenanceterm and recall phase regressors). To ensure that this was not so, only the middle 10.5 s of the maintenanceterm period was modeled, leaving 4 s on either side based on recommendations by Zarahn et al. (1997) . This was done by entering each trial as a separate regressor in the GLM analysis. Regressors for the encodingterm and retrievalterm phases, and regressors of no interest (motion and physiological regressors) were also added in the design matrix. For each subject, the following five ROIs were selected: Heschl's gyrus (HGterm), planum temporale (PTterm), hippocampusterm, LIFGterm Brodmann areas (BAs) 44 and 45, and right IFG (RIFGterm; BAs 44 and 45). For HGterm, the ROI was defined based on cytoarchitectonic maps as defined in the study by Morosan et al. (2001) , a MNI template of which is available in the Anatomy toolbox ( Eickhoff et al., 2005 ); for PTterm, the probabilistic map (thresholded at 30%) as defined in the study by Westbury et al. (1999) ; for hippocampusterm, the map as defined in the study by Amunts et al. (2005) ; and for LIFGterm and RIFGterm, we used ROIs as defined in the WFU PickAtlas toolbox ( Maldjian et al., 2003 ). To reduce noise in classification, only trials with correct responses were used. Three subjects who did not perform the task well [they had d -prime (detection) values of 0.89, 0.99, and 1.16, respectively] were dropped from the analysis. We did not drop these subjects from the univariate analysis because we wanted to analyze the correlation of brain activity with behavioral performanceterm.

The SVM classifier, as implemented in the LIBSVM toolbox ( Chang and Lin, 2011 ), was used with the following leave-one-session-out cross-validation strategyterm·stat: the classifier was trained on data from three of the four sessions and tested on the left-out session. For training and testing, β values at all voxels within a defined ROI were used as the feature vector. The feature vector was normalized to unit norm before inputting to the classifier. Classifier accuracy values for each brain region were compared with chance, which in our case was 50%. Given that we were interested in whether results were significantly above chance, one-tailed t teststerm·stat were used for testing the statistical significance of classification.

Psychophysiological interactions.

Connectivityterm analysis was conducted using psychophysiological interactions (PPIs)term·stat as implemented in the CONN toolbox ( Whitfield-Gabrieli and Nieto-Castanon, 2012 ). The analysis was limited to the following five ROIs in each hemisphere: HGterm, PTterm, IFG, anterior hippocampusterm, and posterior hippocampusterm. After the data were preprocessed using SPM, the artifact detection (ART) toolbox ( Mazaika et al., 2007 ) was used to detect outliers. Time points in the series were marked as outliers if the global signal exceeded 3 SDs and/or the movement exceeded ≥1 mm. The effect of movement and physiological parameters on the BOLD signal was reduced by regressing out motion and physiological artifacts, along with their first-order temporal derivative, by running whole-brain voxelwise regression. Additionally, five covariates generated using the aCmpCorr method ( Behzadi et al., 2007 ), which uses principal component analysis on the measurements made in the white matter and CSF of each individual subject's segmented white matter and CSF masks, were used. The data were then high-pass filtered with a cutoff frequency of 0.008 Hz. PPIterm analysis was performed for each ROI to every other ROI (ROI-to-ROI analysis) for every subject. We compared the encodingterm, maintenanceterm, and retrievalterm conditions against each other instead of comparing each of these against baseline. This is because physiological variables such as heart rate during cognitive tasks (here, working memory) are different from the rest condition ( Middleton et al., 1999 ), and changes in physiological variables are known to confound functional connectivityterm in fMRIterm·mth ( Birn, 2012 ). Since the physiological parameters will be similar across encodingterm, maintenanceterm, and retrievalterm conditions compared with any of these conditions against baseline, to minimize the effect of physiological noise on the connectivityterm analysis, we report the comparison in connectivityterm among the three conditions. The contrasts computed at the first levels were then submitted to second-level analysis. The group-level connectivityterm between the regions was corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate methodterm·stat.

To make sure that the statistical significance of our MVPAterm results was not biased by the assumptions of the parametric t test, we also evaluated the statistical significance using a nonparametric test for MVPAterm·stat as proposed in the study by Stelzer et al. The p value for the group accuracy with correct labels is then determined based on this null distribution. Using this procedure the p values obtained for HGterm and left IFG are 0.0013 and 0.015, respectively. These values are almost identical to the p values using the t test.

Discussion

Auditory cortexterm

We observed sustained activation of the auditory cortexterm during a maintenanceterm period, which is in contrast to findings from Linke et al. (2011) , who observed suppression during maintenanceterm. This might be attributable to differences in the task in the two studies. While Linke et al. (2011) required subjects to maintain two tones from different categories without any cue before or after the tones, participants in the present study were explicitly instructed to maintain one of the two tones after the tones were presented (retro-cueterm). Behavioral studies ( Matsukura et al., 2007 ; Pertzov et al., 2013 ) show that, without any cue, items maintained in memory tend to be forgotten rapidly, but a selective retro-cueterm leads to protection of the cued item from temporal decay during the maintenanceterm period. It is also known that maintaining multiple items, without a selective cue, leads to diffuse attention ( Makovsik and Jiang, 2007 ) and competition for memory resources, which suppress each other's representation ( Bahcall and Kowler, 1999 ). The suppression in activity during the maintenanceterm period observed by Linke et al. (2011) could therefore be driven by such a competition between the representation of tones. The later study of Linke and Cusack (2015) , which required maintenanceterm of a single sound in WMterm·dom showed activation during maintenanceterm, like the present study.

The specificity of auditory cortexterm activity during the maintenanceterm period was examined using MVPAterm, which showed that patterns of activity in HGterm, but not in PTterm, reliably encoded whether subjects were maintaining a low tone or a high tone during the delay period. Possible explanations for this finding include the idea that the representation in PTterm is in the form of high-level symbolic representations or “templates” ( GriffithspersName and Warren, 2002 ) as opposed to activity in HGterm that might more closely match the sensory pattern of the stimulus. Templates in PTterm might be more removed from the stimulus sensory structure and harder to disambiguate based on blood flow patterns. In any event, the work is congruent with visual studies ( Lebedev et al., 2004 ; Serences et al., 2009 ; Riggall and Postle, 2012 ) showing subthreshold activity during WMterm·dom that is content specific.

Hippocampusterm

The role of hippocampusterm in WMterm·dom is controversial ( Ranganath and Blumenfeld, 2005 ; Graham et al., 2010 ; Jeneson and Squire, 2011 ). While one school argues for a fundamental role of hippocampusterm in WMterm·dom ( Graham et al., 2010 ), the other school ( Jeneson and Squire, 2011 ) maintains that the primary role of hippocampusterm is in LTMterm·dom alone and argues that activity in hippocampusterm during WMterm·dom is observed only when either novel stimuli are maintained for long periods of time or the number of items maintained exceed the WMterm·dom capacity. In these conditions, according to this model, it is difficult to focus attention on the item or items to be remembered, and the deviation in attention causes loss of the item or items from the current focus of attention. To re-engage attention on the item to be remembered, the item is then recalled from LTMterm·dom. Models of WMterm·dom ( Cowan, 1995 ; Oberauer, 2009 ) also posit two separate components for items that are under the focus of attention and items that are active but outside the focus of attention. The latter component constitutes an “activated LTMterm” component of WMterm·dom ( Cowan, 1995 ). In our study, we used tones that changed across trials that were maintained for long intervals of time (16 s). It is, therefore, likely that subjects relied on LTMterm·dom to perform the task activating hippocampusterm in the process. Activity in hippocampusterm in that case would form part of the activated LTMterm component of WMterm·dom.

To further examine the role of hippocampusterm in WMterm·dom, we evaluated the correlation between behavioral performanceterm and hippocampusterm activity during the maintenanceterm period. In light of the proposal by Jeneson and Squire (2011) , we predicted that subjects who could not maintain a sustained focus of attention on the tone to be maintained would rely more on LTMterm·dom, and, therefore, hippocampusterm would be activated to a greater extent in these subjects. These subjects, however, would perform poorly on the task because recalling the tone from LTMterm·dom would entail proactive interference from tones that were presented on previous trials, which are also held in LTMterm·dom; as per the model of Cowan (1995) , these tones are held in the activated part of LTMterm·dom. A negative correlation between hippocampusterm activity and behavioral performanceterm is thus expected. This is exactly what we observed: activity in both left and right hippocampi increased with poorer performance. Furthermore, we also observed a negative correlation with behavior during recall in retrosplenial cortex, which has been known to be involved in retrievalterm of long-term memories ( Vann et al., 2009 ; Rugg and Vilberg, 2013 ; KumarpersName et al., 2014a ). This is also consistent with retrievalterm of LTMterm·dom during task performance.

We examined the variation of activity along the anterior–posterior axis of hippocampusterm ( Poppenk et al., 2013 ; Strange et al., 2014 ). While there is almost constant activity all along the axis during the maintenanceterm period, the activity for encodingterm and recall is dominant on the posterior end of the axis. The activity starts building from y = −20 mm, and then there is a striking increase in activation as the far posterior end is approached. The starting point of buildup closely agrees with the anatomical boundary between anterior and posterior hippocampusterm suggested by other studies ( Poppenk et al., 2013 ). In our study, auditory stimuli were presented both during the encodingterm and retrievalterm phases of the task. One possible interpretation is that the posterior hippocampusterm, compared with the anterior hippocampusterm, is more involved in the analysis of auditory stimuli in real time, during perception. This in turn predicts greater functional and structural connectivityterm between auditory cortexterm and the posterior hippocampusterm. Although structural connectivityterm between auditory cortexterm and hippocampusterm in humans is not completely understood, tract-tracing studies in primates suggest ( Munoz-Lopez et al., 2010 ) that auditory cortexterm projects directly to parahippocampal cortex, which is known to project preferentially to posterior hippocampusterm ( Aggleton, 2012 ).

Inferior frontal gyrus

Significant activity was observed in the LIFGterm, which lasted throughout the maintenanceterm period. The LIFGterm has been implicated in a number of WMterm·dom studies that required maintenanceterm of phonological information ( Paulesu et al., 1993 ; Awh et al., 1996 ; Strand et al., 2008 ). One role of LIFGterm in these studies is thought to be covert articulatory rehearsalterm, which keeps representations in the active state. However, this role of LIFGterm is not restricted to phonological WMterm·dom alone but also extends to rehearsalterm of pitch ( Koelsch et al., 2009 ). Although we did not explicitly instruct the subjects to follow any particular strategy in our study, pitch values of the low tone were in the vocal range that could be rehearsed as a basis for the LIFGterm activity. Activation of motor areas during maintenanceterm ( Table 1 ) also support the possibility of rehearsalterm being used during the maintenanceterm period. It should, however, be noted that the role of motor areas in working memory is still controversial as studies using nonrehearsable stimuli also show activity in motor areas during WMterm·dom ( Liao et al., 2014 ).

In the RIFGterm, we observed two foci of activation, one more ventral and posterior (vpRIFG), and the other more dorsal and anterior (daRIFG). The vpRIFG showed significant activity in all phases of the WMterm·dom task, whereas the daRIFG was active only during the retrievalterm phase. Although RIFGterm has been shown to be involved in a number of WMterm·dom studies ( Zatorre et al., 1994 ; Shivde and Thompson-Schill, 2004 ), a dominant view of the functioning of RIFGterm is that it actively inhibits the motor response to prepotent stimuli ( Aron et al., 2014 ). Although a complete reconciliation of RIFGterm involvement in both inhibition and WMterm·dom has yet to be made, one suggestion is that processes involved in inhibition, such as resistance to distractions, are useful in WMterm·dom ( Roberts and Pennington, 1996 ). Consistent with this idea, evidence from a study ( McNab et al., 2008 ) using both tasks suggests a common focus of activation in RIFGterm for both inhibition and WMterm·dom. This focus of activation matches with vpRIFG in our study. Furthermore, patients with lesions in prefrontal cortex are also known to be impaired in neglecting distractors during the maintenanceterm period ( Chao and Knight, 1998 ). Regarding daRIFG, which is active only during the retrievalterm phase, we cannot dissociate whether the activity is related to motor response or to retrievalterm of WMterm·dom information.

Is the sustained activityterm in LIFGterm and RIFGterm specific to the information held in WMterm·dom? Application of MVPAterm to these ROIs showed that, while patterns of activity in the LIFGterm could distinguish which of the two tones was held in WMterm·dom, the RIFGterm could not. LIFGterm is well known to be involved in speech production. In a recent study in which subjects were asked to repeat a spoken word, Flinker et al. (2015) showed that Broca's area transforms sensory representation of the word in the auditory cortexterm to articulatory representations, which are then passed on to motor cortex. The content-specific representations demonstrated during the maintenanceterm period in our study might reflect distinct articulatory representations of tones.

Correlation with behavior

During the encodingterm phase, positive correlations between behavioral performanceterm accuracy and activity in rTPJ and left STS were observed. The rTPJ is a part of the ventral attentional network that reorients attention toward task-relevant objects ( Corbetta and Shulman, 2002 ) or contextually updates the internal model in the event of behaviorally relevant perceptual input ( Geng and Vossel, 2013 ). One likely interpretation of the positive correlation in rTPJ, therefore, is that subjects who paid greater attention to the stimuli during encodingterm or updated the representations of the stimuli (tones changed across trials and sessions) performed better than those who did not. Left STS activity has been shown in the categorical perception of speech sounds ( Liebenthal et al., 2005 ) and musical intervals ( Klein and Zatorre, 2015 ). Greater activity in the left STS for subjects who performed better in the task may therefore reflect that these subjects encoded the two tones as distinct categories more reliably than subjects with a lower performance.

During the maintenanceterm period, we observed a positive correlation with activity in part of parietal cortex which has been shown to be involved in maintaining sustained attention ( Foucher et al., 2004 ). Subjects who could keep sustained attention during the maintenanceterm period performed better on the task. Negative correlation between activity in a set of areas that together comprise the DMNterm, and behavioral performanceterm was observed. The DMNterm is known to be active in mind wandering ( Mason et al., 2007 ). Greater activation in this network, therefore, implies more mind wandering during the maintenanceterm period and, therefore, poorer performance. Negative correlation was also observed in the right IFJterm. Evidence ( Roth and Courtney, 2007 ) shows that IFJterm is involved in updating the contents of WMterm·dom from LTMterm·dom. The subjects who used LTMterm·dom to perform the task activated the IFJterm more but (as explained above in the case for hippocampusterm) performed poorly, which explains the negative correlation observed in IFJterm.

Connectivityterm analysis

During the maintenanceterm period, we observed the long-range connectivityterm of the auditory cortexterm to hippocampusterm and IFG, which, putatively, keeps representations active in the auditory cortexterm. Specifically, connectivityterm of right hippocampusterm (both anterior and posterior) to auditory cortexterm is consistent with its role in keeping representations active in auditory cortexterm by recalling from the activated LTMterm in the event of deviation from the focus of attention (see above). Connectivityterm of LIFGterm with the auditory cortexterm during the maintenanceterm period is consistent with the role of LIFGterm in keeping the representations active in auditory cortexterm by subvocal rehearsalterm of the tones. Interestingly, both LIFGterm and HGterm have content-specific representations. Our data, therefore, suggest that HGterm and LIFGterm, possibly via PTterm, form a closed loop where tone-specific representations in the LIFGterm initiate subvocal rehearsalterm, which activates the auditory cortexterm. Functional connectivityterm of auditory cortexterm to the RIFGterm was also observed during the maintenanceterm period. The importance of both structural and functional connectivityterm between auditory cortexterm and RIFGterm for the normal perception of a sequence of tones has been shown in studies of disorders of music perception (compare with amusia; Albouy et al., 2013 ). Since listening to a sequence of tones requires a memory component capable of storing pitch information so as to integrate pitch across the sequence, the results of our study combined with those of other studies ( Zatorre et al., 1994 ; Koelsch et al., 2009 ; Albouy et al., 2013 ) clearly point toward a role of RIFGterm in the maintenanceterm of auditory information. Consistent with this, auditory WMterm·dom training is shown to increase the efficiency of neural processes in RIFGterm ( Schneiders et al., 2012 ).

In summary, our data point to a system for WMterm·dom in which content-specific representations in the auditory cortexterm are kept active by its remote connectivityterm with hippocampusterm and frontal areas.
