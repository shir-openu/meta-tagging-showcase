A Genusterm·key Definition for <i>Bacteria</i> and <i>Archaea</i> Based on a Standard Genome Relatedness Index.

Abstract In recent decades, the taxonomyterm·key of Bacteria and Archaea , and therefore genusterm·key designation, has been largely based on the use of a single ribosomal gene, the 16S rRNA geneterm·key, as a taxonomic marker. We propose an approach to delineate genera that excludes the direct use of the 16S rRNA geneterm·key and focuses on a standard genome relatedness index, the average nucleotide identityterm. Our findings are of importance to the microbiology community because the emergent properties of Bacteria and Archaea that are identified in this study will help assign genera with higher taxonomic resolution.

INTRODUCTION At the time of writing, 20,768 bacterial and archaeal species/subspecies and over 3,500 genera with validly published names have been described in the taxonomic literature ( 1 ); however, based on a 16S rRNA guided-phylogenetic approach, there are over 200,000 bacterial and archaeal species and 60,000 genera so far detected in the SILVA database ( 2 , 3 ). Sequence data stored in the Joint Genome Institute (JGI) database, which includes data from other databases, have increased exponentially over the past decade ( 4 ), with >76,000 genomes of bacterial and archaeal isolates, >9,000 metagenome-assembled genomes, and >4,000 single-cell amplified genomes being currently available (database accessed on 10 November 2019). There are >210,000 genome assemblies in GenBank (database accessed on 10 November 2019). Despite the increasing number of genomes, there were only <2,000 genomes of type strainsterm·dom that were publicly available at the start of this study, with 1,003 of these genomes only recently published ( 5 ). Currently, there are <9,000 nonredundant genomes of type strainsterm·dom that are publicly available ( 1 ). The number of genomes of type strainsterm·dom is presently increasing by over 1,000 per year, with recent plans to increase this number substantially ( 6 ); therefore, it is becoming easier to access data that represent taxa of interest. Consequently, this added layer of available information could aid in the formal characterization of microorganisms, of which an essential aspect is the proper assignment of genusterm·key and species (rule 12a of the International Code of Nomenclature of Prokaryotes [ICNP] [ 7 ]).

Historically, DNA-DNA hybridizationterm (DDHterm) has been the “gold standard” for species delineation, with a DDHterm value of ≥70% being recognized as the species boundary between two strains (see reference 8 and other references therein). Stackebrandt and Goebel ( 9 ) conducted a correlation analysis between DDHterm values and 16S rRNA geneterm·key sequence identities, and based on this, they proposed a boundary of 16S rRNA geneterm·key sequence similarity of 97% for species delineation. This value, which is still largely used today for operational taxonomic unit (OTU)-based analysis of microbial communities, has been updated by Stackebrandt and Ebers ( 10 ) to a value between 98.7 and 99.0%, based on a greater amount of available sequence data. Subsequently, as genome sequencing has become common, whole-genome comparisons became possible, leading to the advent of genome relatedness indices such as average nucleotide identityterm (ANIterm) ( 11 ), amino acid identity (AAI) ( 12 ), and digital DDHterm ( 13 ). More recently, Kim et al. ( 14 ) proposed a 16S rRNA geneterm·key sequence similarity threshold value of 98.65% for species delineation, equating to ANIterm values of 95 to 96%, which in turn have been equated to the classical standard species delineation threshold DDHterm value of 70% ( 15 , 16 ). A method that only relies on protein-coding genes (i.e., neither rRNA nor tRNA genes are included in analysis) is the Microbial Species Identifierterm (MiSIterm), which employs both alignment fractionsterm (AFterm) and ANIterm for demarcation of species, recommending threshold AFterm and ANIterm values of 0.6 and 96.5% using complete or nearly complete genomes ( 17 ).

Despite these advancements in resolving species delineation, practical guidelines that incorporate genomic properties to demarcate genera have been lacking even though genusterm·key assignment is key to performing meaningful comparisons regarding the physiology, metabolism, and genomic potential of microbes. Methods to demarcate genera have been proposed that are based on either AAI ( 18 ) or the percentage of conserved proteins (POCP; 19 ). The former method provided a range of AAI values (65 to 72%) that were originally obtained by correlation to a now-outdated 16S rRNA geneterm·key identity threshold for genusterm·key. The POCP method directly relies on the 16S rRNA geneterm·key sequence, which is in some cases insensitive to evolutionary changes in the rest of the genome of a given organism, as revealed by different species sharing >99% identity over the length of this gene. This method also arbitrarily sets a genusterm·key boundary at a POCP value of 50%. Additionally, the generally used arbitrary genusterm·key threshold of 95% 16S rRNA geneterm·key identity has been recently revisited to a lower minimum value of 94.5%, with a median sequence identity of 96.4% and confidence interval of 94.55 to 95.05% ( 3 ). In borderline cases, interpretation of results may be unclear if there are no alternative ways to confirm genusterm·key assignment. This is also the case for microorganisms with multiple highly divergent 16S rRNA genesterm·key ( 20 , 21 ). More recently, a taxonomyterm·key for Bacteria based on highly conserved protein-concatenated phylogeny and the normalization of ranks has been proposed ( 22 ). This normalized taxonomyterm·key uses ∼4% of a genome and directly relies on values of relative evolutionary divergence, a nonstandard index. Here, we propose a novel approach that builds on the MiSIterm method ( 17 ) and provides an objective, mathematically sound, and reproducible method of delimiting genera using whole-genome sequences and ANIterm, a standard genome relatedness index. In addition to identifying genusterm·key boundaries, we introduce the concept of the genus inflection pointterm·dom. We implement this approach by testing a variety of taxonomic groups of Bacteria and Archaea . Furthermore, evidence is presented to support the extensive rearrangement of Bacillusterm·key , Clostridium , Lactobacillusterm·key , and Photorhabdus , among other taxa, using a standard genomic index.

This value increases to 92% if known polyphyletic genera (e.g., Bacillusterm·key , Clostridium , Lactobacillusterm·key , and Pseudomonas ) are excluded. These polyphyletic genera had the greatest number of non-type species at or below the genus demarcation boundaryterm·dom. Selected case studies.

In the remainder of the manuscript, we will focus on five selected case studies, each of which has longstanding, historical, taxonomic issues. These cases will be discussed in more detail, with the goal of guiding the reader toward an interpretation of similar scenarios in other taxa. These case studies, along with the examples described above, provide a variety of different scenarios that can be analyzed using our approach.

Genusterm·key assignment in the recently rearranged Hydrogenovibrio - Thioalkalimicrobium - Thiomicrospira cluster was tested as an example of a bacterial group with historical taxonomic issues ( 24 , 25 ) ( Fig. S4A ). Boden et al. ( 26 ) recently provided a detailed evaluation of the characteristics of this cluster, which falls within the radiation of the Piscirickettsiaceae and is proposed to place many of the species into different genera. We tested these newly proposed assignments using the approach described above and the MiSIterm method. When testing genusterm·key assignment to Hydrogenovibrio , the type speciesterm·dom Hydrogenovibrio marinus DSM 11271 T was used as a primary reference ( Fig. 5a ). The single data point that crossed the genus demarcation boundaryterm·dom belonged to Hydrogenovibrio halophilus DSM 15072 T . Aside from this data point, there was no overlap of AFterm and ANIterm values between the type- and non-type species, indicating support for the assignment of H. crunogenus , H. thermophilus , and H. kuenenii to the genusterm·key Hydrogenovibrio . Similarly, the proposed new genusterm·key Thiomicrorhabdus ( 26 ) was also tested ( Fig. S4B ). The results indicate that the new classification is supported by AFterm and ANIterm values, as the type speciesterm·dom of genera in the family Piscirickettsiaceae and Thiomicrorhabdus spp. remain well separated. The reclassification of Thioalkalimicrobium spp. to Thiomicrospira spp. ( 26 ) is also supported by ANIterm and AFterm values ( Fig. S4C ), as these species form a cluster that is well separated from the type speciesterm·dom of genera in the family Piscirickettsiaceae .

Our approach has identified a potential misclassification of H. halophilus (basonym Thiomicrospira halophila ) because it clusters with the type speciesterm·dom of genera in the family Piscirickettsiaceae other than Hydrogenovibrio . Below, we show additional evidence that could support a potential reclassification of H. halophilus . A closer look at the multiple 16S rRNA geneterm·key sequences of H. halophilus revealed that they have 94.0 to 94.2% pairwise identities to the type speciesterm·dom, H. marinus DSM 11271 T . Our results differ from those of Boden et al., who reported 95.7% sequence identity ( 26 ) between H. halophilus and H. marinus . The difference in identity appears to be due to differences in the 16S rRNA geneterm·key sequence between the near-full-length version of Sorokin et al. (1,420 bp, GenBank accession number DQ390450 [ 27 ] and the full-length versions of this gene (1,439 bp each; IMG identifiers [IDs] 2518265101 and 2518265550) originating from a draft genome ( 28 ) and used in this study. The phylogenetic placement of this organism also supports the idea that H. halophilus is distinctly positioned in its own clade as it branches away from the cluster of other Hydrogenovibrio spp. ( Fig. 5b ). One differentiating characteristic of H. halophilus is the DNA G+C content of 56.6%, much higher than the ca. 44% of the other newly classified Hydrogenovibrio spp. Another major differentiating aspect is the higher NaCl optimum/maximum of 1.5 M/3.5 M for H. halophilus versus the lower 0.2 to 0.5/0.6 to 1.2 M for other Hydrogenovibrio spp. Therefore, based on AFterm and ANIterm results and molecular, phylogenetic, and physiological evidence, the assignment of H. halophilus to a new genusterm·key is justifiable. Beyond the taxonomic issue with H. halophilus , we also note the relatively low AFterm and ANIterm values of H. crunogenus and Hydrogenovibrio sp. strain MA2-6 (a strain of H. thermophilus ) in relation to H. marinus . These results in conjunction with the low bootstrap values for branches associated with H. crunogenus and H. thermophilus (<70% in reference 26 and in this study) suggest that there are other taxonomic issues that cannot be resolved at this time in this genusterm·key, in part due to the lack of genomes of type strainsterm·dom (see below for discussion on the genus inflection pointterm·dom).

The AFterm and ANIterm plots reveal different rates of change at the DNA level. In the few cases in which there are sufficient samples to analyze and/or the distribution of values is more widespread, the AFterm and ANIterm plots reveal a polynomial shape approximating a quartic function, with R 2 values generally at >0.96. A second derivative of this function results in a quadratic function that can be used to detect an inflection point with precision. Estimates based on several examples in this study indicate two inflection points. The overall mean of the first inflection point (AFterm, 0.333; ANIterm, 73.10%; Data Set S2C ) generally approximates the mean genus demarcation boundaryterm·dom of all cases herein presented (AFterm, 0.331; ANIterm, 73.98%; Data Set S2B ). Estimations of this genus inflection pointterm·dom via logistic and Gompertz functions generally agree with the estimations by the preferred quartic function, and their use is recommended for accuracy. The second inflection point, at 89 to 92% ANIterm, is consistently lower than the current ANIterm species threshold (≥95% ANIterm) and is seen with the quartic function in each of the cases in which the genus inflection pointterm·dom was able to be estimated. Related to this, genetic discontinuity between 80 and 95% ANIterm has been previously reported in various studies that included analysis of metagenomes and genomes of isolates ( 34 – 37 ). Recent results obtained by Delmont et al. ( 38 ) reported delineation of SAR 11 populations at ANIterm values lower than 95%, consistent with the ANIterm values of the second inflection points that were observed in this study. Because the second inflection point potentially deals with species delineation, detailed exploration (e.g., using subspecies type strainsterm·dom) and discussion of this topic are outside the scope of this study. However, it is noted that ANIterm values of 89 to 92% largely correspond to 16S rRNA geneterm·key identity values of ≥98.65% (see Fig. 3 in reference 14 ). Also, it is noted that there is a continuum of diversity at the genusterm·key level (e.g., as opposed to what is seen at the species level with genetic discontinuity) in addition to clustering (see Fig. 2 ), adding support to the idea proposed by Palmer et al. ( 39 ) that genetic continuum and genomic cohesiveness are not mutually exclusive, at least at the genusterm·key level.

The estimated genus inflection pointterm·dom serves as a practical guide for maximum AFterm and ANIterm values of a genus demarcation boundaryterm·dom and for the identification of a transition zone. Ideally, and assuming abundance of genomes of type strainsterm·dom and, consequently, data points, the estimated genus inflection pointterm·dom should closely match the genus demarcation boundaryterm·dom. As an example, the genus inflection pointterm·dom in Bacillusterm·key was estimated via the quartic function with AFterm of 0.430 and ANIterm of 73.57% ( Fig. 6a ). However, the majority of the non-type Bacillusterm·key spp. (99 out of 117 [85%]) fall below this inflection point at lower AFterm and ANIterm values, suggesting a potentially profound underestimation of genusterm·key diversity within the family Bacillaceae . An analysis of the pangenome of Bacillusterm·key using this estimated genus inflection pointterm·dom as a guide shows that only non-type Bacillusterm·key spp. above the genus inflection pointterm·dom cluster closely with the type speciesterm·dom B. subtilis (the primary reference), while B. anthracis and B. cereus , which are below both the estimated genus inflection pointterm·dom and genus demarcation boundaryterm·dom, cluster with type speciesterm·dom of other genera in Bacillaceae ( Fig. 7 ), corroborating, as pointed out above, that these two pathogens warrant reclassification and renaming. However, doing so would require considerable care so as not to raise objections over safety issues and a push for conservation of the current names over newly proposed names (see rule 56a in reference 5 and opinion 60 in reference 7 ). These results were further corroborated by phylogenomic analysis of Bacillaceae ( Fig. 7 ).

It is important to note that the genus inflection pointterm·dom is estimated based on nonlinear regression and only serves as a guide for future taxonomic designation. This is particularly important in the case of a new taxonomic designation with a genome displaying AFterm and ANIterm values that are right above the effective genus demarcation boundaryterm·dom; is it a novel type speciesterm·dom of a new genusterm·key or a novel non-type species of an existing genusterm·key? Having an estimated genus inflection pointterm·dom helps in making this decision. Whereas the genus demarcation boundaryterm·dom is a hard boundary, the genus inflection pointterm·dom represents a soft boundary that highlights a region where the rate of change is starting to decrease at the genome level. Toward higher ANIterm values, this rate then starts to increase, which would correspond to a species inflection point. Thus far, the genus inflection pointterm·dom could be properly estimated only for a few taxonomic groups due to a lack of genomes of type strainsterm·dom and/or actual isolates. Bacillusterm·key pumilus , Bacillusterm·key safensis , Bacillusterm·key altitudinis , Bacillusterm·key zhangzhouensis , Bacillusterm·key xiamenensis , and Bacillusterm·key australimaris are borderline species positioned within the 99% confidence interval of the estimated genus inflection pointterm·dom. Their 16S rRNA geneterm·key identities in relation to the primary reference range from 96.8% to 97.4%, suggesting that the estimated genus inflection pointterm·dom of Bacillusterm·key corresponds to a value that is much higher than the current genusterm·key threshold based on 16S rRNA geneterm·key identity (94.5%).

The taxonomic placements of species within Bacillusterm·key were also compared to a recent taxonomyterm·key that is based on highly conserved, protein-concatenated phylogeny (Genome Taxonomyterm·key Database [GTDB] [ 22 ]). There are currently >280 Bacillusterm·key spp. in the literature with validly published names, but <50% of these species have available sequenced genomes of their type strainsterm·dom. The MiSIterm method and GTDB taxonomyterm·key (accessed on 7 May 2019) indicate that there are 19 and 26 species within Bacillusterm·key , respectively ( Data Set S3A ). The 19 species placed within Bacillusterm·key by the MiSIterm method represent the type strainsterm·dom that are above the genus inflection pointterm·dom. However, if borderline species (i.e., within the confidence interval of the estimated genus inflection pointterm·dom) are also included, the number of species within Bacillusterm·key increases to 26. Xenorhabdus luminescens had been reclassified to the genusterm·key Photorhabdus , as P. luminescens ( 43 ), based in part on DNA-DNA hybridizationterm analysis, even though this method is not necessarily suitable for genusterm·key demarcation. Separate phylogenetic analyses of both Xenorhabdus and Photorhabdus spp. indicated that in most cases, P. luminescens grouped within the Xenorhabdus cluster ( 44 , 45 ). Now that the genome of P. luminescens has been sequenced, it is evident that it contains multiple 16S rRNA genesterm·key ranging in identity from 99 to 100%. The last case we will analyze is the known polyphyletic Lactobacillusterm·key , a genusterm·key relevant to many subfields of microbiology, including food and public-health microbiology. Lactobacillusterm·key sensu lato has been proposed to have 24 phylogroups ( 47 ). More recently, Lactobacillusterm·key was proposed to contain 10 phylogroups ( 48 ). Analysis of Lactobacillusterm·key following the approach herein presented, using L. delbrueckii delbrueckii as a primary reference, resulted in the identification of the genus demarcation boundaryterm·dom (AFterm, 0.075; ANIterm, 72.80%) and estimated genus inflection pointterm·dom (AFterm, 0.349; ANIterm, 74.71%; Fig. 9 ). Significant differences were seen between non-type species of Lactobacillusterm·key and type speciesterm·dom of Lactobacillales in terms of AFterm ( P < 0.0001) but not ANIterm ( P = 0.1624), already highlighting profound taxonomic issues in this genusterm·key. Indeed, most of the Lactobacillusterm·key spp. (186 out of 193 species considered in this study) have AFterm and ANIterm values that fall below the estimated genus inflection pointterm·dom. Besides L. delbrueckii and its subspecies, only one other species is above the estimated genus inflection pointterm·dom, Lactobacillusterm·key equicursoris . Implications.

The approach we have used to demarcate genera is complementary and does not replace the conventional polyphasic method of circumscribing a genusterm·key, which includes thorough analyses of the full-length 16S rRNA geneterm·key, phylogeny, physiology, and metabolism, among other aspects. The AFterm and ANIterm boundary values for genusterm·key demarcation in a given family or order can be refined as the genomes of more type strainsterm·dom and type speciesterm·dom are sequenced and become publicly available. This approach emphasizes the importance of type strainsterm·dom and type speciesterm·dom in the continuous reevaluation of bacterial and archaeal taxa using genomic indices (e.g., ANIterm or AFterm) because these specimens function as reference points for their respective taxonomic groups and remain available in viable form from multiple public repositories, thus making the approach reproducible, replicable, robust, and generalizable ( 53 ). In this respect, our approach is in line with recommendations regarding taxonomyterm·key, including relevant comparisons to type strainsterm·dom and type speciesterm·dom for the characterization of novel Bacteria and Archaea ( 8 ). It is reiterated that any rearrangement or reclassification of taxa should be in alignment with the ICNP ( 7 ). In particular, a potential rearrangement of type speciesterm·dom must be carefully analyzed, as a genusterm·key can only contain one type speciesterm·dom, and rules and principles of nomenclature must be followed in order to properly do that.

Uncultivated Bacteria and Archaea represented by metagenomic assembled genomes (MAGs) and/or single-cell amplified genomes (SAGs) could potentially be used in the implementation of this approach; however, it is noted that type strainsterm·dom and type speciesterm·dom should be used as references for such analyses, if genomes are available (see “Guidelines,” below). The designation of type strainsterm·dom and type speciesterm·dom is governed by the ICNP and requires a thorough characterization of the microorganism and the deposit of the type strainterm·dom in at least two different culture collections in two different countries. Naturally, the proper characterization, and therefore, the type designation, is in most cases impossible for uncultivated Bacteria and Archaea given the current guidelines set by the ICNP. This could change if genomes are subsequently considered type material, as recently proposed ( 54 ) and discussed ( 55 ). In lieu of type strainsterm·dom and type speciesterm·dom, proper and consistent genomic references must be established in order to analyze taxonomic groups without cultivated representatives as well as rigorous standards for sequence quality. Caution is advised in the interpretation of data resulting from low-quality MAGs since they can represent composite genomes of different strains ( 56 ). In theory, SAGs could serve as good taxonomic frames of reference for uncultivated genera; however, genome incompleteness in SAGs is an issue that would need to be addressed.

The MiSIterm method explicitly excludes tRNA and rRNA genes in order to avoid inflation of AFterm or ANIterm values. However, the current taxonomic framework is largely based on the use of the 16S rRNA geneterm·key for classification. Therefore, the approach herein investigated is not completely independent of the 16S rRNA geneterm·key. The fact that in the majority of the groups tested, with the exception of known polyphyletic genera such as Bacillusterm·key (although significant differences between type and non-type species were still detected in these groups), there was strong concordance of the type speciesterm·dom of genera being delineated from non-type species of the primary reference provides evidence to the success of the use of the 16S rRNA geneterm·key within the taxonomic framework, as far as precision is concerned (i.e., not accuracy). The 16S rRNA geneterm·key has served and could continue to serve the scientific community well, especially in maintaining relative consistency in classifications. However, the estimation of the genus inflection pointterm·dom indicates that the 16S rRNA geneterm·key identity minimum threshold value of 94.5% could underestimate genera diversity in some taxa (e.g., Bacillaceae [ Bacillusterm·key ]) and overestimate it in others (e.g., Enterobacterales [ Photorhabdus ]), especially if multiple nonidentical 16S rRNA genesterm·key are present in the genomes. This uncertainty in delineating genera using the 16S rRNA geneterm·key has also been shown by Yarza et al. ( 3 ), considering that the minimum and median identity values for delineation differ by nearly 2%, at 94.5% and 96.4%, respectively. It is clear that different taxonomic groups are characterized by different AFterm and ANIterm threshold values for genusterm·key demarcation. However, even within established taxonomic groups, the effective AFterm and ANIterm threshold values will still be “moving targets,” dependent on the assignment and reassignment of new type speciesterm·dom. Thus, a single, universal, stationary threshold for genusterm·key delineation will not be able to sensitively resolve genusterm·key assignments for all taxa.

The reader is made aware that variations in bidirectional, matched-pair AFterm values (AF1-2 and AF2-1) exist. These variations are usually small but could be relevant in the interpretation of data, especially in borderline cases. Bidirectional, matched-pair ANIterm values (ANI1-2 and ANI2-1) differ by <0.3% in the majority of the cases, which would make the error bars invisible to the naked eye. The use of ANIterm has been previously stated as not applicable for the demarcation of genera ( 14 , 18 , 19 , 57 ). Our results indicate that the MiSIterm method, and therefore AFterm and ANIterm, can be used to visualize natural breakpoints that can be used to circumscribe genera with objectivity, reproducibility, and high resolution if the guidelines presented in this study are followed. Our study identified potentially misclassified species in numerous genera that could not be previously resolved by alternative methods, including DNA-DNA hybridizationterm, 16S rRNA gene-based phylogeny, and phylogeny using concatenated highly conserved proteins. Adaptation of this method to demarcate higher taxonomic ranks has not been tested, as it is beyond the scope of this study. Nonetheless, such an approach would be warranted. Finally, we highlight that the results demonstrate a conserved genomic coherence at the genusterm·key level for numerous different taxa, shedding light on a fundamental emergent property of Bacteria and Archaea .

MATERIALS AND METHODS ANIterm and AFterm.

ANIterm and alignment fractionterm (AFterm) values were obtained by the Microbial Species Identifierterm (MiSIterm) method using ANIcalculator 2014-127, version 1.0 ( https://aniterm.jgi.doe.gov/html/home.php?page=introduction ) ( 17 ), and also as temporarily implemented in the JGI-Integrated Microbial Genomes and Microbiomes (IMG/M) system ( https://img.jgi.doe.gov/ ) via the Pairwise ANIterm tool (accessed 2017 to 2018). ANIterm, as defined by Varghese et al. ( 17 ), is calculated for a pair of genomes by averaging the nucleotide identity of orthologous genes identified as bidirectional best hits (BBHs), which are the genes that show ≥70% sequence identity and ≥70% alignment of the shorter gene. AFterm, as defined by Varghese et al. ( 17 ), is calculated as a fraction of the sum of the lengths of BBH genes divided by the sum of the lengths of all genes in a genome.

Strains.

Unless otherwise noted, only type strainsterm·dom were used in this study. The NamesforLife Database (NamesforLife, LLC, East Lansing, MI [ 58 ]) was primarily used to retrieve nomenclature (current up to 27 October 2019), nomenclatural history, and taxonomic information about validly published type strainsterm·dom and type speciesterm·dom associated with different genera. Complementary to this, equivalent strain numbers assigned by different biological resource centers (e.g., the German Collection of Microorganism and Cell Cultures [DSMZ]) were searched in their respective online catalogues. In addition to what is mentioned above, primary taxonomic literature sources were used to confirm some of these designations. All strain designations were cross-referenced in at least two databases.

Genomes and 16S rRNA geneterm·key sequences.

Publicly available genomes were obtained from IMG and the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ ). Generally, taxonomic orders/families that have genomes of ≥4 type speciesterm·dom of genera and ≥4 non-type species in a given genusterm·key were considered. Genomes that were unanimously flagged by both IMG and the NCBI as low quality were removed from the data set. About 36% of the genomes included in this study were sequenced as part of the JGI-Genomic Encyclopedia of Bacteria and Archaea , a project that focuses on sequencing the genomes of type strainsterm·dom ( 5 , 59 ). Sequences of the 16S rRNA geneterm·key were obtained from either IMG, the NCBI, or EZBioCloud ( 60 ; https://www.ezbiocloud.net/ ). Alignments were separately performed using the SILVA incremental aligner version 1.2.11 ( 61 ; https://www.arb-silva.de/aligner/ ) and ClustalW via the Geneious platform (version R6; Biomatters, Auckland, New Zealand). Genetic distances were calculated in Geneious. PhyML ( 62 ) was used via the Geneious platform to generate the maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree with the following settings: Hasegawa-Kishino-Yano (HKY85) substitution model, 1,000 bootstraps, estimated transition/transversion ratio, estimated proportion of invariable sites, estimated gamma distribution, and branch lengths and substitution rate optimized.

Pangenome analysis.

Pangenome analysis of Bacillusterm·key and other genera in Bacillaceae were processed in anvi’o (version 5.5) ( 63 ) following the workflow for microbial pangenomics ( http://merenlab.org/2016/11/08/pangenomics-v2/ , last accessed 13 May 2019 [ 64 ]). In brief, we generated contig databases for each genome contig file using the command “anvi-gen-contigs-database.” Prodigal ( 65 ) was used to identify open reading frames, and subsequently, each database was populated with HMM profiles by comparison to a collection of single-copy genes using HMMER ( 66 ). Once contig databases were generated for all genomes, we used “anvi-gen-genomes-storage” to generate a master genome storage database to use in the pangenome analysis. We used the NCBI-BLAST option in “anvi-pan-genome” to calculate gene similarity and MCL ( 67 ) for clustering under the following settings: minbit, 0.5; mcl inflation, 2; and minimum occurrence, 2. For phylogenomic analysis of Bacillusterm·key genomes, we selected a subset of 21 genes based on the following criteria: minimum number of genomes in which gene cluster occurs, 35; maximum number of genes from each genome, 1; maximum functional homogeneity index, 0.9; and minimum geometric homogeneity index, 0.99. We then used the anvi’o command “anvi-get-sequences-for-gene-clusters” to concatenate and align target genes from all genomes and “anvi-gen-phylogenomic-tree” to generate the phylogenomic tree from the concatenated FASTA file. The tree was then rerooted using other genera of Bacillaceae as the outgroup.

Statistical analysis.

All bidirectional, matched-pair values of ANIterm (ANI1-2 and ANI2-1) and AFterm (AF1-2 and AF2-1) were reported as single averaged ANIterm and AFterm values in this study. The nonparametric Wilcoxon test was performed separately for each set of ANIterm and AFterm values to determine significant ( P < 0.05) differences between type and non-type species. All statistical analyses were performed using the statistical software JMP Pro, version 14 (SAS Institute, Inc.).

Rationale.

The nomenclatural type or “type” is defined by the International Code of Nomenclature of Prokaryotes (ICNP) as “that element of the taxon with which the name is permanently associated …” (rule 15 [ 7 ]). The type strainterm·dom acts as the single reference for a given species. Each species and subspecies with a validly published name has a type strainterm·dom that is designated at the time the name is proposed. The vast majority (>98%) are represented by one or more viable deposits that are descended from the original type strainterm·dom, are maintained in pure culture, agree closely with its character in the original description, and are available in one or more public culture collections (rules 18a and 30 [ 7 ]). A type speciesterm·dom, which is represented by its type strainterm·dom, acts as the single reference for a given genusterm·key. Type strainsterm·dom and type speciesterm·dom may not necessarily be the most representative members of a species or genusterm·key, respectively. Rather, type strainsterm·dom and type speciesterm·dom represent the first and often only member of the respective taxa, based on the opinion of the individual proposing each name. The rationale used in this study is that sister species in a given genusterm·key should be relatively similar to the type speciesterm·dom of the cognate genusterm·key, indicating high similarity at the genome level. Therefore, when a type speciesterm·dom of a genusterm·key is compared to a non-type species within the same genusterm·key, the AFterm and ANIterm values should be relatively high. In contrast to this, type speciesterm·dom of different sister genera (i.e., in the same taxonomic family or order) should be relatively dissimilar at the genome level. This dissimilarity should be reflected in relatively low AFterm and ANIterm values.

Guidelines.

In addition to the genome(s) under consideration (e.g., of a cultured strain that needs classification), at least two genomes of type speciesterm·dom in the same family or order are needed, as follows: (i) the primary reference which is the phylogenetically closest relative that is a type speciesterm·dom (i.e., the type speciesterm·dom to which all other microbes will be compared), and (ii) all other type speciesterm·dom (≥1) in the same family or order. Furthermore, all available genomes of non-type species that belong to the same genusterm·key of the primary reference are needed. In order for the results to be meaningful, a species must only be represented by the type strainterm·dom unless its genome is not available, in which case a non-type strain of the same species that is ≥99% identical in 16S rRNA geneterm·key sequence(s) (i.e., all copies of 16S rRNA geneterm·key in the genome) may be used as a proxy until a genome becomes available. The accession numbers for these genomes should be reported. The genus demarcation boundaryterm·dom is set by the highest AFterm and ANIterm values of a type speciesterm·dom (i.e., other than the primary reference) in an order/family.

Interpretation.

Every data point in the AFterm and ANIterm plot is calculated in relation to the primary reference, which is defined above as the type strainterm·dom of the type speciesterm·dom of the specific genusterm·key to be analyzed; therefore, the plot represents genomic similarity to the reference genome. If the primary reference is compared against an identical genome (e.g., or a direct comparison against itself), an AFterm value of 1 and ANIterm value of 100 would result. If the primary reference is compared against relatively dissimilar genomes, the AFterm and ANIterm values will reflect proportionally lower values. Thus, the plots that are generated effectively illustrate how distant other data points are from the primary reference genome in terms of AFterm and ANIterm values, or in other words, how distant other data points are from the upper-right corner (AFterm, 1; ANIterm, 100%).

When all comparisons are made in relation to the primary reference, a type speciesterm·dom (i.e., other than the primary reference) should always cluster with other type speciesterm·dom of genera in the same taxonomic family or order, reflecting relatively low AFterm and ANIterm values. A non-type species should always cluster with other non-type species of the same genusterm·key, reflecting relatively high AFterm and ANIterm values. The distinct clustering of type speciesterm·dom and non-type species should be conserved without any overlap. If a non-type species is positioned within the cluster of type speciesterm·dom in the same taxonomic family or order (i.e., at or below the genus demarcation boundaryterm·dom), this indicates that the non-type species could potentially be assigned to a different genusterm·key, as it is as genomically different from the primary reference, as are other type speciesterm·dom in the same taxonomic family or order. If a type speciesterm·dom is positioned within the cluster of non-type species, there is support for potential reclassification of the type speciesterm·dom within the same genusterm·key of the primary reference, given the accumulation of other supporting evidence (e.g., 16S rRNA geneterm·key identity or phylogeny). It is emphasized that any proposed taxonomic rearrangement or reclassification based on AFterm and ANIterm results will necessarily affect nomenclature; therefore, any corresponding changes in nomenclature resulting from such proposal must be in alignment with the ICNP ( 7 ).
