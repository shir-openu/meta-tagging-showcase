findingMTL ensemble activity changed gradually over tens of seconds and this drift was causally tied to which stimulus was shown, not to recording artifact. — “The ensemble response changed gradually over tens of seconds and was causally affected by the identity of the stimulus presented.”
findingRepeating a stimulus recovered ensemble activity that had been present before its original presentation — a reliable backward contiguity effect. — “The contiguity analysis showed a statistically reliable backward effect ( Figure 4c ) confirming a prediction of models that postulate that the contiguity effect results from a jump back in time of a neural representation.”
findingEnsemble similarity also fell off in the forward direction, which is consistent with either short-term memory or retrieved-context accounts. — “There was a significant decrease in similarity as a function of |lag| in the forward direction, .008 ± .003, F (1, 9) = 7.04, p < .03, consistent with both short-term memory and retrieved context accounts.”
findingMTL ensembles carried category information above and beyond stimulus identity, independent of recency. — “The ensemble similarity between same-category pairs was greater than for different-category pairs, t(56) = 13.9, p < .001.”
findingThe temporally autocorrelated subset of units did not itself show robust individual-stimulus coding, although it did show category specificity. — “Robust stimulus coding was not observed using this method in the temporally-autocorrelated part of the ensemble, t(15) = 0.46.”
findingThe contiguity analysis rules out spike-sorting or environmental artifacts as the sole source of the observed temporal autocorrelation. — “Although artifacts may exist, the contiguity analysis provides very strong evidence against the hypothesis that all of the temporal autocorrelation in ensemble activity is due to artifactual sources.”
findingTHE RIVALS SHARE TWO PREDICTIONS BY CONSTRUCTION: both require a gradually changing state caused by the presented stimuli — “If it is physically implemented, the retrieved temporal context approach requires that the state of the memory system should change gradually over time and that these changes should be caused by the stimuli presented. These predictions are shared with short-term memory models.”
findingAND DIFFER IN ONE ASYMMETRY: short-term memory predicts resemblance only to states following the original presentation, a jump back in time predicts both directions — “The unique predictions of the retrieved temporal context approach are manifest when one considers the comparison between the neural response caused by a repeated stimulus and the neighbors of its original presentation. If a gradually-changing memory state were a signature of a short-term memory that contains stimuli, then the input caused by the repeated stimulus would resemble the states of the short-term memory following its original presentation, when that stimulus was still active in short-term memory. The overlap between the repeated state should decrease going forward from the original presentation as that stimulus decays in short-term memory. However, if repeating a stimulus causes a jump back in time then the state caused by the repeated stimulus will be similar to neighboring states in both the forward and backward directions.”
findingso the signature sought is a repeated stimulus recovering activity present BEFORE its original presentation — “That is, the signature of the jump back in time is that a repeated stimulus recovers activity that was present prior to the original presentation of the stimulus.”
findinglag one regression coefficients were above zero overall and about a quarter of units qualified as temporally autocorrelated — “The distribution of lag one regression coefficients across units was significantly greater than zero, mean .094 ± .01, t(269) = 9.31, p < .001 (see Figure 3a ). We refer to units that had a lag one regression coefficient significantly greater than zero (p < .05) as temporally-autocorrelated units. Almost one quarter of all units recorded (.248) were classified as temporally-autocorrelated units.”
findingautocorrelated units fired above baseline BEFORE the stimulus appeared, so they were not simply responding to it — “Autocorrelated units showed elevated firing in the time prior to stimulus presentation suggesting they were not simply responding to the just-presented stimulus.”
findinghippocampus, parahippocampal region and entorhinal cortex all far exceeded the Type I error rate; the amygdala did not — “The proportion of temporally-autocorrelated units in the hippocampus, parahippocampal region, and entorhinal cortex far exceeded the Type I error rate (.05) and did not reliably differ among those regions, χ 2 (2) = 4.87, p > .05 (see Table 2 ). The number of units in the amygdala classified as temporally-autocorrelated did not exceed the Type I error rate.”
findingBLOCK STRUCTURE REMOVED BY RESTRICTION: all comparisons confined to pairs of events within one block, which are necessarily same-category — “To isolate temporal autocorrelation from the effects of stimulus category and block (which were also autocorrelated in time due to the structure of the experiment) we restricted our attention to pairs of events within the same block.”
findingA LOWER BOUND ON THE TIMESCALE: excluding the eight nearest recencies still left a significant slope, so the ensemble kept changing past 27 seconds — “The change over time was not simply due to an elevated level of ensemble similarity for adjacent events. Because the similarity continued to decrease after recency −9, this means that the ensemble continued changing reliably after nine intervening stimuli, or a delay of more than 27 s.”
findingCATEGORY AND RECENCY SEPARATED BY MATCHING: comparisons made across pairs of similarities at the same recency — “Because of the blocked structure of this experiment, the category of the stimulus is confounded with the event number. In order to isolate the effects of stimulus and category in relative isolation of recency, ensemble similarities were aggregated as a function of recency separately for a) comparisons between presentations of the same stimulus, b) pairs in which both stimuli were from the same category (faces or scenes) but were different stimuli, and c) pairs in which the stimuli were from different categories. findingONE ANALYSIS SERVING TWO PURPOSES: the contiguity analysis both diagnoses a causal link to the stimulus and looks for the jump back in time — “The analysis allows us to diagnose a causal relationship between the stimulus presented and the gradually-changing response, ruling out artifactual accounts of the temporal autocorrelation. This artifact, however, could not account for the stimulus-specificity, sensitivity of the ensemble to the block structure, or the fact that the autocorrelated signal retains information about preceding stimuli.”
findingSORTING ERROR TWO ANSWERED: merging neurons could fake conjunctions, but not a response to the neighbours of a particular stimulus's original presentation — “In the other type of error, spikes from multiple neurons are counted as belonging to one unit.
