Methods Study site Our study site is located at the Haibei Alpine Grasslandterm·key Ecosystem Research Station (101°12′E, 37°37′N, and 3,200 m above sea level) on the northeastern Tibetan Plateauterm·key in Qinghai Province, China. This area has a continental monsoon climate, with a 6-month growing season (from mid-April to mid-October). The mean annual temperature is −1.1 °C, with an extreme maximum air temperature of 27.6 °C in July, and an extreme minimum temperature of −37.1 °C in January. The mean annual precipitationterm·key is 485 mm, with >80% of the precipitationterm·key falling in the growing season. The soil is classified as Mat-Gryic Cambisol, with the average pH value of surface soil (0–10 cm) being 6.4 (refs 59 , 60 ). The alpine grasslandterm·key is dominated by perennial plants, including S. aliena , E. nutans , H. tibeticum , Kobresia capillifolia , Carex przewalskii , Poa annua , M. archiducis-nicolai , Tibetia ruthenia , Gentiana straminea and Saussurea superba . These species together account for ∼70% of aboveground net primary production (g m −2 ).

Experimental design Our experimental plots were established within an area of 50 m × 110 m in 2011, using a randomized block design with warmingterm·dom and altered precipitationterm·key as main treatment factors ( Supplementary Fig. 1a ). Each block contained six treatments, crossing two levels of warmingterm·dom (no warmingterm·dom, warmingterm·dom) and three levels of precipitationterm·key (no precipitationterm·key, drought (50% precipitationterm·key reduction) and wet (50% precipitationterm·key addition)). Each treatment had five replicates, resulting in 30 plots; each plot was 1.8 m × 2.2 m. In the warmingterm·dom treatments, two infrared heaters (1,000 mm length, 22 mm width) were suspended in parallel at 150 cm above the ground within each plot ( Supplementary Fig. 1b ), with an electrical power output of 1,200 W for each heater; the heaters resulted in an increase of 2 °C above ambient temperature at the top 5 cm layer of the soil 61 . Rain shelters were used to control the incoming precipitationterm·key amount in the experimental plots. Four ‘V'-shaped transparent polycarbonate resin channels (Teijin Chemical, Japan) were fixed at the 15° angle, above the infrared heaters, to intercept rainfall. The collected rainfall from the drought plots was supplied to the wet plots manually after each precipitationterm·key event by spraying bottle ( Supplementary Fig. 1c ). To account for the effects of shading, we also installed two ‘dummy' infrared heaters and four ‘dummy' transparent polycarbonate resin channels in the control plots. Stainless steel sheets were inserted into the soil around the edge of each plot to reduce surface runoff.

Plant community monitoring To estimate the biomassterm·key of community and individual species, three 0.15 m × 0.15 m quadrats were randomly chosen within each plot, and clipped at the ground level in late August (the peak of growing season) from 2011 to 2015. Plants clipped from the three quadrats of each plot were pooled together, sorted to species (from 2012 to 2015), and oven-dried at 65 °C for 48 h. Plants were classified into three different groups (dominant, common and rare species) according to their relative abundance and four functional groups (grasses, sedges, legumes and forbs) based on their functional forms. Dominant speciesterm·key included those with relative abundance >5%, common species ranged from 1 to 5% in relative abundance and rare species were those with relative abundance <1% (refs 62 , 63 , 64 ). The three groups consisted of 2, 16 and 35 species, and accounted for 52.3%, 35.9% and 11.9% of community biomassterm·key, respectively.
