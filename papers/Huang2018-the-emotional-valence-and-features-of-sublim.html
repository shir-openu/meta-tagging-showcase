The emotional valence and features of subliminal priming images impact conscious perception of face expressions — TEI-tagged view

content-tags & facets: phenomenon · claim-type · cross-domain-transfer · mechanism · claim-relation · replication-status · limitation — grounded in the full text
phenomenonsubliminal affective priming of facial expression perception
claim-typeexistence
mechanismfaces in the prime are processed preferentially, and the perceived prime face then competes with and interferes with perception of the target face — “This suggest that processing of faces occurs preferentially to the processing of non-face containing images, and that the face perceived in the priming image interferes with the ability to perceive the target-face expression.”
mechanisma face in the prime sets up an expectation of an emotionally charged target, which speeds and biases the response toward 'angry' — “Taken together these results suggest that the face in the prime image prompts the viewer to expect an emotionally charged target-face, which quickens (see Supplement D) and biases the response towards the angry rather than neutral-target face.”
mechanisman egocentric vantage-point recruits action-preparation processes, which lower the priority given to affect processing and so degrade angry-face recognition — “From this result, we suggest that, because of the direct relevance of the egocentric vantage-point to the viewer, the egocentric prime image may engage a series of processes relevant to potential action preparation, which could weaken the priority of affective processing, and thus interfere with the ability to recognize the angry target-face expression.”
relation — “Using the same experimental protocol as in our previous study (Vaina et al., 2014), here we investigate in young healthy subjects how the affective content of the priming image, the presence of a face or the vantage-point of the prime content (image centricity) impact the effect of affective subliminal priming on the identification of target-face expressions.”
relation — “This result is at odds with the findings of Andrews et al. (2011), who reported that there was no subliminal affective priming effect when priming with emotional facial expressions (Andrews, Lipp, Malan, & Könjg, 2011).”
relation — “This result is in agreement with Kemps, Erauw, and Vandierendock (1996) finding that facial expressions influenced the liking rating of Korean ideographs at exposure duration of 30 ms and longer, while pictures without faces did not elicit an affective reaction until they were presented for at least 100 ms.”
replicationuntested
limitationThe design cannot separate the emotion of the depicted scene from the emotion of the facial expression within the prime; the authors state they will add further prime dimensions to do so. — “We will extend this study by adding additional dimensions to the priming stimuli, to dissociate between the emotion of the scene and that of the facial expressions.”

content-observations: finding · quantity · variable · definition · key-term — grounded in the full text
findingPriming images that contained a face impaired subsequent identification of the target face's expression relative to primes without a face. — “Priming images containing a face compared to those not containing a face significantly impaired performance on neutral or angry targetface evaluation.”
findingPleasant primes containing a face selectively damaged recognition of angry target-face expressions. — “Recognition of angry target-face expressions was selectively impaired by pleasant prime images which contained a face.”
findingAfter an egocentric prime, neutral target expressions were recognised significantly better than angry ones. — “For egocentric primes, recognition of neutral target-face expressions was significantly better than of angry expressions.”
findingAmong face-containing primes, neutral ones produced better target-face performance than either aggressive or pleasant ones. — “Subjects performed better on perceiving target-face expressions when the affective value of the face-containing prime image was neutral compared to aggressive or pleasant (neutral vs. aggressive: WRS, z = 2.060, p = 0.039) (neutral vs. pleasant: WRS, z = 1.966, p = 0.049).”
findingThe authors conclude that the affective primacy hypothesis survives in subliminal priming only in the special case where the prime image contains a face. — “Our results suggest that, first, the affective primacy hypothesis which predicts that affective information can be accessed automatically, preceding conscious cognition, holds true in subliminal priming only when the priming image contains a face.”
findingThe face-versus-non-face prime difference reached significance only for aggressive prime content. — “was significant only when the affective content of the prime image was aggressive (aggressive, face: μ = 73.4%, se = 1.99%; aggressive, non-face: μ = 81.0%, se = 1.85%) (WRS, z = -3.835, p = 0.001).”
quantity16 ms — exposure duration of the subliminal prime image, followed by a 100 ms mask — “Next, a priming image, randomly selected from the experimental database, was shown for 16 ms and immediately followed by a mask displayed for 100 ms.”
quantityThirty-three subjects — number of participants, with their sex split and mean age — “Thirty-three subjects (17 females, mean age = 18.17 years, SD ± 1.49) participated in the study.”
quantity120 trials — total number of trials in the SAFFIMAP test — “The next trial began immediately after the subject’s response, or after 4000 ms, if no response was entered. There were in total 120 trials”
quantity74.20% vs 78.00% — mean accuracy on target-face evaluation after face primes versus non-face primes — “In trials where primes contained a face, subjects were poorer at correctly identifying the emotion expressed in the target face, compared to when the prime image did not contain a face. (face: μ = 74.20%, se = 1.79%; non-face: μ = 78.00%, se = 1.49%) (WRS, z=-2.266, p = 0.023)”
quantity73.4% vs 81.0% — mean accuracy after aggressive primes with a face versus aggressive primes without a face — “was significant only when the affective content of the prime image was aggressive (aggressive, face: μ = 73.4%, se = 1.99%; aggressive, non-face: μ = 81.0%, se = 1.85%) (WRS, z = -3.835, p = 0.001).”
quantity81.64% vs 77.31% — mean accuracy on neutral target faces after egocentric versus allocentric primes — “egocentric compared to when it was allocentric (egocentric: μ = 81.64%, se = 2.03%; allocentric: μ = 77.31%, se = 2.05%) (WRS, z = -2.215, p = 0.017).”
variableaffective category of the priming image (aggressive, pleasant, neutral) (independent) — “The priming images used in this experiment were categorised by their affect (aggressive, pleasant, or neutral), and further sub-categorised by the vantage-point of the image content (egocentric or allocentric) and the presence or absence of a face.”
variablepresence or absence of a face in the priming image (independent) — “We applied a General Linear Mixture Model (GLMM) to model the effect of the priming categories (affective category, face content (face present vs. face absent), and centricity) and the target-face (angry vs. neutral) on the evaluation of the target-face expression via a probit-link function, due to the binary response variable (correct vs. incorrect).”
variablecentricity (vantage-point) of the priming image (independent) — “The priming images were broadly categorised as aggressive, pleasant, or neutral and further subcategorised by the presence of a face and by the centricity (egocentric or allocentric vantage-point) of the image content.”

This is referred to as the congruency effect.”
