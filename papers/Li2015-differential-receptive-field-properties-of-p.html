mechanismPV neurons pool a wider range of excitatory inputs and receive stronger subthreshold drive, so more of that input crosses spike threshold, producing broad, weakly selective spike tuning — “The broader/weaker frequency tuning of PV neurons was attributed to a broader range of synaptic inputs and stronger subthreshold responses elicited, which resulted in a higher efficiency in the conversion of input to output.”

findingParvalbumin (PV) inhibitory neurons in layer 2/3 auditory cortex have broader tonal receptive fields, lower intensity thresholds and stronger tone-evoked spiking than somatostatin (SOM) neurons. — “We found that PV cells exhibited broader tonal receptive fields with lower intensity thresholds and stronger tone-evoked spike responses compared with SOM neurons.”
findingSOM neurons are as sharply frequency-tuned as excitatory neurons even though they too receive a broad range of excitatory input. — “SOM neurons, although also receive a broad range of inputs, display similar frequency selectivity as excitatory neurons.”
findingBoth the synaptic input onset and the spike onset of SOM neurons occur significantly later than those of PV and excitatory neurons. — “In addition, onsets of both the input and spike responses of SOM neurons were significantly delayed compared with PV and excitatory cells.”
findingMeasured 10 dB above threshold, PV receptive fields were twice as broad as those of SOM and excitatory neurons, which did not differ from each other. — “On average, PV neurons had TRFs twice as broad as those of SOM and excitatory neurons, while the latter 2 did not differ in their TRF bandwidths”
findingSOM neurons required significantly louder tones to respond than PV or excitatory neurons, implying they cannot supply inhibition at low sound intensities. — “The intensity threshold of SOM neuron TRFs was significantly higher than PV and excitatory neurons”
findingSOM neurons fired with significantly less precise timing, showing larger first-spike jitter than PV and excitatory neurons. — “SOM neurons had significantly larger jitters of first-spike timing compared with PV and excitatory neurons”
findingPV neurons converted almost their whole synaptic input range into spike output (highest spike-range/input-range ratio), whereas SOM neurons converted the least. — “Overall, the ratio of spike tuning range over input range was the highest for PV neurons, and lowest for SOM neurons”
findingTone-evoked subthreshold depolarizations (EPSPs) were significantly larger in PV neurons than in SOM or excitatory neurons. — “Notably, the amplitude of EPSPs was significantly larger in PV than SOM and excitatory neurons, as shown by the rightward shift of the cumulative distribution of EPSP amplitudes”
findingEvery recorded PV cell had a spike trough-to-peak interval below 0.5 ms while every recorded excitatory cell was above 0.5 ms, so waveform width separates the two classes in layer 2/3. — “Similarly, we found that spike waveforms of all the recorded PV cells exhibited a trough-to-peak interval (TPI) shorter than 0.5 ms, while those of all the recorded excitatory neurons had TPIs longer than 0.5 ms”
findingPV neurons fired spontaneously at a significantly higher rate than excitatory cells. — “the spontaneous firing rate of PV neurons was significantly higher than excitatory cells and tended to be higher than SOM neurons”
findingThe three cell groups were recorded at statistically indistinguishable cortical depths, so the broad PV tuning is not a depth-sampling artefact. — “Finally, the 3 cell groups did not differ significantly in their distributions of cortical depths”
findingPV receptive fields were about twice as broad as SOM and excitatory ones, which did not differ from each other — “On average, PV neurons had TRFs twice as broad as those of SOM and excitatory neurons, while the latter 2 did not differ in their TRF bandwidths”
findingthe same result held for a second bandwidth measure taken from an envelope fit — “Again, BW50% of PV cells was at least twice as broad as those of SOM and excitatory neurons (Fig. 4 C ). These 2 measures of frequency tuning all indicated that PV neurons were less frequency-selective than SOM and excitatory neurons. findingPV cells fired much faster to tones than SOM or excitatory cells, and had higher spontaneous rates — “As for response level, PV neurons displayed much higher tone-evoked firing rates than SOM and excitatory cells (Fig. 2 C ). In addition, the spontaneous firing rate of PV neurons was significantly higher than excitatory cells and tended to be higher than SOM neurons”
findingSOM cells had higher intensity thresholds, so they may not inhibit at low sound levels — “The intensity threshold of SOM neuron TRFs was significantly higher than PV and excitatory neurons (Fig. 4 D ), suggesting that SOM neurons may not be able to provide inhibition under low-intensity stimuli.”
findingSOM cells had larger first-spike jitter than PV or excitatory cells — “In addition, we calculated jitters of response timing from repetitions of CF-tone-evoked spike responses as the standard deviation of first-spike timing. SOM neurons had significantly larger jitters of first-spike timing compared with PV and excitatory neurons”
findingspikes occur in the order PV, excitatory, SOM, with SOM markedly delayed — “Spike responses of these neurons are evoked in a PV-excitatory-SOM neuron sequence, with SOM neuron responses markedly delayed relative to PV and excitatory neurons.”

findingthree candidate causes remain for the disagreement: indirect optogenetic versus direct visual targeting, contamination in tungsten electrode recording, and general versus premapped A1 — “The discrepancy between Moore and Wehr study and our current result may be due to different targeting methods (optogenetics aided indirect targeting vs. visually guided direct targeting). One concern about the tungsten electrode recording approach as in Moore and Wehr study is the potential contaminations from other types of units of nearby neurons. This potential problem perhaps can be prevented by coupling cell-attached loose-patch recordings with optogenetic identification, as cell-attached recording is more reliable than single-unit recording in faithfully picking up spike signals only from the patched individual neuron ( Wu et al. 2008 , 2011 ; Zhou et al. 2012 ).
