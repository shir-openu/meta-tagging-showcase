Abstract

Author summary

Correctly perceiving behaviorally significant sounds—speechterm, music, and the acoustic environment—requires integrating acoustic information over time to extract relevant regularities. A fundamental question about this process is: How does the auditoryterm brain integrate information of continuously varying sounds, typical of many natural auditoryterm signals? How does the brain “sample” the input? To investigate this question, we measured how cortical activity is entrained by soundterm using the noninvasive technique magnetoencephalography. We presented sounds with temporal structure at different timescales and examined how the brain encodes such signals. Introduction

Speechterm, music, and many natural sounds have a rich temporal structure over multiple timescales [ 1 – 5 ]; such sounds contain perceptually critical information that is encoded over short periods (e.g., the identity and exact sequence of phonemes in a spoken word) and, concurrently, information encoded over longer periods (e.g., the intonation change over a word that signals intent or affect). Successful perceptual analysis of these signals requires the auditoryterm system to extract acoustic information at multiple scales. This presents a specific problem: how does the auditoryterm system process different, co-occurring rates of information across multiple timescales? And, by extension, how can the requirements on temporal and spectral resolution simultaneously be met? To derive the appropriate perceptual representations, the auditoryterm system must extract rapidly varying information on a scale of milliseconds (approximately 10–50 ms), operating with high temporal resolution, and concurrently analyze more slowly varying signal attributes on a scale of hundreds of milliseconds (about 150–300 ms), enabling sufficient spectral resolution [ 6 ]. A strictly hierarchical model, starting, say, with short/small integration windows at more peripheral processing regions, which are then concatenated to build longer windows, cannot by itself meet the perceptual demands [ 7 – 9 ].

Behavioral research suggests that the human auditoryterm system may optimize processing by operating within separate temporal ranges instead of in a unitary way across a continuum of temporal variation [ 10 – 15 ]. Timescales on the order of tens of milliseconds are argued to be optimized for rapid temporal integration, such as in modulation detection [ 16 ], gap detection [ 17 ], and nonsimultaneous masking [ 18 ]. On the other hand, previous models of temporal integration typically assuming leaky integration demonstrate timescales above 150 ms, such as loudness summation [ 19 , 20 ], signal detection in noiseterm [ 21 , 22 ], and temporal integration at threshold [ 14 , 23 – 26 ].

Here, we investigate whether these very clear behavioral results arguing for distinct timescales could be illuminated, especially in terms of their neural implementation, by considering cortical oscillations. Cortical oscillations reflect rhythmic activity of neural populations at different scales [ 27 , 28 ], and the frequencies of cortical oscillations are thought to reveal the corresponding temporal characteristics of sensory processing [ 29 – 32 ]. Therefore, by hypothesis, neural activity in the human auditoryterm system may show a multiscale oscillatory pattern, which could reflect temporal scales of auditoryterm processing. A short processing timescaleterm, which guarantees high temporal resolution and may reflect the extraction or decodingterm of fine-grained acoustic information, could be reflected in the gammaterm bandterm of oscillations that tracks fast acoustic dynamics, 30–50 Hz. A longer processing timescaleterm, which integrates acoustic information on a timescaleterm of 150–300 ms, could be reflected in the thetaterm bandterm oscillationterm, 4–7 Hz.

Of course, sounds contain information in ranges lying between these well-separated temporal regimes. Is there really such a compelling segregationterm of function in the time domainterm? Apart from the 2 timescales mentioned above, cortical oscillations within the alphaterm bandterm (8–12 Hz) have been found in many studies to critically correlate with auditoryterm attentionterm [ 33 – 35 ], auditoryterm working memory [ 36 , 37 ], and listening effort [ 34 , 38 ] and, furthermore, to predict speechterm intelligibility under challenging environments [ 39 ], which demonstrates a significant role for the alphaterm bandterm in auditoryterm processing. It is reasonable to hypothesize that the alphaterm bandterm is fundamental to auditoryterm perceptionterm, as demonstrated in a range of studies, but the timing (i.e., its intermediate position between lower thetaterm and higher gammaterm activity) also invites the conjecture that alphaterm range activity reflects aspects of auditoryterm processing on a timescaleterm of approximately 100 ms. Here, we investigate directly the most straightforward hypothesis: does alphaterm bandterm activity reflect the processing of acoustic information in a manner parallel to lower and higher neural frequencies?

Studies using amplitude-modulated sounds or click trains have shown a low-pass modulation transfer function (MTFterm) with a rebound above 30 Hz [ 40 – 45 ]. Although these findings demonstrate dominant auditoryterm responses in the low frequencyterm range (delta-theta and alphaterm) and the gammaterm bandterm, the cortical oscillations entrained by stimuli of corresponding frequencies of the regular modulation are not sufficient to demonstrate the importance of thetaterm, alphaterm, and gammaterm bands in auditoryterm processing, because the entrainmentterm could simply reflect a modulation-frequency following response which may have little to do with the auditoryterm system actively processing acoustic information in the thetaterm and gammaterm bands.

In studies using speechterm stimuli with irregular and complex modulations, it has been found that thetaterm bandterm activity entrains to the envelope of speechterm (and other signals), and that phaseterm locking in the thetaterm bandterm is enhanced by increased speechterm intelligibility [ 46 – 54 ]. Different evidence suggests that the gammaterm bandterm also plays an important role in phonemic and syllabic processing and comprehension of speechterm [ 55 – 60 ]. The results from speechterm processing suggest that thetaterm bandterm oscillations, instead of being simply entrained by modulations of the corresponding frequencyterm, may actively “chunkterm” complex acoustic signals at a timescaleterm corresponding to periods of the thetaterm bandterm [ 61 , 62 ], while the gammaterm bandterm is involved in processing detailed information, because the comprehension of speechterm and syllable processing requires access to acoustic information at the phonemic scale.

Here, we aimed to elucidate what kind of mechanism might form the basis of such multiscale hearing by using irregular temporal modulations of auditoryterm signals to ask whether neural oscillations are entrained equally to auditoryterm stimuli of different irregular modulation rates or, rather, only specific, restricted bands; moreover, we asked whether different stimulusterm modulation rates can be decoded from specific neural frequencyterm bands. We focus on cortical oscillations in the thetaterm and gammaterm bands as well as the alphaterm bandterm, in which strong effects related to auditoryterm processing have been reported [ 63 , 64 ].

Building on experiments by Boemio et al. (2005) [ 65 ] and Luo and PoeppelpersName (2012) [ 66 ], we generated acoustic stimuli with modulation rates that were centered at the typical periods of the neural thetaterm (4–7 Hz), alpha-beta (8–15 Hz), and gammaterm (31–45 Hz) frequencyterm bands (See Fig 1a for illustration of the stimuli). We measured using magnetoencephalography (MEGterm·mth) the robustness of neural responses evoked by the stimuli and examined the correlations between the neural responses and the acoustic structure of the stimuli. We manipulated the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the stimuli and evaluated the behavioral relevance of oscillations at the different rates. Next, classificationterm·mth analyses of the MEGterm·mth data were performed to test which components of the neutrally elicited MEGterm·mth signal were informative about the auditoryterm signals. We show, contrary to expectation, that different temporal rates of the neurophysiological signal are differentially related to the behavioral, classificationterm·mth, and neural results. We further support the findings of processing at distinct timescales by performing mutual informationterm analyses. Our combined neurophysiological and psychophysical results point to oscillatory neural mechanisms that underlie the segregated and discontinuous multiscale auditoryterm processing of signals which, subjectively, feel seamless and continuous. In particular, we argue that the neural computations reflected in thetaterm and gammaterm bandterm activity differ in a principled way from those reflected in the alphaterm bandterm and that alphaterm “splits” auditoryterm processing into paired low and high processing scales.

Discussion

In this MEG-based neurophysiological experiment, we investigate temporal codingterm at different scales by exploring the entrainmentterm of auditoryterm cortical oscillations to sounds with different modulation rates. Because healthy listeners (appear to) perceive sounds that contain modulation rates over various temporal scales in a manner that reflects a continuous MTFterm with a low-pass filter shape [ 16 , 72 ], the most straightforward hypothesis suggests that different auditoryterm stimulusterm rates are tracked in a comparable manner across modulation rates.

Contrary to this hypothesis, we find that oscillations in the (slower) thetaterm and (faster) gammaterm bands reliably track acoustic dynamics—but not in the (intermediate) alphaterm and betaterm bands. Subsequent analyses showed that the information carried in the neural thetaterm bandterm contributes to the decodingterm of all modulation rates used in this study, whereas the gammaterm bandterm mainly contributes to decodingterm only gamma-modulated sounds. Moreover, intertrial coherence in the thetaterm bandterm correlates with identification performance across all stimuli, underscoring that there is a clear perceptual consequence of the entrained oscillatory activity.

Our results are consistent with previous work showing entrainmentterm of auditoryterm cortical activity to low acoustic modulation rates [ 31 , 32 , 45 – 47 , 50 , 51 , 53 , 54 , 57 , 73 – 81 ]. The present results lend support from a new perspective to those studies using modulated sounds with amplitude modulation rates less than 10 Hz that have found strong entrainmentterm in the delta and thetaterm bands. Importantly, the failure to observe entrainmentterm in the alphaterm and betaterm bands in the present study also aligns with previous electrophysiological data of monkey primary auditoryterm cortex: cortical oscillations could entrain to modulation rates in the delta-theta bands but not at 12 Hz [ 73 ]. Preliminary MEGterm·mth data have also revealed such a response pattern [ 66 ].

The neural gammaterm bandterm, in addition to the delta and thetaterm bands, also reliably codes temporal information. The finding that the neural gammaterm bandterm is entrained by sounds with a corresponding temporal modulation rateterm—and that this alignment contributes to the classificationterm·mth of γ sounds—indicates that the auditoryterm system can track acoustic dynamics over short timescales, approximately 30 ms. This observation is consistent with studies using amplitude modulation created by binaural beats; in that work, strong entrainmentterm both in the thetaterm and gammaterm bands is found—but, again, not in the alphaterm or betaterm bands [ 82 ]. Recordings in the primary auditoryterm cortex of monkeys also show a phase-locked response using amplitude modulation at 30 Hz [ 83 , 84 ]. The data we show also confirm the contribution of gammaterm bandterm entrainmentterm to speechterm separation found in the multiple talker environments [ 57 ]. These studies, complemented by the data shown here, support the emerging view that the auditoryterm system extracts precise temporal information mainly on 2 discrete, segregated timescales.

The classificationterm·mth results we report show that the phaseterm information of thetaterm bandterm oscillations contributes to decodingterm sounds with modulation rates not only at the thetaterm bandterm timescaleterm but also at the alphaterm and gammaterm scales. One possible explanation is that thetaterm bandterm oscillations (i) track acoustic dynamics at that specific temporal scale and (ii) at the same time actively chunkterm (at a scale of the mean thetaterm bandterm period) sounds with faster modulation rates, so that acoustic properties over larger scales can be further extracted [ 85 ]. The gammaterm bandterm exclusively contributes to the classificationterm·mth of sounds with corresponding temporal dynamics. This may indicate specific processing at a fine-grained scale for acoustic temporal details. Overall, thetaterm bandterm oscillations may be necessary, although not sufficient, for processing sounds with temporal variations across different scales, and gammaterm bandterm oscillations may be needed for fine-grained processing.

The classificationterm·mth results also reveal data patterns that have not been shown in previous findings on the MTFterm. As the MTFterm shows decreased neural responses with increasing modulation rateterm, it is plausible to conjecture that temporal codingterm for acoustic dynamics would also show such a pattern. However, as observed in the classificationterm·mth analysis, the temporal codingterm capability does not correspond to the magnitude of the MTFterm—large ITCterm values for the alphaterm bandterm do not indicate high temporal codingterm capability. Some previous findings, though, do show a tendency for a rebound of neural activity in the gammaterm bandterm [ 40 , 41 , 44 ], although the magnitude of gammaterm bandterm activity is small compared with lower frequencyterm bands [ 45 ]. The most relevant finding is from Wang et al. [ 45 ], in which a robust response to amplitude-modulated sounds of 31.5 Hz was indicated by the percentage of subjects that showed robust auditoryterm steady-state response (aSSR). These results were suggestive, although the study did not test temporal codingterm of different frequencyterm bands and concluded “the MTFterm of the low-frequency aSSR generally has a low-pass pattern and only weakly depends on the carrier bandwidth.” Therefore, although we built our current study on assumptions about the MTFterm, the previous findings could not resolve the question of auditoryterm temporal codingterm on different timescales or demonstrate that the powerterm of alphaterm bandterm is preserved (and “reserved”) not for temporal codingterm but for other auditoryterm cognitive process.

The absence or marked reduction of trackingterm acoustic dynamics in the alphaterm bandterm suggests that neural activity reflected in the alphaterm bandterm may play a different role in audition. In the auditoryterm system, the neural computations reflected in the alphaterm bandterm signal may be more explicitly involved in auditoryterm attentionterm, working memory, listening effort, or functional inhibition [ 34 , 36 – 39 , 86 ], and the alphaterm bandterm may as such be more related to aspects of auditoryterm perceptionterm that differ from constructing the elementary perceptual representations. The alphaterm bandterm is associated with suppressing activity of cortical areas that are irrelevant for ongoing sensory processing according to tasks [ 87 ], which suggests (for the current context) that alphaterm bandterm oscillations co-occur with and segregate 2 temporal codingterm regimes (thetaterm and gammaterm bands) and modulate auditoryterm processing as a top-down process [ 88 ]. The alphaterm bandterm is involved in processing in other sensory systems and has been well established in visual and somatosensory perceptual analysis [ 89 – 92 ]. It will be relevant to further investigate in the auditoryterm domain how the alphaterm bandterm interacts with thetaterm and gammaterm domains to comprehend auditoryterm analysis more fully.

The finding that, like alphaterm bandterm activity, the betaterm bandterm also does not track acoustic dynamics may reflect that neural oscillations on that scale are reflective of different operations as well. Betaterm bandterm oscillations have been argued to play a role in predictive coding [ 93 , 94 ]; the task of the present study does not require active prediction. The random phaseterm in the betaterm bandterm adds more noiseterm to the classificationterm·mth process, so removing the phaseterm information of the betaterm bandterm actually results in better classificationterm·mth performance.

Our finding of entrainmentterm, and specifically concurrent parallel processing at different scales of the thetaterm and gammaterm bands, converges with the 2 perceptual time constants often found in behavioral studies [ 13 ]. Experiments on temporal integration frequently report a time constant in the (few) hundreds of milliseconds [ 14 , 21 – 26 ], while studies examining the high temporal resolution of the auditoryterm system show a time constant less than 30 ms [ 16 – 18 ]. The behavioral results we show also point to higher perceptual sensitivity for thetaterm and gammaterm sounds compared with alphaterm sounds. However, the behavioral method used in the present study cannot circumvent a concern with a selection biasterm to different sounds, because participants may identify θ sounds and γ sounds more easily simply because the modulation rates of these 2 sounds are located at the perceptual extremes in this experimental design. We acknowledge that this behavioral task is suboptimal—and primarily employed to ensure attentionterm during neurophysiological recording—so we scrupulously refrain from overinterpreting these data, beyond pointing out that the pattern is consistent with our hypothesis. Notwithstanding this potential concern, results from a recent psychophysical study dovetail with the view that the auditoryterm system works concurrently on a short timescaleterm (about 30 ms) to extract fine-grained acoustic temporal detail while processing more global acoustic patterns on a longer timescaleterm (>200 ms) [ 95 ].

Importantly, our results, based on nonspeech stimuli, suggest that such dual-scale entrainmentterm is not speechterm specific but may rather reflect an intrinsic auditoryterm processing property. The auditoryterm cortex tunes to both thetaterm frequencyterm and gammaterm frequencyterm acoustic dynamics [ 65 ]. The alphaterm bandterm reflects different operations. This segregated, dual tuning of the auditoryterm system at different scales may facilitate the extraction of information of different types in speechterm, such as featural, segmental, or phonemic information versus syllabic scale information [ 1 ]. We suggest that the measured oscillatory patterns at different timescales encode acoustic information over multiple scales, which leads to a temporal multiplexingterm of sensory information [ 59 , 96 ].

Mounting evidence shows that human perceptual systems employ a discrete process in which continuous signal information is broken up into segments [ 29 , 30 , 97 – 99 ]. As natural sounds contain information at multiple scales, the auditoryterm system may chunkterm continuous sounds using temporal windows of different sizes to sample information at different timescales, instead of processing acoustic information on a unitary scale. This multiplexingterm strategy solves the requirement in auditoryterm processing that both fine resolution and integration over time are needed for perceiving sounds with regularities both at large and small scales. One model proposes that, although a very high resolution is represented in subcortical areas, in the auditoryterm cortex, there are 2 main temporal windows used for processing acoustic information: one centered around 200 ms and the other around 30 ms [ 6 , 100 ]. On this view, acoustic information is analyzed and integrated using 2 temporal windows at these scales so that perceptual information at such “global” and “local” scales, whether in speechterm or nonspeech, can be abstracted concurrently to form a unitary percept that forms the basis for perceptual decision-making, lexical access, memory encoding, and other cognitive operations building on elementary perceptual representations. This design, however, builds in a hole in processing, a segregationterm of function between low and high processing rates—perhaps optimized for sensory sampling—by an intermediate rate, perhaps optimized for allocating attentional and memory resources and functionally inhibiting task- or stimulus-irrelevant actions. Whereas we typically address segregationterm of function in the spatial domainterm, i.e., different regions are specialized for different operations, here, we provide a compelling example of cortical segregationterm of function in the time domainterm.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the New York University Institutional Review Board (IRB# 10–7277) and conducted in conformity with the 45 Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) part 46 and the principles of the Belmont Report.

Participants

Sixteen right-handed volunteers (9 females; mean age: 24.8; standard deviation: 3.2) participated in this experiment. All participants provided informed written consent and received monetary compensation for their participation. Handedness was determined using the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory [ 101 ]. All participants had normal hearing and no neurological deficits. We excluded the data from 1 participant because of noiseterm issues during neurophysiological recording. Therefore, the analysis included the data from 15 participants (8 females; mean age: 25.2; standard deviation: 3.0).

Stimuli and experimental procedure

We created 3 stimulusterm types following the methods used in Boemio et al. (2005) and Luo and PoeppelpersName (2012). Each stimulusterm was 2 s long and generated by concatenating narrow-band frequency-modulated segments. The mean starting frequencyterm of each segment was randomly drawn from 2 frequencies, 1,000 Hz and 1,500 Hz. If the mean starting frequencyterm is 1,000 Hz, the frequency-modulated segment could sweep up to 1,500 Hz. If the mean starting frequencyterm is 1,500 Hz, the frequency-modulated segment could sweep down to 1,000 Hz. The bandwidth of segments was 100 Hz (within a critical bandterm at the center frequencies used). We generated each segment by adding up 100 frequency-modulated sinusoids with randomized amplitude and phaseterm. To create a segment that sweeps down, the starting frequencyterm of 100 sinusoid is randomly distributed between 1,450 Hz and 1,550 Hz and the end frequencyterm is distributed between 950 Hz and 1,050 Hz. To create a segment that sweeps up, the starting frequencyterm of 100 sinusoid is randomly distributed between 950 Hz and 1,050 Hz and the end frequencyterm is distributed between 1,450 Hz and 1,550 Hz.

The duration of the segments for each of the 3 stimulusterm types was drawn from a Gaussian distribution with means of 190 ms, 100 ms, and 27 ms, with standard deviations of 30 ms, 15 ms, and 3 ms, respectively. The distribution of the segment durations of the stimuli aligned with the range of periods typical of thetaterm (4–7 Hz), alphaterm/low betaterm (8–15 Hz), and low gammaterm (30–45 Hz) bandterm neural oscillations. We refer to the stimulusterm type with mean segment duration of 190 ms as a thetaterm ( θ ) soundterm, the stimulusterm type with mean segment duration of 100 ms as an alphaterm ( α ) soundterm, and the stimulusterm type with mean segment duration of 27 ms as a gammaterm ( γ ) soundterm. The cochleograms of the 3 stimuli were created using a Gammatone filterbank with 64 banks to decompose the stimuli from 50 to 22,050 Hz [ 102 , 103 ] and are shown in Fig 1a , with the corresponding prior distributions of segment duration for each stimulusterm type.

We generated white noiseterm segments of 4 s using the random number generator, the function “randn,” in Matlab R2014a (The MathWorks, Natick, MA). Then, we embedded the 3 types of clean stimuli into white noiseterm to create noise-masked stimuli at 5 levels of SNR: −9, −13, −17, −21, and −25 dB. The onset of white noiseterm preceded the onset of the embedded clean stimulusterm for a random interval uniformly distributed from 1 s to 1.5 s. Thirty stimuli for each SNR level and each stimulusterm type were created using individually generated noiseterm. As only 1 sample of each stimulusterm type was generated, the θ , α , and γ sounds were the same across all conditions. Therefore, there were 18 total conditions that included 3 clean stimuli and 15 (3 types × 5 SNR levels) noise-masked stimuli. In total, 540 trials (18 conditions × 30 trials per condition) were presented. The order of all stimuli was pseudorandomized for each participant. After each stimulusterm was presented, participants were required to push 1 of 3 buttons to indicate the type of stimulusterm. Between 1 and 2 s after participants responded, the next stimulusterm was presented, so that all stimuli were presented at random onset points. Participants were required to keep their eyes open and to focus on a white fixation cross in the center of a black screen.

All stimuli were normalized to about 65 dB SPL and delivered through plastic air tubes connected to foam ear pieces (E-A-R Tone Gold 3A Insert earphones, Aearo Technologies Auditoryterm Systems).

MEGterm·mth recording and channel selection

MEGterm·mth signals were measured with participants in a supine position and in a magnetically shielded room using a 157-channel whole-head axial gradiometer system (KIT, Kanazawa Institute of Technology, Japan). A sampling rate of 1,000 Hz was used with an online 1–200 Hz analog band-pass filter and a notch filter centered around 60 Hz. After the main experiment, participants were presented with 1-kHz tone beeps of 50 ms duration as a localizer to determine their M100 evoked responses, which is a canonical auditoryterm response [ 104 ]. Ten channels in each hemisphere, selected based on the peak of M100 response between 60 ms and 120 ms, were used as auditoryterm channels for each participant individually. Behavioral data analysis

Behavioral data analysis was conducted in MATLAB using the Palamedes toolbox 1.50 [ 105 ]. For each SNR level as well as the 3 clean stimuli, a 3-by-3 confusion matrix was created and then was collapsed into three 2-by-2 tables by treating 1 stimulusterm as “target” and pooling the observations on the other 2 stimuli as “noiseterm.” Correct identification of the target stimulusterm was counted as a “hit” while misidentification of the other 2 stimuli as the target stimulusterm was counted as “false alarm;” d-prime values were computed based on hit rates and false alarm rates of each table. A half artificial incorrect trial was added to the table with all correct trials [ 106 ].

MEGterm·mth data preprocessing and analysis

MEGterm·mth data analysis was conducted in MATLAB using the Fieldtrip toolbox 20140619 [ 107 ] and wavelet toolbox. Raw MEGterm·mth data were noiseterm reduced offline using the time-shifted principle component analysis [ 108 ] and sensor noiseterm suppression [ 109 ]. Trials were visually inspected, and those with artifacts such as signal jumps and large fluctuations were discarded. An independent component analysis was used to correct for eye blink, eye movement, heartbeat-related and system-related artifacts. Twenty-five trials were included in the analysis for each condition. Each trial was divided into 5-s epochs (1-s prestimulus period and 4-s stimulusterm period). Baseline was corrected for each trial by subtracting out the mean of the whole trial before further analysis.

To extract time-frequency information, single-trial data for each condition in each MEGterm·mth channel were transformed using functions of the Morlet wavelets embedded in the Fieldtrip toolbox, with a frequencyterm ranging from 1 to 50 Hz in steps of 1 Hz. To balance spectral and temporal resolution of the time-frequency transformation, from 1 to 20 Hz, the window length increased linearly from 1.5 circles to 7 circles and was kept constant at 7 circles above 20 Hz. Phaseterm and powerterm responses (squared absolute value) were extracted from the wavelet transform output at each time-frequency point.

The ITCterm, a measure of consistency of phase-locked neural activity entrained by stimuli across trials, was calculated for each time-frequency point (details as in [ 110 ]). ITCterm in different frequencyterm bands reflects phaseterm trackingterm of cortical oscillations to temporally modulated stimuli. As the baselines of phase-locking may be different across frequencyterm bands, which may be affected by powerterm distributions with a 1/f characteristic, a shuffling method of onset time was used to avoid this confound. Within each condition, the onset time of the stimulusterm was randomly chosen on each trial and a new dataset for each condition was created. The same analysis of ITCterm was applied on this new dataset. To create a distribution of shuffled ITCs, this shuffling procedure was repeated 1,000 times. The z -score of ITCterm was computed using the percentile of the original ITCterm in the distribution.

Induced powerterm was normalized by dividing the mean powerterm value in the baseline range (−0.6 to −0.1 s) and taking logarithms with base 10 and then was converted into values with the unit of decibel by multiplying by 10. The evoked powerterm response was computed by applying the time-frequency transform on averaged temporal responses across all trials. The baseline correction was the same as that used in computing the induced powerterm.

The ITCterm and powerterm data were averaged from 0.3 s to 1.8 s poststimulus onset to minimize the effects of stimulus-evoked onsets and offsets and within 4 frequencyterm bands: thetaterm (4–7 Hz), alphaterm (8–12 Hz), betaterm (13–30 Hz), and gammaterm (31–45 Hz).

To examine whether phaseterm and powerterm in different frequencyterm bands can explain behavioral performance, correlations between behavioral performance and neural measurements were tested by calculating correlation coefficients between d′ and either ITCterm or powerterm across the 5 SNRs, and then a 1-sample t test was performed for each frequencyterm bandterm and each stimulusterm type to determine whether the correlation is significant. As ITCterm is not normally distributed, the rationalized arcsine transform was applied before calculation of correlation coefficients [ 111 ].

All calculations were first conducted in each MEGterm·mth channel and then averaged across selected auditoryterm channels. Statistical analyses of ITCterm and powerterm were conducted separately for the 3 clean stimuli and the 15 masked stimuli using rmANOVA. When multiple comparisons were performed, to control familywise error rate and, at the same time, not to cause a high rate of false negatives, the Bonferroni correction was used when there were less than 10 comparisons performed, and an adjusted FDR was used when there were more than 10 comparisons performed [ 67 , 68 ].

Cochlear-scaled correlationterm·mth and mutual informationterm

The stimuli in this study were created by concatenating frequencyterm sweep segments of different durations, which may create sharp acoustic edges at the boundary between 2 adjacent segments. These acoustic edges indicate boundaries of frequencyterm sweep segments and represent (one aspect of) the temporal structure of the stimuli, which correlates with modulation rates. By computing mutual informationterm between temporal patterns of these acoustic edges and phaseterm series of MEGterm·mth signals, we can investigate in which frequencyterm bandterm the neural phaseterm pattern best correlates with the temporal structure of stimuli. The results could tell us whether a frequencyterm bandterm is more or less involved in processing certain stimuli.

To quantify the acoustic edges and extract the temporal structure of the stimuli, inspired by the concept of cochlear-scaled entropy [ 112 ], we created an index, cochlear-scaled correlationterm·mth. We first used a Gammatone filterbank of 64 banks, ranging from 50 Hz to 22,050 Hz, to decompose the sounds and extracted the envelope of each cochlear bandterm. Then, a moving average window of 10 ms was applied on the envelope of each cochlear bandterm to create a vector of length 64 at each time point. We computed the Pearson’s correlationterm·stat between 2 vectors of adjacent time points and the correlation results were then down-sampled to a sampling rate of 100 Hz, which corresponds to the sampling rate of the phaseterm series of the MEGterm·mth signals. The cochlear-scaled correlationterm·mth for each soundterm is shown in Fig 4a . The visualization shows that cochlear-scaled correlationterm·mth can confer the temporal structure of stimuli. Within a frequencyterm sweep segment, the cochlear-scaled correlationterm·mth is high—close to 1—and at the boundaries of frequencyterm sweep segments, a sudden drop of correlation coefficient is evident. The temporal structure of these sudden drops of correlation efficiency correlates with modulation rates, with the θ soundterm having the fewest drops and the γ soundterm having the most drops.

To quantify shared information between the cochlear-scaled correlationterm·mth and phaseterm series of MEGterm·mth signals, we used the framework of mutual informationterm·stat [ 96 , 113 ]. Mutual informationterm (MI) was calculated with the Information Breakdown Toolbox in MATLAB [ 114 , 115 ]. We computed the MI between phaseterm series of each frequencyterm (2–50 Hz) extracted from the time-frequency analysis described above and the cochlear-scaled correlationterm·mth of the θ , α , and γ sounds [ 50 , 59 , 80 , 116 ]. For example, when we computed MI between the cochlear-scaled correlationterm·mth of the θ sounds and the 3 phaseterm series evoked, respectively, by θ , α , and γ sounds, we used the phaseterm series evoked by α and γ sounds as control conditions and examined whether MI between the cochlear-scaled correlationterm·mth of θ sounds and the phaseterm series evoked by θ sounds is significant. The mutual informationterm value of each frequencyterm was calculated for each subject and for each channel across trials before averaging.

The cochlear-scaled correlationterm·mth we compute is simply the values at each time point corresponding to the time point of phaseterm. For each frequencyterm of the MEGterm·mth response, the phaseterm distribution was composed of 6 equally spaced bins: 0 to pi/3, pi/3 to pi * 2/3, pi * 2/3 to pi, pi to pi * 4/3, pi * 4/3 to pi * 5/3, and pi * 5/3 to pi * 2. By choosing 6 bins for phaseterm information, we ensured that there was enough temporal resolution to capture acoustic dynamics, because (at least) greater than 2 times the temporal resolution than the frequencyterm focused on is needed to quantify information at the frequencyterm. The cochlear-scaled correlationterm·mth was grouped using 8 bins equally spaced from the minimum value to the maximum value. Eight bins were chosen because we wanted to have enough discrete precision to capture changes in acoustic properties while making sure that each bin has sufficient counts for mutual informationterm analysis, because the greater number of bins would lead to zero counts in certain bins.

The estimation of mutual informationterm is subject to bias caused by finite sampling of the probability distributions because limited data were supplied in the present study (a finite number of trials). Therefore, a quadratic extrapolation embedded in the Information Breakdown Toolbox was applied to correct bias. MI is computed on various subsets of the trials of the dataset of each condition. A quadratic function is then fit to these data points, and the actual mutual informationterm is taken to be the zero-crossing value. This new value reflects the estimated mutual informationterm for an infinite number of trials and greatly reduces the finite sampling bias [ 117 , 118 ].

Single-trial classificationterm·mth

A single-trial classificationterm·mth analysis of stimulusterm type was carried out on the clean stimulusterm condition to examine how the auditoryterm system encodes information at different timescales. The procedure was described in detail in Ng et al. (2013), and similar methods were also used in Luo and PoeppelpersName (2012), Herrmann et al. (2013), and Cogan et al. (2011). For each stimulusterm type, 1 trial was left out, and then a template was created by averaging across the remaining trials for this type of stimulusterm (the circular mean is used for phaseterm average). Three templates were created, and the distance between each template and the left-out trial from 1 of the 3 stimulusterm types was computed. The circular distance was applied for phaseterm classificationterm·mth by taking the circular mean over time and frequencyterm; the l 2 norm of the linear distance was used for powerterm classificationterm·mth. A trial was given 1 template’s label if the distance between this trial and the template was the smallest among 3 templates.

A confusion matrix of classificationterm·mth was constructed by carrying out classificationterm·mth for each trial of each stimulusterm type on each auditoryterm channel. Then, classificationterm·mth performance was measured using the same method used in the behavioral data analysis: correctly labeling the target stimulusterm was counted as a “hit” while labeling the other 2 stimuli as the target stimulusterm was counted as “false alarm;” d′ was calculated based on hit rates and false alarm rates and averaged across all auditoryterm channels. Instead of d′ , D′ was used to differentiate d′ computed in the classificationterm·mth analysis from d′ in behavioral results. An index of classificationterm·mth efficiency using phaseterm and powerterm response of difference frequencyterm bandterm was indicated by the mean of D′ over 3 stimulusterm types, which was compared to the total d′ of the identification task, which indicates participants’ sensitivity in the behavioral study [ 106 ].

Temporal progression of classificationterm·mth performance in the thetaterm and gammaterm bands

We carried out classificationterm·mth analysis using only the thetaterm and the gammaterm bands on each time point to examine how classificationterm·mth performance progresses temporally in 2 frequencyterm bands. We assumed that, on each time point, phaseterm angles across trials can be summarized using a von Mises distribution—the circular analogue of the normal distribution—with its mean approximated by the group mean of phaseterm angles across trials and its kappa value, an index for variance of von Mises distribution, estimated by computing variances of phaseterm angles across trials [ 119 ]. For each soundterm, we calculated the mean and kappa value from 24 out of 25 trials on each time point for 1 soundterm and left 1 trial out as for classificationterm·mth. The means and kappa values for 3 sounds were estimated and then were used to estimate likelihoods of the left-out trial from each soundterm belonging to 3 distributions. We computed the likelihoods for each channel and each frequencyterm and summarized the likelihoods across all channels selected and frequencies with each frequencyterm bandterm by adding up log likelihoods of each channel and each frequencyterm. The summarized log likelihood was used to classify the left-out trial. For example, if a left-out trial from a θ soundterm has a high log likelihood in the distribution estimated by 24 trials from α soundterm but lower log likelihoods in the other 2 distributions, we classified this left-out trial from θ soundterm as from an α soundterm.

A confusion matrix of classificationterm·mth was constructed by carrying out classificationterm·mth on each time point in each frequencyterm bandterm for each trial of each stimulusterm type. Then, classificationterm·mth performance was converted to D′ using the procedure described above in Single-trial classificationterm·mth.

A cluster-based permutation testterm·stat was conducted on the classificationterm·mth results [ 120 ]. For each frequencyterm bandterm, after we assigned the classified labels to the trials from all 3 sounds, we randomly shuffled the classified labels across 75 trials from 3 sounds and created a new dataset of classificationterm·mth results. We then converted the classificationterm·mth results to D′ and conducted 1-tailed 1-sample t tests to determine whether D′ on each time point is larger than the baseline line, D′ = 0. We set the threshold of significance as 0.05 and computed cluster-level t values of each cluster comprising time points above the threshold. The cluster with the largest cluster-level t value was picked for creating a distribution. This procedure was repeated 1,000 times and a distribution over cluster-level t values was formed. We set the 95th percentile of the distribution over cluster-level t values as the threshold. Then, on the classificationterm·mth results from the original data, we conducted a 1-tailed 1-sample t test in each frequencyterm bandterm for each soundterm and set the threshold as 0.05. The cluster-level t values of clusters comprising time points with significant classificationterm·mth performance were computed and the clusters with cluster-level t values larger than the threshold determined from the distribution created by permutation were considered as significant clusters. This cluster-based permutation testterm·stat was conducted in each frequencyterm bandterm for each soundterm.
