Introduction

Adaptationterm·dom to stimulus statisticsterm is a fundamental principle of sensory processing 1 – 3 , which enables the brain to represent sensory information in ways that are computationally efficient 3 , 4 and robust to noise 5 , 6 . Certain forms of adaptationterm·dom to stimulus statisticsterm have been well studied and are known to be present at early sensory processing levels. In the visual system, for example, retinal responses adapt to mean light intensity 7 , while in the auditory system, adaptationterm·dom to mean sound levelterm has been demonstrated at the level of the auditory nerve 8 . Nevertheless, it remains poorly understood how adaptationterm·dom to higher stimulus statisticsterm changes as a result of hierarchical processing within the sensory systems or how this links to perception.

In both the visual and auditory systems, stimulus contrastterm·def—the variability of light or sound levelterm—is a stimulus statistic that results in neuronal adaptationterm·dom 9 – 11 . Contrastterm adaptationterm·dom may affect multiple neuronal response properties, but is accomplished principally by adjustments in response gain that compensate for the distribution of stimulus levels in a given sensory environment. This specific form of contrastterm adaptationterm·dom is known as contrastterm gain controlterm (or contrastterm normalizationterm·dom). Visual contrastterm gain controlterm is implemented at several stages of the visual system 10 – 17 , and is partially guided by corticofugalterm projections from primary visual cortexterm 18 . The perceptualterm consequences of visual contrastterm adaptationterm·dom are controversial 19 , although one report suggests that this enhances the ability of observers to detect subsequent contrastterm changes 20 . In the auditory system, however, the relative contributions of subcorticalterm and cortical structures and their role in contrastterm gain controlterm have not yet been fully elucidated, and it is not known how contrastterm gain controlterm affects perception.

Contrastterm gain controlterm is a prominent feature of neuronal responses in the auditory cortexterm of mice 21 and ferrets 9 , but in ferrets it is less robust in the midbrainterm 6 . Although this implies a primary role for auditory cortexterm in contrastterm gain controlterm, other studies have shown that the responses of subcorticalterm neurons are influenced by sensory context 22 – 28 , as well as motor and cognitive demands 29 – 32 . This raises the possibility that subcorticalterm circuits may also contribute to adaptationterm·dom to stimulus contrastterm·def. Furthermore, descending influences from the cortexterm need to be considered: manipulation of auditory corticofugalterm projections can alter the excitabilityterm and tuning properties of neurons in both the thalamusterm 33 – 35 and midbrainterm 34 – 37 , but their involvement in adaptationterm·dom to stimulus statisticsterm remains largely unexplored 27 , 38 .

In this study, we demonstrate the effects of contrastterm adaptationterm·dom on humanterm perception, by showing that acuity in a level discriminationterm·mth task is rapidly adjusted to partially compensate for changes in sound contrastterm. Discussion

Our results demonstrate that auditory contrastterm adaptationterm·dom, which has been associated mainly with the auditory cortexterm 9 , 21 , is exhibited to a similar degree by neurons in lemniscalterm subcorticalterm structures—the CNICterm and MGBvterm. Moreover, we have shown that this subcorticalterm adaptationterm·dom is independent of cortical activity. We also found that perceptualterm thresholds in a sound levelterm discriminationterm·mth task compensate for contrastterm in a similar way, and that the strength of perceptualterm contrastterm adaptationterm·dom can be predicted from the gain controlterm exhibited by auditory neurons.

Previous work in the ferretterm has shown that contrastterm adaptationterm·dom is weaker and less consistent in the CNICterm than in A1term 6 , and does not consistently compensate for stimulus contrastterm·def. In contrastterm, the results of this study show that compensatory contrastterm gain controlterm in mice is not purely a cortical computation, but is present to a comparable degree in both the lemniscalterm auditory midbrainterm and the thalamusterm. Although the contrasts used by Rabinowitz et al. 6 were different from those used in the present study, it is possible that this reflects a difference in subcorticalterm computations between mouseterm and ferretterm. In both species, however, the data suggest a hierarchyterm of contrastterm adaptationterm·dom, wherein subcorticalterm structures exhibit contrastterm gain controlterm but in cortexterm this becomes more consistent across neurons (in ferrets) or more temporally stable (in ferrets and mice). In the visual system, a hierarchyterm of contrastterm normalizationterm·dom is present at multiple processing levels from the retina upwards 41 . Similarly, predictionterm error signals increase along the auditory pathway 42 . Altogether, these findings suggest that some aspects of adaptationterm·dom to stimulus statisticsterm are organized in a serial fashion in the brain.

It is possible that contrastterm gain controlterm is also exhibited by neurons in more peripheral structures, particularly as adaptationterm·dom to mean sound levelterm takes place in the auditory nerve 8 . However, modeling studies suggest that contrastterm gain controlterm is present to a very limited degree in the auditory nerve 6 . In any case, our results show that auditory subcorticalterm neurons can execute contrastterm gain controlterm without the involvement of cortical activity. A full understanding of contrastterm gain controlterm will therefore require new hypotheses to be developed about the subcorticalterm neural circuitry and mechanisms that underlie this fundamental property of auditory neurons.

Although we found that the overall strength of contrastterm gain controlterm is similar in CNICterm, MGBvterm and A1term, adaptationterm·dom is not the same at each level of the processing hierarchyterm. A reduction in baselineterm firing rateterm during high-contrast stimulation, which may provide an additional mechanism for making overall firing rates invariant to contrastterm, is found only in the MGBvterm and A1term. Furthermore, the temporal dynamics of contrastterm gain controlterm change as we ascend the auditory pathway, suggesting that additional contrast-dependent processing happens at each level. In keeping with Rabinowitz et al. 6 , we found that the time constants for auditory contrastterm gain controlterm become longer at higher levels of the processing hierarchyterm. This mirrors previous results showing that the temporal integration window for auditory inputs becomes longer from the CNICterm, through MGBvterm, to A1term 43 , 44 .

The changes we observe in adaptationterm·dom time constantterm cannot be accounted for by temporal bandwidthterm changes in neuronal STRFs. This suggests that neurons at each processing level may actively adapt to the recent history of stimulus contrastterm·def over a range of time scales, rather than merely acting as relays for the transmission of auditory contrastterm. The progressive increase in the time constantterm of adaptationterm·dom along the auditory hierarchyterm is likely to result in an increasingly stable representation of the auditory environment in the cortexterm relative to subcorticalterm nuclei. Furthermore, the presence of multiple time scales of adaptationterm·dom at different levels of the auditory pathway may provide an effective means for representing sounds presented in different acoustical environments or tasks. Such diversity of dynamics among different cells also exists for visual contrastterm adaptationterm·dom in the retina 45 and adaptationterm·dom to mean level in the CNICterm 26 , suggesting that this may be a widespread property of sensory systems.

Contrastterm gain controlterm in the auditory cortexterm appears to be a specific case of neuronal normalizationterm·dom wherein the sensitivityterm of neurons adjusts to compensate for stimulus contrastterm·def 41 , 46 . It has been suggested that normalizationterm·dom is a canonicalterm computation in sensory systems and is present at multiple processing levels 41 . The results presented in this study expand on this idea by demonstrating that contrastterm gain controlterm is not only a property of neurons in auditory cortexterm, where it has been studied most extensively 6 , 9 , 21 , 47 , but, at least in mice, is equally robust in the CNICterm and MGBvterm. Contrastterm gain controlterm is therefore established at a relatively early processing level in the auditory pathway.

Our results demonstrate for the first time an important role for the thalamusterm in contrastterm adaptationterm·dom, by both increasing the duration of the adaptationterm·dom time constants and introducing a subtractive component (y-offsetterm adaptationterm·dom) that is subsequently inherited by cortexterm. Neither the contrastterm gain controlterm nor the subtractive component in contrastterm adaptationterm·dom found in the thalamusterm is dependent on auditory cortical activity. Thus, the thalamusterm is an active contributor to contrastterm adaptationterm·dom in the ascending auditory pathway, and not merely a relayterm from the midbrainterm to the cortexterm.

The longer adaptationterm·dom time constants we observe in the cortexterm suggest that further contrast-related processing happens there. As the representation of sound features changes along the ascending auditory pathway 48 , corresponding changes in contrastterm adaptationterm·dom may be required at each successive stage. If that is the case, an important question for future research will be whether contrastterm gain controlterm is implemented via different neural architectures, as has been shown for other neuromodulatory computations 49 , 50 . Thus, although auditory contrastterm normalizationterm·dom can be viewed as a canonicalterm computation in the brain, it is unlikely to be implemented by a canonicalterm neural circuit.

Corticofugalterm projections have previously been shown to have modulatory effects on the excitabilityterm and tuning properties of neurons in subcorticalterm nuclei in the auditory 33 – 37 , 51 , 52 , visual 53 , and somatosensory 54 , 55 systems. Several studies have reported a net excitatory effect of corticothalamic feedback, which can contribute to changes in receptive field shape 33 , 35 , 53 , 54 , 56 . In the auditory system, corticofugalterm modulation has mostly been assessed by measuring spontaneous activity and responses to tones and noise 33 – 35 , 56 , and evidence for how complex sound processing in the thalamusterm is affected is sparse. However, recent work suggests that corticothalamic feedback from layer VI of A1term to MGBvterm contributes to auditory scene analysis 57 . Furthermore, in the somatosensory system, in vitroterm recordings have demonstrated that the effects of corticothalamic feedback are dynamic, changing from suppressive to facilitatory depending on stimulation frequency 58 , suggesting that corticothalamic input may contribute to context-dependent processing of sensory information.

Our cortical silencingterm·mth results indicate that while corticofugalterm inputs have a strong effect on the overall excitabilityterm of thalamic neurons (and a weaker effect on the CNICterm), the receptive field properties of neurons in these subcorticalterm structures remain unchanged. The reduction in excitabilityterm induced by transient optogeneticterm·mth silencingterm·mth of the auditory cortexterm, and the difference in corticofugalterm effects on CNICterm and MGBvterm, are in accordance with what would be expected from previous studies of the effects of widespread inactivation of A1term on subcorticalterm responses to simple stimuli 59 . However, focal silencingterm·mth or activation of auditory cortical areas can shift the BF of neurons in both the MGBvterm and CNICterm 34 , 35 . Manipulating the activity of frequency-specific regions of auditory cortexterm may therefore have similar effects on the structure of the STRFs acquired from complex sounds, which would be consistent with a potential role for corticofugalterm feedback in the task-dependent STRFterm·mth plasticity of auditory midbrainterm neurons 29 .

It has been proposed on the basis of in vitroterm investigations that corticothalamic feedback provides synaptic noise, which helps thalamic neurons to integrate synaptic inputs more linearly 60 , 61 . However, by isolating the corticofugalterm contribution to the representation of ongoing stimuli in vivo, our results suggest that corticofugalterm activity decreases the linear input–output relationship and the reliabilityterm of neuronal responses in the CNICterm and MGBvterm. In the CNICterm, this effect of cortical silencingterm·mth on the transfer function of the neurons appears to depend on wakefulness, but in the MGBvterm was present even under anesthesia, implying that it is not simply a result of trial-to-trial variability in corticofugalterm synaptic transmission due to changes in cognitive state.

Although cortical silencingterm·mth alters the excitabilityterm, reliabilityterm, and linearity of MGBvterm and CNICterm responses, we found no effect on the strength of contrastterm gain controlterm. This is consistent with the lack of effect of widespread cortical cooling on adaptationterm·dom to mean level by IC neurons 27 . However, cortical deactivation does prevent the change in the rate of adaptationterm·dom by IC neurons following repeated exposure to stimuli with different sound levelterm distributions 27 . It is therefore possible that descending corticofugalterm inputs might play a role in contrastterm adaptationterm·dom in rapidly changing acoustic environments.

The role of corticofugalterm inputs could be clarified by investigating contrastterm adaptationterm·dom in non-lemniscal subdivisions of the auditory thalamusterm and midbrainterm. In this study, we aimed to investigate the evolution of contrastterm adaptationterm·dom across the primary inputs to A1term. However, neurons in non-lemniscal regions of the MGB and IC display stronger stimulus-specific adaptationterm·dom than their lemniscalterm counterparts 25 , 38 and receive stronger inputs from the auditory cortexterm 38 , 62 ; these neurons may also show correspondingly stronger contrastterm adaptationterm·dom, which is under the influence of cortical activity.

The behavioral consequences of adaptationterm·dom to stimulus statisticsterm in the auditory system have received very little attention. Presenting sounds with interaural level differences 63 or interaural time differences 64 that follow specific statistical distributions results in comparable adaptive changes in the sensitivityterm of binaural neurons in the brain and in the perceptualterm sensitivityterm of humanterm listeners. Furthermore, adaptationterm·dom to mean level and contrastterm can improve the decoding of complex sounds from population neuronal activity, potentially providing a mechanism for establishing noise invariance 6 .

Our results show for the first time that humanterm auditory perception is subject to a behavioral form of contrastterm gain controlterm. We also show that the strength of perceptualterm contrastterm adaptationterm·dom in humans is predictable from physiological contrastterm adaptationterm·dom in midbrainterm, thalamic, and cortical auditory neurons in mice. This highlights the importance of adaptationterm·dom in regulating both neuronal and perceptualterm sensitivityterm according to the ongoing statisticsterm of the sensory environment. Furthermore, there is evidence that contrastterm gain controlterm may mediate the effects of attention on neural processing 65 , 66 . It would therefore be interesting to determine whether contrastterm gain controlterm at different levels of the auditory system can be differentially modulated depending on the sensory and behavioral contexts in which sounds occur.

The demonstration in this paper of the widespread and robust nature of auditory contrastterm adaptationterm·dom at both physiological and perceptualterm levels highlights the importance of this adaptive mechanism, and shows that a complex computation with strong implications for behavior can be implemented in subcorticalterm circuitry without the need of cortexterm.

Methods

Mice

All animal experiments conformed to ethical standards approved by the Committee on Animal Care and Ethical Review at the University of Oxford and were licensed by the UK Home Office (Animal Scientific Procedures Act, 1986, amended in 2012). A total of 39 mice were used in this study. Four strains of male and female mice were used in the electrophysiological experiments: C57BL6/J (Envigo, UK), GAD2-IRES-cre (Jackson Laboratories, USA), VGAT-ChR2-YFP (Jackson Laboratories, USA), and C57BL6/NTac.Cdh23 67 . C57BL6/J , GAD2-IRES-cre , and VGAT-ChR2-YFP were 7–12-weeks old at the time of data collection, and C57BL6/NTac.Cdh23 were 10–20-weeks old at the time of data collection. All experiments were carried out in a sound-attenuated chamber.

Humanterm subjects

All procedures conformed to ethical standards approved by the Inter-divisional Research Ethics Committee at the University of Oxford ( R52936 /RE001). Eight (four male, four female) (plus two additional participants (both male) for the level control experiment) humanterm participants (18–30-years-old) with normal audiometry provided informed consent and participated in the contrast-dependent sound levelterm discriminationterm·mth study. All experiments were carried out in a sound-attenuated chamber.

Electrophysiologyterm·mth stimuli

Stimuli were presented with a Tucker-Davis Technologies (TDT) RX6 Multifunction processor at ∼200 kHz. Sounds were amplified by a TDT SA1 stereo amplifier and delivered via a modified Avisoft ultrasonic electrostatic loudspeaker (Vifa) positioned ~1 mm from the ear canal. The sound presentation system was calibrated to a flat (±1 dB) frequency-level response between 500 and 64,000 Hz.

Stimuli consisted of dynamic random chords (DRCs) with individual chords having a duration of 25 ms (including 5 ms on and off ramps) and comprising 25 superposed frequencies, logarithmically spaced between 1000 and 64,000 Hz (1/4 octave intervals). The tones of the DRCterm were played at sound levels that were randomly drawn from one of two uniform distributions: 30–50 dB sound pressure level (SPL) (low contrastterm) or 20–60 dB SPL (high contrastterm). The mean of the distribution was therefore constant, at 40 dB SPL. The logarithmic statisticsterm of the decibel scale have been found to better match the statisticsterm of natural sounds 44 , 68 . The overall sound levelterm of the DRCs was calibrated to be 79–83 dB SPL. A DRCterm for any given trial was played for either 40 s or 5 s (5-s trial duration in optogeneticterm·mth experiments), with inter-trial intervals of 2–10 s. DRCs have previously been used to assess contrastterm adaptationterm·dom in the auditory system of ferrets and mice 6 , 9 , 21 , 47 .

The overall sound levelterm of high-contrast stimuli was slightly (∼3 dB) higher than that of the low-contrast stimuli, due to the nonlinearityterm·mth inherent in the logarithmic scale. An additional experiment was therefore carried out in which the overall sound levels of DRCs were matched in low- and high-contrast stimuli, at the expense of equality of sound levels of individual tones in the DRCs, to control for possible effects of this small difference in overall sound amplitude (see Supplementary Fig. 5 ).

In vivo extracellular recordings

We carried out extracellular recordings using 32- or 64-channel silicon probes (NeuroNexus Technologies Inc.), in a 4 × 8, 8 × 8, or 2 × 32 electrode configuration. Electrophysiological data were acquired on a Tucker-Davis technologies (TDT) RZ2 BioAmp processor and collected and saved using custom-written Matlab code ( https://github.com/beniamino38/benware ).

For experiments carried out under anesthesia, mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg kg −1 ) and medetomidine (0.14 mg kg −1 ). We also administered intraperitoneal injections of atropine (Atrocare, 1 mg kg −1 ) to prevent bradycardia and reduce bronchial secretions, and dexamethasone (Dexadreson, 4 mg kg −1 ) to prevent brain edema. Prior to initial surgery, bupivacain was administered as an analgesic under the scalp. The depth of anesthesia was monitored via the pedal reflex and small additional doses of the ketamine/medetomidine mix were given subcutaneously approximately every 15 min once the recordings started (∼1–1.5 h post induction of anesthesia). The dosage of individual top-ups depended on the depth of anesthesia at the time, but corresponded to ∼50 mg kg −1 h −1 of ketamine and ∼0.07 mg kg −1 h −1 of medetomidine. All recordings were performed in the right hemisphere. A silver reference wire was positioned in visual cortexterm of the contralateral hemisphere, and a grounding wire was attached under the skin on the neck. The head was fixed in position with a metal bar acutely attached with bone cement to the skull over the left hemisphere. We then made 2-mm diameter circular craniotomies above the IC (centered ∼5 mm posterior from bregma and ∼1 mm lateral from midline), over the visual cortexterm for auditory thalamic recordings (centered ∼3 mm posterior from bregma and ∼2.1 mm lateral from midline), and/or over the auditory cortexterm (centered ∼2.5 mm posterior from bregma and ∼4.5 mm lateral from midline). Following exposure of the brain, the exposed dura mater was kept moist with saline. The silicon probe was then inserted carefully into the recording site of interest.

In the mouseterm, the dorsal surface of the IC is not covered by the cortexterm, and is very distinct 62 . The craniotomies over the IC were always large enough to see the entire exposed IC surface, so we could visually target the probes. We inserted the probe in the center of the IC, and therefore above the CNICterm, where the overlying dorsal cortexterm is relatively thin 62 . We confirmed this by checking for a clear dorso-ventral tonotopic gradient in the STRFs that is indicative of this nucleus 69 , 70 . The tuning widths of the STRFs measured in CNICterm are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2 . We also estimated frequency response areas using tones, which confirmed the presence of dorso-ventral tonotopic gradients with narrow tuning (data not shown). When we were positioning the electrode array, we observed tightly locked multiunit responses to noise stimuli, characteristic of CNICterm neurons, and post-mortem inspection of the midbrainterm confirmed that the probe had indeed been located in the CNICterm.

Prior to insertion into auditory thalamusterm, the probe was coated in DiI (Sigma-Aldrich) for subsequent histological verification of the recording site. Recording sites were confirmed as being located in auditory thalamusterm if multiunit activity responded to broadband noise and was frequency tuned when the tip of the probe was ∼2.5–3.5 mm below the brain surface. Auditory thalamic recordings were subsequently attributed to MGBvterm by histological investigation of recording sites and by analysis of physiological responses. Based on an immunohistochemical study by Lu et al. 71 on the shape and size of subdivisions of the mouseterm auditory thalamusterm, we allocated recording sites to the MGBvterm if they responded reliably to DRCterm stimulation on electrode channels < 500 μm from the lateral border of the MGB (see data inclusion criteriaterm·mth).

Finally, A1term was identified by robust neuronal responses to broadband noise bursts, and a caudo-rostral tonotopic axis. Cortical tonotopy was assessed in 4/10 mice by estimating frequency response areas from responses to pure tones on four recording shanks spaced 200 µm apart spanning 600 µm along a rostro-caudal gradient.

For awaketerm recordings in the IC, we chronically implanted a recording chamber under isoflurane (1.5–2% in O 2 ) general anesthesia. The recording chamber consisted of a metal cylinder positioned over a craniotomy, with a lightly attached circular window in order to close the recording chamber. We placed the recording chamber above the IC, together with a head bar and a reference (silver wire) in the contralateral hemisphere. We then fixed the implant to the skull using a dental adhesive resin cement (Super Bond C&B). Following full recovery, on a subsequent day the mouseterm was head-fixed, the recording chamber was opened, and a sterile recording probe was acutely inserted into the brain via the recording chamber.

Optogeneticterm·mth silencingterm·mth of auditory cortexterm

To transiently silence the activity of auditory cortical excitatory neurons, we employed either a transgenic or a viral approach to express ChR2 in auditory cortical inhibitory neurons. VGAT-ChR2-YFP mice express ChR2-YFP in GABAergic neurons throughout the adult brain. Optogeneticterm·mth activation of cortical inhibitory neurons is the most effective available method for inhibiting cortical activity at sub-second time resolution 72 over the time window required for this study, and has been used extensively to transiently silence excitatory activity (including corticofugalterm outputs) in cortical areas in mice 18 , 73 – 75 . Viral injection surgeries were performed under isoflurane (∼1.5%) anesthesia, with the animal positioned in a stereotaxic frame (Kopf instruments, USA). For viral transfection, we injected a floxed AAV5-DIO-ChR2-eYFP (UNC gene therapy vector core) into auditory cortexterm of GAD2-IRES-cre mice. We injected ∼400 nl of virus, spread over three locations (spaced caudal-rostrally ∼400 μm apart) at three depths (700, 500, and 300 μm from cortical surface), to ensure widespread expression in auditory cortexterm (Supplementary Fig. 5 ). Mice were used for electrophysiological recordings > 4 weeks post injection of virus. This ensured strong expression of ChR2-eYFP in the auditory cortexterm.

For optogeneticterm·mth silencingterm·mth, we exposed the auditory cortexterm to blue (470 nm) LED light. This was achieved by placement of a 200 μm ( VGAT-ChR2-YFP experiments) or 1 mm optical fiber ( GAD2-cre + viral ChR2 experiments) immediately above the dura mater over the auditory cortexterm to allow for blue light exposure to ChR2-expressing cells. For silencingterm·mth of auditory cortical activity during recordings in MGBvterm or CNICterm, we stimulated with blue light at 40 Hz frequency using sinusoidal waves or 15 ms pulses (10 ms gaps). When recording from auditory cortexterm, we stimulated with blue light at 40 Hz using either sinusoidal waves or 15 ms pulses (10 ms gaps) or constant light stimulation. Light power was ∼5–7 mW mm −2 at the tip of the fiber. We found that light stimulation (40 Hz (sinusoid or pulsed) or constant light) effectively silenced activity in auditory cortical neurons by driving inhibitory neurons for the duration of the DRCterm stimulation (5 s) (Supplementary Fig. 6 ).

Humanterm psychoacoustic experiments

Stimulus presentation and response collection were performed using PsychoPy 1.85.6 76 . Sounds were presented using a MOTU 828 mkII soundcard and delivered via Sennheiser 650HD headphones in a sound-attenuated chamber. The headphones were calibrated to a flat (±1 dB) frequency-level response between 125 and 19,500 Hz.

Stimuli consisted of broadband noise bursts (100 ms) and dynamic random chords (DRCs) comprising 25-ms duration chords with 29 frequencies logarithmically spaced between 150 and 19,200 Hz. DRCs were constructed with each tone of the DRCterm being played at levels randomly assigned from a uniform distribution, ranging from 35 to 45 dB SPL (low contrastterm) or 25 to 55 dB SPL (high contrastterm) around a fixed mean amplitude of 40 dB SPL. The total sound amplitude of the DRCs was measured to be 64–69 dB SPL. The stimulus for each trial was 1,950 ms long, consisting of 1000 ms of DRCterm, followed by 100 ms broadband noise (reference: 60 dB SPL), 250 ms of DRCterm, 100 ms of broadband noise (Target: 52–68 dB SPL), and ending with 500 ms of DRCterm. The overall sound levelterm of high-contrast stimuli was slightly higher relative to low-contrast stimuli (∼4 dB).

A control experiment was also carried out, where the overall sound levels of DRCs were matched in low- and high-contrast stimuli, at the expense of the equality of levels of individual tone levels in the DRCs, to determine whether the small difference in overall sound amplitude between the high- and low-contrast stimuli could account for the JNDterm change with contrastterm (Supplementary Fig. 1 ).

Spike sorting

We clustered potential neuronal spikes using KiloSort 77 ( https://github.com/cortex-lab/KiloSort ). Following this automatic clustering step, we manually inspected the clusters in Phy ( https://github.com/kwikteam/phy ), and removed noise (movement artefacts, optogeneticterm·mth light artefacts etc.). In order to identify units that were continuously responsive to DRCterm stimulation, we measured the signal powerterm (SP) and noise power (NP) of the neural responses 39 . For all results, unless otherwise specified, we excluded units for which the ratio NP/SP > 60, indicating that these units did not respond reliably to the DRCs on repeated trials.

Spectro-temporal receptive fields

Neuronal response rates were binned to produce peri-stimulus time histograms (PSTHs) at the same temporal resolution (25 ms) as the chords in the DRCs. To exclude transient onsetterm responses, we excluded the first 500 ms of each stimulus and response. Linear spectro-temporal receptive fields (STRFs, k fh ) were then estimated to describe the relationship between the PSTHs and the sound levels (in dB SPL) of the tones in the DRCs. The STRFs were constrained to be separableterm·mth in frequency ( f ) and time history ( h ), i.e. k f h = k f ⊗ k h , and were fitted using maximum likelihood 78 . The separability constraint was used because it reduces the number of parameters that need to be estimated, and can give good STRFs when experimental data are limited 11 . We found that this approach produced acceptable STRFs in all three areas that we recorded from.

Subsequently, a single overall STRFterm·mth was fitted to data from both contrasts, for estimation of contrast-dependent output nonlinearities.
