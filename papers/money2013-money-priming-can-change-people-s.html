phenomenonmoney priming
claim-typemechanism
mechanismPriming works by supplying an interpretation for an ambiguous outcome situation, so the more constrained and unambiguous the outcome, the less room the prime has to shift the response and the smaller the effect — “The more specific and less ambiguous the outcome (such as when responding to the word doctor after seeing the word nurse), the smaller the expected priming effect because there is less wiggle room for the prime to alter the response (e.g., Loersch & Payne, 2011).”
mechanismFrequent real-world contact with money keeps the concept chronically accessible, so that even subtle cues suffice to activate it and change behaviour — “When the content of a prime is easily accessible, it can take only subtle cues reminiscent of it to alter outcomes (Higgins & Brendl, 1995).”
mechanismMoney cues set two opposing forces in motion - one routing from money to free markets to endorsement of the systems supporting them, the other routing from money to personal agency and self-sufficiency, which removes the need to endorse those systems - and the two cancel — “Integrating the two lines of reasoning suggests that there might be countervailing forces when people are primed with money and asked to contemplate the righteousness of existing social systems.”
mechanismMoney primes act by activating the phenomenological self as an owner of money, which is why primes tying money to the participant personally produce parallel and stronger effects — “Parallels between general money prime effects and primes that highlight the self owning money suggest that self activation might be an important contributor.”
relation — “Thus began our investigation of whether money priming could alter responses related to system justification, social dominance, belief in a just world, and free market ideology.”
relation — “In summary, there are two theoretically derived reasons why Rohrer et al.’s (2015) results were inconsistent with Caruso et al.’s (2013).”

Content tags
content-observations: finding · quantity · variable · definition · key-term — grounded in the full text
findingAcross the accumulated money-priming literature the author identifies two robust effects: reduced interpersonal attunement, and a shift into a work and business mentality. — “Money priming experiments (totaling 165 to date, from 18 countries) point to at least 2 major effects.”
findingPeople reminded of money become less prosocial, less caring and less warm, and avoid interdependence. — “First, compared to neutral primes, people reminded of money are less interpersonally attuned.”
findingPeople reminded of money work harder on difficult tasks, perform better on objective outcomes, and report feeling efficacious. — “They exert effort on challenging tasks, demonstrate good performance, and feel efficacious.”
findingFeelings of power were tested repeatedly as the explanation for money-priming effects and were not supported: money primes did not change measured power. — “We measured feelings of power multiple times using a published scale (Schmidt & Frieze, 1997) as well as items we created but did not see changes in power as a result of money priming.”
findingThe independence-interdependence self-construal account fared only marginally better: scores moved in the predicted direction but only weakly. — “Scores were in the predicted direction, but only weakly.”
findingThe sign of the effect depends on how the money is presented: new or clean money increases moral, fair and prosocial responding, whereas used or dirty money produces greed and exploitation. — “Compared to used money or neutral primes, people reminded of new money make more moral decisions, think of themselves as prosocial and act accordingly, seek to be fair, and think about honesty and fair dealings.”
findingMoney priming is not interchangeable with priming other means of exchange: credit card cues loosen spending constraints while cash cues sharpen attention to price and cost. — “Cash cues, in contrast, heighten sensitivity to costs, as seen in more paying attention to price, reduced desire to spend, and a broad motivation to avoid mistakes (Chatterjee et al., 2013; Chatterjee & Rose, 2012; Mandel & Johnson, 2002; Tong et al., 2013).”
findingThe same pattern of effects appears in undergraduates, working adults, managers and children as young as four, across North America, Europe and Asia. — “College students, working adults, children as young as 4 years old, and business managers show similar effects (Tables 1 and 2; for a fuller review of the experiments to date [165 and counting], see Baumeister & Vohs, 2015).”
findingDifferent delivery methods for the money prime - images, physical handling, even play money - yield comparable effects. — “Viewing images of money, touching money, and even seeing or holding play money can produce similar effects”
findingPrimes that link the money to the participant's own holdings, such as withdrawing cash from a machine or thinking about one's own wages, cut helpfulness and volunteering, suggesting self-relevance strengthens the effect. — “Two field studies in France showed that getting money out of a cash machine (and thus being reminded of one’s own money) reduced behavioral helpfulness more than walking by a cash dispenser (Guéguen & Jacob, 2013).”
findingThe proposed reason the Caruso et al. system-justification result failed to replicate is that money priming induces self-sufficiency, which works against the defensive motive that normally drives system justification. — “Money primes stimulate a sense that one can survive and thrive on one’s own, which might quell a defensive need to bolster the existing systems.”
findingA second proposed reason for the replication discrepancy is sample-specific meaning of money: Caruso et al.'s University of Chicago and 2011 Mechanical Turk participants plausibly held a tighter, more favourable link between money and free-market systems than later samples. — “Rohrer, Pashler, and Harris (2015) failed to replicate those effects.”
quantity165 — total number of money-priming experiments counted to date, run in 18 countries — “Money priming experiments (totaling 165 to date, from 18 countries) point to at least 2 major effects.”
quantity200% — increase in intended donations produced by a credit card prime relative to no prime — “A credit card prime, compared to a no prime condition, led to a 200% increase in intended donations in a laboratory experiment (Feinberg, 1986).”
quantity113% — increase in bids in a real binding auction for a credit card prime versus a cash prime — “A credit card prime, versus cash, led to a 113% percent increase in a real, binding auction (Prelec & Simester, 2001).”
