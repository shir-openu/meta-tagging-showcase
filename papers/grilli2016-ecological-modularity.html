The appealingly simple idea of a block structureterm·dom has been formalized in the measure of modularityterm·dom, Q 3 4 , calculated as the difference between observed and expected within-group interactionsterm·key, divided by the total number of interactionsterm·key. Positive values indicate that interactionsterm·key occur predominantly within-groups, while negative values that interactionsterm·key are more frequent betweenthan within-group. Modularityterm·dom has become one of the most investigated networkterm·key metrics, with applications spanning biological, social and technological networks 6 7 8 9 10 11 . Unveiling the relationship between the networkterm·key structure of biological systems and their dynamical properties has been a long-sought goal of the discipline, and many authors have hypothesized that biological networks must be shaped by evolution 12 13 14 15 and co-evolution 16 , favouring configurations yielding controlled dynamics 17 18 19 . In ecology, the idea that a modular organization would be beneficial for the local stabilityterm·dom of ecologicalterm·key communities (that is, their ability to recover from small perturbations) dates back to work on complexity and stabilityterm·key by May 20 , where he suggested ‘that our model multi-species communities [...] will do better if the interactionsterm·key tend to be arranged in blocks'. This hypothesis was challenged by a number of authors 21 22 , who produced simulations showing the opposite effect. However, recent studies found that modularityterm·dom can indeed enhance speciesterm·key persistence 23 24 , further complicating the picture. Though the relationship between block structureterm·dom and dynamics on networks has since been investigated in many fields, including epidemiology 25 , neuroscience 26 and complex systems in general 27 , a systematic classification is still lacking. Here we study how the local stabilityterm·dom of ecologicalterm·key systems is influenced by modularityterm·dom, providing new results on the theory of random matrices that can be used to draw a direct relationship between modularityterm·dom Q and local stabilityterm·dom. The mathematical results are briefly stated in the Methods section, while a calculation based on the cavity methodterm 28 29 30 31 with quaternions is carried out in the Supplementary Notes . We show that, with respect to the corresponding unstructured case, modularityterm·dom can have moderate stabilizing effects for particular parameter choices, while anti-modularity can greatly destabilize networks. The rich range of possible effects associated with the same level of modularityterm·dom stresses the fact that a given networkterm·key structure is not ‘stabilizing' or ‘destabilizing' per se , but only for particular regimes. Results Building communityterm·key matrices We study the stabilityterm·key of a community matrixterm·mth M , modelling a continuous-time, dynamical ecologicalterm·key system composed of S populations, resting at a feasible equilibrium pointterm. We remove self-interactions from the matrixterm·key (setting M ii =0), so that we can concentrate on inter-specific effects (adding intra-specific effects would not qualitatively alter the results 32 ). M is obtained by multiplying element-by-element two matrices, a matrixterm·key of interaction strengths, W , where W ij expresses the effect of speciesterm·key j on speciesterm·key i around equilibrium, and the adjacency matrixterm of an undirected graph, K . The community matrixterm·mth is therefore M = W ○ K ( Fig. 1 ). Initially, we independently sample the coefficients in W in pairs, drawing ( W ij , W ji ) from a bivariate distribution with identical marginals, defined by the mean , the variance and the correlation . By varying these parameters, we can model different types of interactionsterm·key between the speciesterm·key from preponderantly predator–prey to dominated by competition or mutualism 33 . The binary matrixterm·key K dictates ‘who interacts with whom', and is symmetric because we assume pairwise interactionsterm·key. Thus, K determines which interactionsterm·key in W are activated, and which are suppressed. Here we study the case of a block-structured K : we assume that the communityterm·key is composed of two subsystemsterm·key, of sizes αS and (1− α ) S , respectively (with α ≤1/2), and that speciesterm·key in the same subsystem interact with probability C w (within-subsystem connectanceterm·mth), while speciesterm·key in different subsystemsterm·key with probability C b (between-subsystem connectanceterm·mth). This parameterization is especially intuitive, because for C b = C w , we recover the well-studied case of a random ecologicalterm·key communityterm·key 20 33 . Hence, by varying C b and C w , we can isolate the effect of having a modular or anti-modularterm·key structure ( Fig. 2 ), with the two extreme cases being networks composed of two separate subsystemsterm·key (perfectly modular), and those in which interactionsterm·key occur exclusively between subsystemsterm·key (perfectly anti-modularterm·key or bipartite). For simplicity, we speak of a ‘modular' structure whenever C w > C b , and of an ‘anti-modularterm·key' structure when C w < C b . The case of C w = C b represents ‘unstructured' systems, such as those studied by May and other authors 20 33 . Equivalently (Methods), we can express C w and C b in terms of the overall connectanceterm·mth C (that is, the overall density of interactionsterm·key in K ) and Q , the modularityterm·dom of the networkterm·key 3 4 , defined as: where L w is the observed number of interactionsterm·key within the subsystemsterm·key, L b the number of between-subsystem interactionsterm·key and is the number of within-subsystem interactionsterm·key we would expect by chance. Effect of modularityterm·dom on stabilityterm·key We want to study the effect of modularityterm·dom on stabilityterm·key. For an unstructured matrixterm·key ( Q =0), the ratio is exactly 1. In Fig. 4 , we show the effect of modularityterm·dom on stabilityterm·key in a communityterm·key composed of 1,000 speciesterm·key, when we set C =0.2. Take the case of two equally sized subsystemsterm·key ( α =1/2), for which we derive new results allowing us to express the ratio analytically (Methods; Supplementary Information ): when μ ≥0, we have no effect of modularityterm·dom on stabilityterm·key; when μ <0, on the other hand, a bipartite structure is highly destabilizing, while a modular structure is moderately stabilizing. Both effects are stronger in the case of a negative correlation. When the two subsystemsterm·key have different sizes ( α <1/2), the stabilizing effect of modularityterm·dom found for the case of μ <0 is greatly diminished, and eventually also modularityterm·dom can become destabilizing (especially for positive ρ and ). For μ >0, any Q ≠0 is destabilizing, while for μ ≈0, a modular structure is always destabilizing, and a anti-modularterm·key structure can be stabilizing, provided that ρ is sufficiently negative. These effects hold qualitatively for different levels of C (Methods; Supplementary Figs 2–3 ), with higher connectances leading to more marked effects. Though we cannot predict the ratio in full generality for the case of α <1/2, we can treat the extreme cases of a perfectly modular and perfectly bipartite structure, and this is sufficient to understand the qualitative behaviour of all cases (Methods). The picture emerging from these results is much more nuanced and complex than what was previously hypothesized 20 21 22 : modularityterm·dom can have a moderate stabilizing effect when the two subsystem have about the same size, and the mean μ is negative or destabilizing, when μ ≥0 and the subsystemsterm·key have different sizes. Similarly, anti-modularity is highly destabilizing for μ ≠0, but can be stabilizing for μ =0. The qualitative behaviour of these systems can be understood quite simply, when considering the distribution of the eigenvalues of the block-structured matrices in the complex plane. As shown in Fig. 5 , when there are two subsystemsterm·key the spectrum of Mterm is composed of a ‘bulkterm' of eigenvalues, and up to two ‘outlier', real eigenvalues. When μ ≈0, there are no outliers, and thus stabilityterm·key is determined in all cases by the rightmost eigenvalueterm·key(s) in the bulkterm. When μ ≠0, we have only one outlier in the case of unstructured networks: if μ <0, then the outlier lies to the left of the bulkterm and thus has limited effects on stabilityterm·key; if μ >0, on the other hand, the outlier lies to the right of the bulkterm and therefore solely determines stabilityterm·key. The modular case is similar to the unstructured one, though we now have two outilers, in that both lie either to the right ( μ >0) or the left ( μ <0) of the bulkterm. In the bipartite case, however, for any μ ≠0 the spectrum presents an outlier to the right (determining stabilityterm·key) and one to the left of the bulkterm. These simple observations are sufficient to understand the very strong destabilizing effect of a bipartite structure when μ <0: in this case, the stabilityterm·key of the unstructured networkterm·key, , is determined by the bulkterm of the eigenvalues, while that of the block-structured networkterm·key, Re( λ M ,1 ), by the outlier to the right of the bulkterm ( Fig. 5 , red). When both Re( λ M ,1 ) and are determined by the bulkterm (for example, modular case with μ <0 or any structure with μ ≈0), the either stabilizing or destabilizing effect is going to be moderate. Moderate effects are also observed when both Re( λ M ,1 ) and are associated with an outlier lying to the right of the bulkterm ( μ >0). When both Re( λ M ,1 ) and are determined by the same type (bulkterm, outlier) of eigenvalueterm·key, the precise stabilizing or destabilizing effect depends nonlinearly on the parameters α , C , Q , μ , σ and ρ (Methods). To summarize, a block structureterm·dom for an otherwise random ecologicalterm·key system can help stabilization in only two cases: (a) when the structure is modular, and μ <0 (though small α or a positive ρ could reverse this effect); and (b) when the structure is bipartite, μ ≈0, and the correlation is negative. For all the other cases, the effect of a block structureterm·dom ranges from neutral to highly destabilizing. Food-web structure Clearly, ecologicalterm·key systems do not follow a random graph structure, for example, displaying a directionality in the flow of energy from producers to consumers. This directionality proved important in our previous study 36 , where we showed that when the mean of the negative effects dominates that of the positive effects, systems built according to the cascade 37 model (in which ‘larger' speciesterm·key consume ‘smaller' ones) are more likely to be stable than their random counterparts. We therefore analysed matrices constructed using a variation of the cascade modelterm, where we assign a ‘size' to each speciesterm·key and each speciesterm·key can only consume smaller speciesterm·key, and has a preference for those in the same subsystem ( Q >0), or for those in the other subsystem ( Q <0). Note that in this case, we need to set a mean for the positive effects and another one for the negative effects (Methods). Discussion We have studied the effect of a modular or anti-modularterm·key networkterm·key structure on the stabilityterm·key of an otherwise random ecologicalterm·key system. Our parameterization makes it easy to compare the effect of the networkterm·key structure with that one would obtain for unstructured, random systems such as those studied in the past 20 33 : a ratio Γ<1 stand for stabilizing and Γ>1 for destabilizing effect of networkterm·key structure. For block-structured matrices, we showed that modularityterm·dom can have a positive effect on stabilityterm·key only when (a) the system is composed of two subsystemsterm·key of about the same size and (b) the overall mean interaction strength is negative. The stabilizing effect is stronger for negative correlations. Anti-modularity, on the other hand, is typically strongly destabilizing, besides the case in which the average interaction strength is close to 0 (a well-studied case 20 33 , though of limited biological realism). When the mean interaction strength is positive, both modularityterm·dom and anti-modularity are destabilizing. Through numerical simulations, we have investigated the more complex case of an interaction between modularityterm·dom and food-web structure, and found that the results are qualitatively unchanged. The picture emerging from both simulations and mathematical analysis is much more complex than previously hypothesized. Block structureterm·dom can have an effect on the local asymptotic stabilityterm of the underlying system. However, unless we are in particular areas of the parameter space, the effect tends to be destabilizing. Our results stress the fact that, when discussing the relationship between networkterm·key structure and local stabilityterm·dom, we need to qualify our statements, as a given structure is not stabilizing or destabilizing per se , but is only so under certain specific conditions. Though we have illustrated this point by studying the modular structures, we believe this phenomenon to hold generally: any networkterm·key structure can have different effects on stabilityterm·key, depending on the choice of parameters. To reinforce this message, in Fig. 7 , we show three cases in which an empirical networkterm·key structure makes the system more or less stable than its random counterpart, depending on the parameterization of the coefficients. Practically, this means that the challenge of proving that biological networkterm·key structure emerges because of a selective process, removing configurations yielding unfavourable dynamics 17 18 19 is much harder than expected: networkterm·key structure, without estimates of the distribution of the coefficients, cannot be used to determine the effect on dynamical properties.
