phenomenonsleep attenuation of auditory responses in high-order cortex
claim-typeexistence
mechanismNREM sleep imposes a graded, cumulative attenuation that builds up along the processing hierarchy rather than a single discrete gate at one anatomical station — “The robust correlation of NREM sleep attenuation with response latency suggests that NREM sleep may exert a gradual attenuation of cortical responses, rather than imposing a discrete “gate” at a specific brain region.”
mechanismBreakdown of cortical effective connectivity in NREM sleep means signals lose more strength at each additional synapse, so responses arriving later are attenuated more — “The fact that late responses exhibit stronger attenuation in NREM sleep is in line with the notion of breakdown of cortical effective connectivity during NREM sleep ( Massimini et al., 2005 ) that may lead to greater attenuation with increasing synaptic connections.”
mechanismthe disconnection is not at the sensory front end: auditory cortex responds nearly as strongly asleep as awake, so whatever gates perception acts further downstream — “In addition, several recent studies in the auditory system showed that responses in the primary and secondary auditory cortex (AC) are comparable across wakefulness and natural sleep”
mechanismperirhinal cortex is the candidate site because it is multimodal, downstream of several primary cortices, and carries intrinsic inhibition controlling flow to the hippocampus — “Moreover, it has been shown to contain intrinsic inhibitory mechanisms that affect information flow to the hippocampus ( de Curtis and Paré, 2004 ; Pelletier et al., 2004 ).”
mechanismthe operative variable is TIME not PLACE: whatever suppresses the response acts on late activity wherever it occurs, not on a particular region — “Beyond the separation according to cortical region, response latency in each neuronal cluster was correlated with the degree of NREM sleep attenuation, such that late (>40 ms) responses in all monitored regions diminished during NREM sleep.”
mechanismthe older explanation - thalamic gating - was already in trouble, because olfactory responses that bypass the thalamus are attenuated too — “However, this view was challenged by sleep attenuation of responses in the olfactory cortex that are not relayed through thalamus ( Murakami et al., 2005 ).”

The attenuation is graded along the pathway rather than switched on at one place, which is the same claim this record's [3] on claim_relations makes about latency - a gradual attenuation rather than a discrete gate. replicationuntested
limitationStimulation was passive, so effects of behaviourally relevant or rewarded sounds were not assessed. — “Of note, we used a passive auditory stimulation scheme where sounds were not associated with specific behaviors or rewards.”
limitationThe latency-attenuation relationship found in NREM sleep did not hold in REM sleep. — “In REM sleep, attenuation did not clearly follow response latency as was the case during NREM sleep (data not shown), nor did we observe a significant difference between attenuation for neuronal pairs with different latencies recorded on same microelectrodes ( p = 0.07, 5% in early-responding neurons vs −20% in late-responding neurons).”
limitationThe proposed REM-sleep explanation is offered as speculation. — “For example, although speculative, it is possible that, during REM sleep, ongoing mnemonic activity during dreaming interferes with bottom-up sensory processes ( Nir and Tononi, 2010 ; Andrillon et al., 2016 ).”
limitationTen rats, male only, in two strains. — “Experiments were performed in 8 Long–Evans and 2 Wistar adult male rats (250–350 g) individually housed in transparent Perspex cages with food and water available ad libitum .”
limitationThe result is a correlate, and the paper says the mediating mechanism remains to be investigated. — “The robust attenuation of late responses prevalent in PRC represents a novel neural correlate of sensory disconnection during sleep, opening new avenues for investigating the mediating mechanisms.”
limitationPrior evidence about perirhinal auditory responses is inconsistent, with anaesthetised preparations failing to find them at all. — “While perirhinal neurons reliably respond to sounds during wakefulness ( Allen et al., 2007 ; Furtak et al., 2007 ), separate studies during anesthesia did not reveal significant auditory responses as one moves ventrally toward rhinal fissure ( Doron et al., 2002 ).”
limitationThe literature the study enters is thin: few studies have examined sensory responses in natural sleep at the neuronal level at all. — “Surprisingly, only few studies investigated sensory responses during natural sleep at the neuronal level ( Hennevin et al., 2007 ).”

content-observations: finding · quantity · variable · definition · key-term — grounded in the full text
findingAuditory cortex responses changed only about 10% across vigilance states, whereas perirhinal cortex responses were cut by 47% in NREM sleep and 36% in REM sleep. — “Neuronal responses in AC showed modest (∼10%) differences in response gain across vigilance states, replicating previous studies. By contrast, PRC neuronal responses were robustly attenuated by 47% and 36% during NREM sleep and REM sleep, respectively.”
findingMedian gain factors quantified the perirhinal attenuation as -47% in NREM sleep and -36% in REM sleep, with confidence intervals excluding zero. — “PRC neurons showed attenuation with median gain factor of −47% (CI = [−56%, −34%]) in NREM sleep and −36% (CI = [−43%, −30%]) in REM sleep”
findingAuditory cortex neurons showed only small median gain changes of -12% in NREM sleep and -6% in REM sleep. — “By contrast, AC neurons only exhibited modest changes, with median gain factor of −12% (CI = [−20%, −3%]), and −6% (CI = [−19%, 2%]) in NREM sleep and REM sleep, respectively.”
findingAcross all recorded neurons, the later a neuron's peak response latency, the more its response was attenuated in NREM sleep, a strong negative correlation. — “Next, we went beyond a binary separation to early- and late-responding neurons and found a significant correlation ( r = −0.55, p = 10 −15 ) between peak latency and NREM sleep gain attenuation”
findingNeurons whose latency did not match their region behaved according to latency, not anatomy: late-responding auditory cortex neurons were attenuated by 43% while early-responding perirhinal neurons were attenuated by only 3%. — “We found that late-responding AC neurons show a substantial attenuation profile resembling that observed in PRC (gain factor of −43%, CI = [−60%, −25%]), whereas early-responding PRC neurons only showed modest attenuation in NREM sleep, as was observed in AC (gain factor of −3%, CI = [−16%, 33%]”
findingEven for pairs of neurons recorded on the very same microwire, the late-responding member was attenuated roughly eightfold more in NREM sleep than the early-responding member, ruling out an electrode-position explanation. — “we found a robust (8-fold) difference in gain attenuation during NREM sleep such that early-responding neurons showed a modest average gain (−5.4 ± 9.8%) versus robust (−41 ± 8.6%) attenuation for late-responding neurons”
findingPeak response latency was bimodal and tracked region: 85% of neurons peaking under 20 ms were in auditory cortex and 89% of those peaking over 40 ms were in perirhinal cortex. — “Responses of early-responding neurons with peak latencies <20 ms were mostly (85%) observed in AC, whereas the majority (89%) of late-responding neurons with peak latencies >40 ms were found in PRC”
findingBaseline firing rates in auditory cortex were 21-26% higher in wakefulness and REM sleep than in NREM sleep. — “Analysis of baseline firing rates revealed that AC neurons exhibited 21%–26% higher firing rates during wakefulness (6.0 ± 0.6 Hz) or REM sleep (5.6 ± 0.6 Hz) compared with NREM sleep (4.4 ± 0.4 Hz, p values ≤ 10 −8 via signed-rank tests)”
findingWithin NREM sleep, late-responding neurons responded more strongly to sounds delivered during local OFF periods than during ON periods. — “whereas late-responding neurons showed significantly stronger responses when stimuli were delivered during local OFF periods (median ON/OFF gain = −41%, CI = [−44%, −19%], p = 10 −4 )”
findingShort-latency neurons tended to have narrower action potential waveforms than long-latency neurons. — “There was also a correlation ( r = −0.24, p = 0.001) between response latency and the width of AP wave”
findingAuditory cortex responses change only about 10% between waking and sleep - replicating earlier work. — “Neuronal responses in AC showed modest (∼10%) differences in response gain across vigilance states, replicating previous studies.”
findingPerirhinal responses fall by 47% in NREM and 36% in REM - the disconnection is downstream, not at the input. — “By contrast, PRC neuronal responses were robustly attenuated by 47% and 36% during NREM sleep and REM sleep, respectively.”
findingLate responses are suppressed wherever they occur, so latency rather than anatomy is the better predictor of sleep attenuation. — “In addition, we find that the response latency of each neuronal cluster (typically <20 ms in AC and >40 ms in PRC) is correlated with the degree of NREM sleep attenuation, and better predicts attenuation than does cortical region.”
findingSleep does not abolish sensory processing - some discriminative processing survives even though stimuli rarely reach perception. — “During sleep, external stimuli rarely affect perception ( Nir and Tononi, 2010 ), but it is also clear that some discriminative sensory processing persists ( Andrillon et al., 2016 ).”
