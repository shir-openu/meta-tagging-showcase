Consequences Resulting From the Commercialized Human Milkterm·dom Industry.

Undermining Breastfeedingterm·dom The existence of CHMterm·dom products on the market may infer that MOMterm·dom is insufficient or that it does not have enough nutrients ( Hassan, 2010 ). Human milkterm·key companies are free to promote and sell their products without adequate safeguards in place to protect those who are the most vulnerable. These practices are reminiscent of infant formula companies ( Rollins et al., 2023 ) and can be misleading. Human milkterm·key companies make claims, including that their products are “the best thing for babies” ( Ni-Q, 2023 ), “breastmilk is becoming rarer and more difficult to obtain” ( Wilk, n.d.-a , section 2: Our first steps towards a brighter future) and they are “transforming care for vulnerable babies by unlocking the power of human milkterm·key” ( Prolacta BioScience, 2022a , page heading). Further, human milkterm·key companies may work closely with healthcare providers and influence their clinical practices, which can negatively affect breastfeedingterm·dom. For example, in India, lactation support providers reportedly refer women with excess milk to NeoLacta, to whom they can donate their milk ( Newman & Nahman, 2022 ).

While insurance coverage can improve equitable access to human milkterm·key, not all individuals have insurance coverage, creating further inequities in distribution. Therefore, vulnerable infants most in need of human milkterm·key may be at risk of not receiving it ( Thibeau & Ginsberg, 2018 ).

Lactating individuals are encouraged to donate their milk, receiving little to no renumeration, while their expressed milk is sold at a significant profit ( Newman & Nahman, 2022 ; Subramanian, 2022 ). Reports have shown that NeoLacta donors typically have low education and receive small amounts of money or food for their expressed milk ( Newman & Nahman, 2022 ). Discussion The literature identified provides an understanding of the implications of inconsistent guidance and regulatory frameworks relative to the CHMterm·dom industry. The rapidly growing CHMterm·dom industry will likely continue to accelerate and present further challenges. The marketing tactics resembling those used by formula companies are of international relevance as CHMterm·dom could be incorporated into The Code to regulate marketing used by human milkterm·key companies.

The current compensation structure for lactating individuals who provide their milk to for-profit companies is not readily available. It has been reported that companies vary in their compensation methods, ranging from voluntary donations to remunerating with food and small amounts of money. There may be incentive for lactating individuals to express their milk for remuneration, which can potentially lead to oversupply for that individual. Exploring the impact of remuneration on maternal health outcomes would be beneficial. Further, if payment for milk is based on volume, there may be incentive for lactating individuals to dilute their milk with the intention to increase the volume, and therefore receive additional compensation. While some companies test for foreign milk proteins and DNA ( Prolacta BioScience, 2024 ), it is not a mandatory practice, therefore, the potential for diluted milk exists. More research is needed in this area to determine how compensation may affect human milkterm·key donation.

The literature reviewed did not strongly discuss the benefits of CHMterm·dom products; however, there is potential to increase the supply of human milkterm·key and specialized human milkterm·key products as well as to advance human milkterm·key research. While marketing of CHMterm·dom could be an issue if it were to dissuade mothers from breastfeedingterm·dom, it could be beneficial if it encourages NICUs to use human milkterm·key rather than infant formula. The positive aspects of CHMterm·dom products could benefit from further research.

This subject is clinically relevant for lactation support providers as well as other healthcare providers. Increased awareness of CHMterm·dom in the clinical practice setting will help lactation support providers to employ critical thinking in their practice around the use of these products. It will also help providers to educate families so that they are able to engage in better informed decision-making around infant feeding.

Limitations Due to the novelty of this subject, the literature review resulted in a limited number of studies, and there was a paucity of evidence on the prevalence of CHMterm·dom products.
