transferfrom invertebrate neuroethology: corollary discharge as a framework, imported from systems where the motor copy is a single identified neuron — “Notably, whereas the gain of stimulus-evoked responses in visual cortical pyramidal cells increases with locomotion 5 , 25 , perhaps to compensate for increased visual flow, auditory cortical responses to tones decreased during movement. This suppressive effect, which resembles corollary discharge signals described in the auditory systems of animals ranging from insects to humans 6 , 41 , may reflect a general strategy where motor-related signals transiently dampen sensitivity to predictable low-intensity sounds, enabling auditory neurons to maintain responsiveness to unexpected high-intensity stimuli”

findingJust before and during locomotion, head movements, grooming and other movements, auditory cortical excitatory neurons showed less variable membrane potentials and a slight depolarization. — “Immediately prior to and during a variety of movements including locomotion, head movements, and other body movements such as grooming, auditory excitatory neurons exhibited decreased variability in their subthreshold membrane potential fluctuations and a slight depolarization”
findingThese membrane potential changes began on average more than 200 ms before locomotion started and persisted about as long after it ended. — “By monitoring the onset and duration of locomotor bouts, we found that changes in membrane potential dynamics of auditory cortical excitatory neurons preceded locomotion on average by >200 ms and typically outlasted locomotion by a similar duration”
findingBecause the cortical changes precede movement onset by hundreds of milliseconds, they cannot be caused by sound or other sensory feedback from the movement itself and must instead be motor-related. — “The finding that changes in auditory cortical membrane potential dynamics preceded locomotion onset by several to many hundreds of milliseconds indicates they cannot be caused by sensory reafference generated by the ensuing movements, and instead could reflect a motor-related signal.”
findingUnlike visual cortex, where locomotion boosts responses, tone-evoked synaptic responses of auditory cortical excitatory neurons were significantly reduced during movement. — “In contrast to findings in the visual cortex, stimulus (i.e., tone)-evoked synaptic responses of auditory cortical excitatory neurons were significantly diminished during movements”
findingRoughly 60 percent of the movement-related suppression of tone-evoked responses originates within the auditory cortex itself rather than upstream, since optogenetically evoked thalamocortical responses were suppressed in proportion to tone responses. — “We also found that the degree of suppression of optogenetically evoked activity during movement was linearly related to the degree of suppression of tone-evoked activity during movement (slope = 0.6), indicating that approximately 60 percent of the movement-related suppression of tone-evoked responses in auditory cortical excitatory neurons arises through mechanisms local to the auditory cortex”
findingThe rising slope of thalamocortical EPSPs was unchanged between movement and rest, ruling out presynaptic depression of thalamic terminals as the cause of suppression. — “We then measured the onset slope of optogenetically-evoked thalamocortical synaptic potentials, and found no difference in the rising slope of the EPSP across movement and rest conditions, indicating that thalamic terminals in the auditory cortex are not depressed during movement”
findingThe movement-related membrane potential change reversed near -72 mV, close to the reported chloride equilibrium potential, pointing to a postsynaptic inhibitory (chloride-mediated) current. — “The movement-related change in mean membrane potential reversed in sign at ~ −72 mV, which was 3 mV depolarized relative to the average resting Vm and close to the chloride equilibrium potential reported for mouse auditory cortical pyramidal neurons”
findingAround movement, PV+ and other fast-spiking interneurons in auditory cortex increased their firing while putative excitatory neurons decreased theirs. — “Before and during movements, the firing rates of PV+ cells and other fast-spiking interneurons increased, whereas the firing rates of putative excitatory neurons decreased”
findingThe inhibitory neurons' firing rose several hundred milliseconds before movement onset and before excitatory cells dropped below baseline, consistent with inhibition leading the suppression. — “As a population, inhibitory neuron firing rates increased well before movement onset and also before the firing rates of auditory cortical excitatory cells decreased below their baseline levels (−805 ms for PV+ cells, −605 ms for VGAT+ cells, and −490 ms for excitatory cells, relative to movement onset).”
findingExcitatory neurons in secondary motor cortex increased their firing about 870 ms before movement onset, earlier than any of the auditory cortical changes. — “we determined that M2 excitatory neuron action potential activity increased prior to a variety of movements, including locomotion ( Fig. 3c ; −870 ms, relative to movement onset)”
findingOptogenetically activating M2 axon terminals in the auditory cortex of a resting mouse reproduced the movement signature: reduced membrane potential variability, reduced tone-evoked responses and slight depolarization. — “optically activating ChR2+ M2 terminals in the auditory cortex of resting mice decreased the membrane potential variability and tone-evoked responses of excitatory cells and also resulted in a slight depolarization, highly similar to the effects of movement”
findingThe terminal-activation effect was not an indirect antidromic one: it survived pharmacological silencing of M2 cell bodies with TTX. — “First, optogenetic activation of M2 terminals was equally efficacious in modulating auditory cortical dynamics when M2 cell bodies were pharmacologically silenced with the sodium channel blocker TTX”
findingThe auditory cortical change began within 7 ms of terminal stimulation, faster than the roughly 12 ms antidromic conduction time back to M2, again excluding an indirect route. — “Second, the onset of changes in auditory cortical dynamics following optogenetic activation of M2 terminals occurred more rapidly (<7 ms) than the antidromic propagation time from auditory cortex to M2 (~12 ms)”
findingSilencing ipsilateral M2 restored rest-like membrane potential dynamics in auditory cortex within about 70 ms of laser onset, before the animal actually stopped moving. — “Notably, silencing ipsilateral M2 rapidly (~70 ms after laser onset) restored rest-like membrane potential dynamics in the auditory cortex”
findingSilencing ipsilateral M2 while the mouse was still moving recovered about 40 percent of the tone-evoked response, showing M2 activity is necessary for the movement-related gain reduction. — “Presenting tones during the initial phase of M2 suppression, when auditory cortical membrane potential dynamics had transitioned to a rest-like state but before locomotion offset, revealed a strong (~40 percent) recovery of tone-evoked responses”
findingauditory cortical excitatory neurons are suppressed before and during movement, due in part to increased activity of local PV+ interneurons — “Using in vivo intracellular recordings in behaving mice, we find that excitatory neurons in the auditory cortex are suppressed prior to and during movement, due in part to increased activity of local parvalbumin-positive (PV+) interneurons.”
findingthe change led locomotion by more than 200 ms and outlasted it by about as much — “By monitoring the onset and duration of locomotor bouts, we found that changes in membrane potential dynamics of auditory cortical excitatory neurons preceded locomotion on average by >200 ms and typically outlasted locomotion by a similar duration”
findingtone-evoked synaptic responses were suppressed during movement, opposite to the visual cortex — “In contrast to findings in the visual cortex, stimulus (i.e., tone)-evoked synaptic responses of auditory cortical excitatory neurons were significantly diminished during movements”
findingPV and other fast-spiking cells increased firing before movement while excitatory cells decreased — “Before and during movements, the firing rates of PV+ cells and other fast-spiking interneurons increased, whereas the firing rates of putative excitatory neurons decreased”
findingthe firing order was M2 at -870 ms, PV at -805 ms, other inhibitory at -605 ms and excitatory at -490 ms — “As a population, inhibitory neuron firing rates increased well before movement onset and also before the firing rates of auditory cortical excitatory cells decreased below their baseline levels (−805 ms for PV+ cells, −605 ms for VGAT+ cells, and −490 ms for excitatory cells, relative to movement onset).”
findingM2 cells defined by their projection to auditory cortex behaved like the general M2 population — “Two-photon calcium imaging of GCaMP6s-expressing M2 neurons in head-fixed mice running on a treadmill revealed that M2 ACtx cells exhibited movement-related increases in fluorescence with a time course indistinguishable from that of the general M2 population”
findingactivating M2 terminals in resting mice reproduced the movement-like dynamics — “Following viral infection of AAV-ChR2 in M2 ( Extended Data Fig. 8d ), optically activating ChR2+ M2 terminals in the auditory cortex of resting mice decreased the membrane potential variability and tone-evoked responses of excitatory cells and also resulted in a slight depolarization, highly similar to the effects of movement”

findingDECOUPLING EXPERIMENT: with ipsilateral M2 silenced the cortex returned to a rest-like state while the mouse was still moving, and tones then recovered about 40 percent of their response — “The observation that silencing the ipsilateral M2 could restore rest-like auditory cortical dynamics several hundred milliseconds prior to movement offset allowed us to determine whether unilaterally silencing M2 was sufficient to enhance tone-evoked responses in the ipsilateral auditory cortex while the mouse was still moving.
