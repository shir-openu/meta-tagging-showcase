Discussion These results confirm that sounds evoke activity in visual cortexterm 2 – 7 but provide an alternative interpretation for this activity based on the widespread neural correlates of internal stateterm and body movementterm 10 , 12 – 14 , 27 . We found that different sounds evoke different uninstructed body movements such as whisking, which reflect rapid changes in the internal stateterm. Crucially, we discovered that these movements are sufficient to explain the activity evoked by sounds in visual cortexterm in our experiments. These results suggest that, at least in our experiments, the soundterm-evoked activity had a behavioral origin. Confirming this interpretation, we found that soundterm-evoked activity in visual cortexterm was independent of projections from auditory cortexterm. This result contrasts those of studies that ascribed the activity evoked by sounds in V1term to a direct input from auditory cortexterm. These studies used multiple methods: silencing of auditory cortexterm 2 , 5 , stimulation of its projections to visual cortexterm 2 , 3 or transectomyterm·mth of these projections 2 . However, the first two methods would interfere with auditory processing, and thus could affect soundterm-evoked behaviorterm. We thus opted for transectomyterm·mth 2 , which is less likely to modify behaviorterm, and we performed bilateral recordings to have an internal controlterm·mth—the uncut side—within the same mice and with the same behaviorterm. In accordance with our interpretation, these manipulations did not reduce soundterm-evoked activity in V1term. This result contrasts with the original study that introduced the transectomyterm·mth 2 , and the difference in results may be due to differences in methods. First, the previous study was conducted intracellularly and mostly in layers 2/3 (where sounds hyperpolarized cells, unlike in other layers where sounds increased spiking), whereas we recorded extracellularly mainly in layers 4-6 (and observed mainly increases in spiking). Second, the previous study performed recordings hours after the transectomyterm·mth, whereas we performed them days later. Third, the previous study anesthetized the mice, whereas we did not, a difference that can profoundly affect V1term activity 28 . Our results indicate that soundterm-evoked activity is widespread in visual cortexterm and even in the hippocampal formationterm, and in both regions, it is low-dimensionalterm. These properties echo those of movementterm-related activity, which is distributed all over the brain 13 , 14 , 22 , 27 and low-dimensionalterm 13 . We indeed found that movementterm-related neural activity even in the absence of sounds spanned essentially the same dimensions as soundterm-evoked activity. Moreover, the movements elicited by the sounds in each trialterm accurately predicted the subsequent soundterm-evoked activity. This is remarkable considering that all the movements we measured are in the face, and that our analyses are linear. It is possible that movements of other body parts, or more complex analyses, would provide even better predictions of the neural activity elicited by sounds. Our findings do not exclude the possibility of genuine auditory signals inherited by visual cortexterm from auditory cortexterm. After all, projections from auditory to visual cortexterm exist and may carry auditory signals for other stimuli or in other behavioral contexts. Moreover, some discrepancies between our results and the literature 2 – 7 could be due to differences in recording techniques and in the associated sampling biases 29 . Our V1term recordings were biased toward layers 4–6. However, layerterm 2/3 also exhibits substantial movementterm-related activity 10 – 14 , so its soundterm-evoked activity might be similarly explained by movements. Finally, it is also possible that auditory projections affect only a minor fractionterm of V1term neurons, or that they affect neurons that do not fire at high rates, and that we missed these neurons in our recordings. Distinguishing putative auditory signals from the large contribution of internal stateterm and behaviorterm will require careful and systematic controls, which are rarely performed in passively listening mice. Some studies have controlled for eye movements 5 or for overt behaviors such as licking 7 . However, even these studies may have overlooked the types of movementterm that we observed to correlate with neuronal activity, which were subtle twitches of the whiskers or the snout (Supplementary Videoterm 1 ). An exception is a study 20 that explored the contribution of whisking to soundterm-evoked activity V1term neurons in layerterm 1. In agreement with our results, this study found that whisking explains a fractionterm of those neurons’ soundterm-evoked activity. However, it did not explain all the neural activity. This discrepancy could be due to differences in recording methods (two-photonterm·mth imaging versus electrophysiologyterm·mth) in cortical layers (layerterm 1 versus layers 4–6) or in the analyses. For instance, the previous study relied on a hard thresholdterm·mth to call a response auditory versus movementterm-related, whereas we estimated the fractionterm of soundterm-evoked activity explained by movementterm. Our results do not imply that cortical activity is directly due to body movements; instead, cortical activity and body movements may both arise from changes in the internal stateterm. Consistent with this view, we found that soundterm-evoked activity in V1term is low-dimensionalterm, and thus different from the high-dimensionalterm representation of visual stimuli 13 . This interpretation would explain some of the soundterm-evoked activity in visual cortexterm under anesthesia 2 , 3 , where movements are not possible, but state changes are common and difficult to controlterm·mth and monitor 30 , 31 . Finally, these observations suggest that changes in states or behaviorterm may also explain other aspects of neural activity that have been previously interpreted as being multisensoryterm·dom 9 . Stereotypedterm body movements can be elicited not only by sounds 16 – 19 but also by images 32 – 36 and odors 33 , 37 . For instance, in our experiments, the videos evoked visual responses in both V1term and in hippocampal formationterm, and the latter could be largely explained by videoterm-evoked body movements. Such movements may be even more likely in response to natural stimuli 19 , which are increasingly common in the field. Given the extensive correlates of body movementterm observed throughout the brain 13 , 14 , 21 , 27 , 38 , these observations reinforce the importance of monitoring behavioral state and body movementterm when interpreting sensory-evoked activity.
