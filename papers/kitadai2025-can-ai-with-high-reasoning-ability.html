Object of study —

Research question — Does enhancing the reasoning abilityterm of LLM-driven agents make their ultimatum-game behaviour more human-like, or does it instead push them toward the theoretically rational but descriptively unrealistic solution?

Contribution — By varying only reasoning-related prompting (zero-shot, few-shot, chain-of-thought) across GPT models in a one-shot ultimatum gameterm, the study shows that stronger agent reasoning moves simulated behaviour toward the game-theoretic equilibrium and away from real human experimental data.

1 Introduction

Economic experimentsterm offer a powerful framework for understanding human behavior under controlled economic conditions (Smith 1976, 1982). By systematically manipulating incentives and institutional settings, researchers can test economic theories, evaluate policy interventions, and gain insights into decision-making processes, which remains a widely used and foundational methodology in economics today (Andrighetto et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2024). However, these experiments often face practical challenges: ensuring statistically significant results requires a large number of participants, which entails considerable monetary compensation and logistical effort (Long and Lang 1992; Jobjörnsson et al. 2023).

To address these limitations, researchers have begun exploring the use of large language models (LLMsterm) in multi-agent simulations (MAS). In particular, LLM-driven MAS, where each agent is powered by an LLM and called a generative agentterm·dom, has emerged as a novel and potentially transformative approach for simulating humanlike behavior at scale (Park et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2024; Oliński et al. 2024). While MAS has long been discussed in the social sciences (Axelrod 1997; Epstein 1999; Gilbert and Terna 2000), lacking the rationale on modelling agent behaviorterm previously hindered widespread adoption in experimental economics. The emergence of LLM agentsterm creates new opportunities to augment, rather than replace, traditional economic experimentsterm, potentially offering an additional methodological toolterm alongside traditional human-subject experimentsterm.

5.2 Toward the Replication of Human-like Decision Making

In this study, we investigated the reasoning abilityterm of generative agents within LLMsdriven MAS to reproduce the results of economic experimentsterm reported by (Lin et al. 2020). Building on the findings of KitadaipersName et al. (2023), we aimed to isolate the impact of reasoning abilityterm by deliberately excluding persona settingsterm, including demographic attributes such as age, gender, and personality. This decision was driven by the recognition that personaterm·mth configurations, while critical to human-like decision making, can introduce variability that hinders the effects of reasoning abilities. The randomly generated personaterm·mth attributes in previous studies likely acted as noise, limiting the systematic evaluation of reasoning-related outcomes.

The absence of persona settingsterm allowed us to focus on understanding how reasoning abilityterm influences decision-making outcomes and evaluate the alignment of simulated behavior with theoretical and experimental results. As described in Sect. 2.3, this approach revealed that higher reasoning abilityterm does not necessarily bring simulation outcomesterm closer to experimental data but aligns them more closely with theoretical predictions.

Our findings indicate that LLMsterm, even when endowed with advanced reasoning capabilities, do not naturally exhibit human-like economic behavior unless explicitly guided by prompts that incorporate social contextterm. Consequently, using LLMdriven MAS as stand-ins for real human groupsterm is currently unwarranted. Moreover, as LLMsterm continue to improve, there is a risk that such systems could misleadingly portray human behavior as excessively rational. Thus, treating LLMs as direct substitutes for human subjectsterm is a flawed approachterm. This does not preclude the possibility of employing LLMsterm in economic modeling or behavioral simulationterm·mth, but doing so effectively will require deliberate methodological scaffoldingterm. If one seeks to explore such potential within the framework of the Platonic Representation Hypothesisterm·def by Huh et al. (2024), careful design and contextualization become essential.

While our methodological choice clarifies the role of reasoning abilityterm, it also represents a limitation of this study. Persona settingsterm are known to play a critical role in shaping human-like behavior in simulations because they reflect contextual variablesterm such as culture, age, and gender, which significantly influence human decision-makingterm (Horton 2023; Phelps and Russell 2024; Han et al. 2024). Current LLMsterm inherently lack these attributes unless explicitly prompted or configured, underscoring the need for future advancements in LLM architecture and experimental design. In fact, we attribute the difference between Fig. 2a, b (the results of KitadaipersName et al. (2023)) and Figs. 3a, 4a (the results of the simulationterm·mth in this study) to the existence of persona settingsterm because the other settings of the simulations were the same, suggesting that the settings of the personas of generative agents are essential for applying LLMs-driven MAS in economic experimentsterm.

The discrepancy between experimental results and theoretical predictions in the ultimatum game has been extensively studied within the theoretical paradigms of behavioral economics. By interpreting these discrepancies through the lens of behavioral economics, we can formulate a hypothesis regarding the role of persona settingsterm. First, the human tendency to reject low offers is explained by models of inequity aversionterm, such as those proposed by Fehr and Schmidt (1999) and Bolton and Ockenfels (2000), as well as fairness equilibriumterm by Rabin (1993). Furthermore, deviations from the subgame perfect equilibrium in human decision-makingterm correspond to the concept of bounded rationalityterm by Simon (1955). This connection is further supported by our earlier observation that persona settingsterm influenced the degree of deviation from human behavior: their inclusion may act as a proxy for embedding social preferencesterm and bounded rationalityterm into in LLM-driven MAS.

Future research should systematically explore the interplay between personaterm·mth configurations and reasoning abilities to achieve a better balance between reproducing theoretical rationality and human-like behavior. This could involve designing prompts to specify demographic characteristics or fine-tuning LLMsterm on culturally diverse datasets to bridge the gap between LLM-generated behavior and human diversity.
