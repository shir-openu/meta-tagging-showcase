For example, candidate causal genes can be proposed when the overlap in association signals between a complex trait and functional data (e.g. gene expression) is a consequence of both traits sharing a causal variantterm·key, i.e. the association signals for both traits colocalize. The abundance of significant associations identified by GWASterm·mth means that chance overlap between association signals for different traits is likely 1 . Consequently, overlap does not by itself allow us to identify causal variantsterm·key 1 , 2 . Statistical colocalizationterm·mth methodologies seek to resolve this. By constructing a formal statistical model, colocalizationterm·key approaches have been successful in identifying whether a molecular trait (e.g. gene expression) and a disease trait share a causal variantterm·key in a genomic region 3 – 7 , and potentially prioritise a candidate causal gene. A number of statistical methods have been developed to assess whether association signals across a pair of traits colocalize 3 – 7 . The first of these criteria is that all the traits must share an association with one or more variants within the region. P R , which we refer to as the regional association probability, is the probability that this criterion is satisfied. By itself, this criterion does not guarantee that there is a single causal variantterm·key shared by all traits, because it could be the case that two or more traits have distinct causal variantsterm·key in strong LDterm·dom with one another. To safeguard against this, we have a second criterion that ensures the shared associations between all traits are owing to a single shared putative causal variantterm·key. P A is the probability that this second criterion is satisfied. We refer to P A as the alignment probabilityterm·mth as it quantifies the probability of alignment at a single causal variantterm·key between the shared associations. Both P R and P A have linear computational cost in the number of traits m , making a calculation of P P F C ^ possible when analysing vast numbers of traits. If the first criterion is satisfied, but the second is not, this may be because it is possible to partition the traits into clusters, such that each cluster has a distinct causal variantterm·key. HyPrColocterm·mth additionally seeks to identify these clusters. Fig. 2 Illustration of the HyPrColocterm·mth approximation. We illustrate the HyPrColocterm·mth approach with m = 2 traits. Statistical colocalizationterm·mth between traits which do not share an association region, i.e. do not have shared genetic predictors, is not possible (no colocalizationterm·key criteria satisfied). However, traits which do (satisfying criterion 1) possess the possibility. HyPrColocterm·mth first assesses evidence supporting all m traits sharing an association region, which quickly identifies utility in a colocalizationterm·key framework. HyPrColocterm·mth then assesses whether any shared association region is due to colocalizationterm·key between the traits (criteria 1 and 2) or due to a region of strong LDterm·dom between two distinct causal variantsterm·key, one for each trait (criterion 1 only). Results from these two calculations are combined to accurately approximate the PPFC .

Identification of clusters of colocalized traits If P P F C ^ falls below a threshold value, τ , we reject the hypothesis H m that all m traits colocalize to a shared causal variantterm·key. In practice, this threshold is specified by defining separate thresholds, P R * and P A * , for P R and P A , such that τ = P R * P A * (‘Methods’). If H m is rejected, HyPrColocterm·mth seeks to determine if there are values ℓ < m such that H ℓ cannot be rejected; i.e. if there exist subsets of the traits such that all traits within the same subset colocalize to a shared causal variantterm·key. Starting with a single cluster containing all m traits, our branch and bound divisive clustering algorithm (Supplementary Figs. S1a, b ) iteratively partitions the traits into larger numbers of clusters, stopping the process of partitioning a cluster of two or more traits when all traits in a cluster satisfy both P R > P R * and P A > P A * . The process of partitioning a cluster into two smaller clusters is performed using one of two criteria: (i) regional ( P R ) or (ii) alignment ( P A ) selection (‘Methods’ and Supplementary Note ). For k ≤ m traits in a cluster, the regional selection criterion has O k Q computational cost and is computed from a collection of hypotheses that assume not all traits in a cluster colocalize because one of the traits does not have a causal variantterm·key in the region. The alignment selection criterion has O k Q 2 computational cost and is computed from hypotheses that assume not all traits in a cluster colocalize because one of the traits has a causal variantterm·key elsewhere in the region ( Supplementary Note ). By default, the HyPrColocterm·mth software uses the more computationally efficient regional selection criterion to partition a cluster.

Discussion We have developed and applied a deterministic Bayesianterm·key colocalizationterm·key algorithm, HyPrColocterm·mth, for multi-trait statistical colocalizationterm·mth analyses. HyPrColocterm·mth is based on the same underlying statistical model as COLOC 2 , but enables colocalizationterm·key analyses to be performed across massive numbers of traits, owing to the insight that the posterior probability of colocalizationterm·key at a single causal variantterm·key can be accurately approximated by enumerating only a small number of putative causal configurations. HyPrColocterm·mth avoids repeated rounds of pairwise colocalizationterm·key analyses which can inflate the false negative rate and have reduced performance in identifying a shared causal variantterm·key. The HyPrColocterm·mth algorithm was validated using simulations and used to assess genetic risk shared across CHDterm·dom and related traits. Using CHDterm·dom data from 2015 17 , in which 46 regions were genome-wide significant ( P < 5 × 10 −8 ), our multi-trait colocalizationterm·key analysis identified 43 regions in which CHDterm·dom colocalized with ≥1 related trait. With this approach, we were able to identify CHDterm·dom loci that were not known at the time of the data release (2015), demonstrating the benefit of synthesising data on related traits to uncover potential new disease-associated loci 8 , 36 . A further five regions, we postulate, may be identified as CHDterm·dom loci in the future. Others have considered pleiotropic effects of CHDterm·dom loci previously 43 , but our formal colocalizationterm·key analyses are more robust, e.g. in the ABO region we show colocalizationterm·key of T2D and DBP in addition to the previously reported pleiotropic effect with LDL. We integrated eQTL and pQTL data to prioritise candidate genes at some loci, e.g. GUCY1A1 , BCAR1 and APOE .

The HyPrColocterm·mth algorithm identifies regions of the genome where there is evidence of a shared causal variantterm·key (by dissecting the genome into distinct regions) and also allows for a targeted analysis of a specific genomic locus of primary interest, e.g. when aiming to identify the perturbation of a biological pathway through the influence of a particular gene. Moreover, these region-specific analyses can highlight candidate causal genes, which will help improve biological understanding and may indicate potential drug targets to inform medicines development 44 .

We have described HyPrColocterm·mth under the assumption of at most one causal variantterm·key per trait. Future work is required to extend this methodology and algorithm to multiple-causal variants. We note that the reliability of results under the single causal variantterm·key assumption only break down when secondary causal variantsterm·key explain as much trait variation as the shared variant ( Supplementary Information ). An example of which is the expression of SH2B3 , where multiple causal variantsterm·key for the expression of this gene masks colocalizationterm·key with the CHDterm·dom signal, we discuss an approach to building colocalizationterm·key analyses which might help support the single causal variantterm·key assumption ( Supplementary Information ). We note that misspecification of LDterm·dom between causal variantsterm·key has a major impact on correct detection of multiple causal variantsterm·key in a region 45 , making a single causal variantterm·key assessment the most reliable when accurate study-level LDterm·dom information is not available. To overcome challenges when specifying the prior probability of a causal configuration, we have suggested two different parsimonious configuration priors (‘Methods’). The computational advantages of HyPrColocterm·mth make it practical to assess sensitivity of results to the specification of prior and threshold parameters as part of regular use. The HyPrColocterm·mth software includes a tool to do this, visualizing any changes in the clusters of colocalized traits identified as parameters are varied. Nevertheless, other priors may be more appropriate for particular applications.

In summary, we have developed a computationally efficient method that can perform multi-trait colocalizationterm·key on a large scale. As the size and scale of available data on genetic associations with traits increase, computationally scalable methods such as HyPrColocterm·mth will be increasingly valuable in prioritizing causal genes and revealing causal pathways.

Methods SNP association models Let Y i denote one of i = 1,2,…, m , traits assessed in a maximum of m studies, i.e. two or more traits can be measured in the same study, and G ij denote the genotype of the j th genetic variant. The function h i is typically taken as the identity function for continuous traits and the logit function for binary traits. The aim of colocalizationterm·key analyses is to identify genomic loci where there exists an G ij that is causally associated with at least two of the m traits. For each of the m traits and Q genetic variants, we assume that GWAS summary statisticsterm·mth β ^ i j and var β ^ i j are available. We use these data to perform colocalizationterm·key analyses in genomic loci.

Configuration prior probabilities We consider two different strategies for determining the priors for different hypotheses: variant-level priors and uniform priors.

Variant-level prior probabilities The prior probability space for a single genetic variant can be fully partitioned into the prior probability that the genetic variant is not associated with any of the m traits, p 0 , the prior probability that the genetic variant is associated with only the first trait, p 1 ,…, the prior probability that the SNP is associated with a subset of k traits { j 1 , j 2 ,…, j k }, p j 1 j 2 … j k , …, the prior probability that the genetic variant is associated with all traits, p 12… m . Hence, 9 p 0 + ∑ k = 1 m ∑ j 1 = 1 m ∑ j 2 > j 1 … ∑ j k > j k − 1 p j 1 j 2 … j k = 1 .

The space therefore requires the specification of 2 m prior parameters which, even for modest values of m , is computationally impractical. Following 2 , 8 we set that the prior probability to not vary by genetic variant, nor by the specific collection of colocalized traits of a given size, but by the number of colocalized traits, i.e. a SNP associated with a total of k traits has a prior probability that depends on the number k but not the specific collection of traits. To allow for the assessment of large numbers of traits we propose variant-level priors where the prior probability that a genetic variant is associated with k traits is given by, 10 p 12 … k = p ∏ i = 2 k 1 − γ i − 1 , k = 2 , … m , where p is the probability of the genetic variant being associated with one trait and γ is a parameter which controls the probability that a genetic variant is associated with an additional trait. Notably, 1 − γ is the probability of a variant being causal for a second trait given it is causal for one trait, i.e. it is the conditional colocalizationterm·key prior p c , p c = 1 − γ , 1–γ 2 is the probability it is causal for a third trait given it is causal for two traits, and so on.

This prior set-up allows evidence to grow in favour of k traits colocalizing conditional on evidence supporting k − 1 traits colocalizing ( Supplementary Information ). For example, if the first k traits are believed to share a causal variantterm·key a-priori, then the prior probability that the ( k + 1) th is also colocalized, conditional on the other k traits, increases as the number of colocalized traits k grows. The marginal prior probability of k traits colocalizing is always very small, however, which controls the false positive rateterm·mth (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Figs. S2 – 6 ; Supplementary Tables S2 – 3 ). Conditional growth limits the loss of power when assessing colocalizationterm·key across a large number of traits. A loss in power necessarily occurs when analysing large numbers of colocalized traits, due to the rapid growth in the number of hypotheses in which a subset of traits can colocalize relative to all traits colocalizing. Evidence supporting these ‘subset’ hypotheses will eventually overwhelm evidence in favour of the maximum number of truly colocalized traits for a fixed sample size (top row Fig. 4 ). S2 – 6 ) and previous investigations 14 , we recommend users set p c = 0.02, i.e. γ = 0.98, and report results from the clusters of colocalized traits which satisfy P R P A > 0.7. The BB algorithm accepts evidence supporting all m traits sharing a single causal variantterm·key if P R P A ≥ P R * P A * , after which the algorithm returns the HyPrColocterm·mth estimate of PPFC and stops. We repeat this process of branch selection and partitioning on the cluster of m − 1 traits until we identify either: (A) a cluster of traits of size k ≥ 2 whose regional and alignment statistics satisfy P R P A ≥ P R * P A * , or (B) there is one trait left in the cluster. In scenario B, the trait is deemed not colocalize with any other trait in the sample and the BB selection algorithm is repeated using m − 1 traits. The entire process is repeated until all clusters of colocalized traits, whereby each cluster of traits colocalize at a distinct causal variantterm·key, have been identified, all other traits are deemed not to share a causal variantterm·key with any other trait.
