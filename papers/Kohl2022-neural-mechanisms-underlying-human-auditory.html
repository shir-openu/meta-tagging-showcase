transferfrom cat visual cortex: the original canonical circuit description, and the pyramidal cell morphology — “An early description of a fundamental anatomical circuit of sensory processing was based on cat visual cortex (Gilbert 1983 ; Gilbert and Wiesel 1989 ) and states that excitatory cells in layer IV of the neocortex receive thalamic inputs and project to superficial layers, which in turn project to deeper layers. This fundamental layer specific relay of sensory information has since been confirmed in other sensory systems, including somatosensory (Di et al. 1990 ; Schroeder et al. 1995 ) and auditory cortex (Ojima et al. 1991 , 1992 ), with confirmation from a number of studies and species (e.g. Mitani and Shimokouchi 1985 ; Mitani et al. 1985 ; Pandya and Rosene 1993 ; Huang and Winer 2000 ; Sakata and Harris 2009 ; Atencio and Schreiner 2010 ; Ji et al. 2016 ). Importantly, the commonality of this laminar sequence of sensory information flow in different sensory areas suggests that the basic laminar structure and organization is similar across sensory regions and deterministic of the early sensory response”
transferfrom 1950s auditory psychophysics: the two rival explanations of contralateral dominance, used here as the parameters to adjust — “One explanation for contralateral dominance is that, while auditory processing is not as lateralized as, for example, somatosensory processing, those pathways between ear and cortex which cross over nevertheless appear dominant, with greater numbers of fibers and faster transmission speeds (Rosenzweig 1951 ; Kimura 1961 ). This feature is most likely to account for temporal differences, as stronger pathways may lead to faster transmission of information, resulting in earlier contralateral AEF peaks. However, it is not immediately apparent that the integrity of fiber tracts alone necessarily leads to responses of greater magnitude. A second explanation states that larger areas of cortex are activated by contralateral stimulation (Rosenzweig 1951 ; Gross et al. 1967 ; Pantev et al. 1986 ), which would lead to greater amplitude responses. Gross et al. ( 1967 ) found that the cortical area in which responses could be evoked was larger when tones were presented contralaterally as opposed to ipsilaterally. Importantly, these explanations are based on evidence almost exclusively from animal models, and it is unknown if they can account for the contralateral dominance observed in human macroscale AEFs.”
mechanismA distal feedback drive arriving at the supragranular dendrites excites layer V pyramidal cells and pushes current down the apical dendrites, and the strength of that excitation sets the depth of the N100m trough. — “Since distal layer V pyramidal excitation pushes currents down the dendrites, creating the N100m trough, decreasing the excitatory inputs to these cells leads to a smaller N100m magnitude as seen in left hemisphere AEFs.”

mechanismStronger, faster crossed auditory pathways deliver input earlier and recruit more cortical cells, which the model represents as shorter input latencies plus a larger network scale, producing contralateral dominance. — “We expected the strength of a pathway to correlate primarily with the speed of the relay of information, which corresponds to the input time parameters in HNN.”
mechanisma proximal feedforward drive produces the P50m, a distal feedback drive to supragranular layers produces the N100m, and a second proximal drive produces the P200m — “Motivated by this consistency along with the homology of canonical cortical circuitry in sensory areas built into HNN, we applied the input sequence of the existing SEF model distributed with HNN as a starting point for our AEF simulation (namely the ‘default.param’ parameter file). This sequence consisted of a proximal input, followed by a distal input, followed by a second subsequent proximal input (Jones et al. 2007 ; Neymotin et al. 2020 ), and represents a canonical feedforward, feedback, feedforward pattern of activation.”

Specifically, we found that by decreasing the AMPA mediated excitation of layer V pyramidal cells by the distal drive (see Table 1 and Table S2), and keeping all other parameters fixed between hemispheres, we were able to reproduce the AEF waveform associated with the left hemisphere (Table 1 ). mechanismand the contralateral dominance by two changes taken from the literature - a larger active network and earlier input times — “After establishing mechanisms that can reproduce the contralateral AEFs in both the left and right hemisphere, we next tested the specific hypotheses based on prior literature that differences between contralateral and ipsilateral AEFs emerge from differences in the size of the active network (Rosenzweig 1951 ; Gross et al. 1967 ), and alterations in the timing of the exogenous input to the network (Tunturi 1946 ; Kimura 1961 ). Introduction

Brain activity evoked by auditory stimulation has been studied for many decades and remains not only commonly used in cognitive neuroscience (Wagner et al. 2017 ; ParviainenpersName et al. 2019 ) but also clinically relevant (Paulraj et al. 2015 ; Samatra et al. 2020 ). In humans, the sequence of neural activation evoked by auditory stimulation can be measured using electroencephalography (EEGterm) or magnetoencephalography (MEGterm), and the surface-recorded responses are typically divided into three categories based on their latencyterm. Broadly speaking, early responses (within ~ 10 ms) primarily reflect brain stem activity, middle-latency auditory responses (10–50 ms) are thought to reflect processing in thalamocortical structures, and late-latency responses (50–250 ms) are associated with cortical activity (Picton et al. 1974 ). Late latencyterm responses are elicited in the primary auditory cortexterm and surrounding areas and typically consist of components labelled P50m-N100m-P200m (or P1-N1-P2), which peak at around 50, 100 and 180 ms respectively (Eggermont and Ponton 2002 ). Here, we refer to this cortical P50m-N100m-P200m sequence as auditory evoked fields (AEF)term·def. Although the P50m-N100m-P200m waveform is commonly found in response to any auditory event, the shape and amplitudeterm of its components depends on a number of characteristics of the evoking stimuli, such as their acoustic complexity, intensity, duration, and frequency (Picton 2011 ). Here, we use simple pure tones at 1 kHz, which have been shown to produce robust N1 responses and are neutral in terms of linguistic associations, and adopted a paradigm that has been successfully used to evidence the fundamental response properties of the humanterm auditory processing pathway (Mäkelä et al. 1994 ; Salmelin et al. 1999 ).

While AEFs are commonly used as correlates for a number of cognitive processes (Shahin et al. 2003 ; Fan et al. 2017 ), developmental stages (ParviainenpersName et al. 2019 ; van Bijnen et al. 2019 ), and clinical observations (Goodin et al. 1978 ; Stephen et al. 2017 ), several fundamental questions about their neural origin remain unanswered. A deeper understanding of the cellular and circuit mechanisms generating AEFs is essential to understanding the role of AEFterm components in cognition and to developing treatments based on regularizing neuropathological AEFterm features.

Insights into the biophysical mechanisms underlying auditory processing have been gained using invasive recordings in animalterm models. An early description of a fundamental anatomical circuit of sensoryterm processing was based on cat visual cortex (Gilbert 1983 ; Gilbert and Wiesel 1989 ) and states that excitatory cells in layer IVterm of the neocortex receive thalamicterm inputs and project to superficial layers, which in turn project to deeper layers. This fundamental layer specific relay of sensoryterm information has since been confirmed in other sensoryterm systems, including somatosensory (Di et al. 1990 ; Schroeder et al. 1995 ) and auditory cortexterm (Ojima et al. 1991 , 1992 ), with confirmation from a number of studies and speciesterm (e.g. Mitani and Shimokouchi 1985 ; Mitani et al. 1985 ; Pandya and Rosene 1993 ; Huang and Winer 2000 ; Sakata and Harris 2009 ; Atencio and Schreiner 2010 ; Ji et al. 2016 ). Importantly, the commonality of this laminar sequence of sensoryterm information flow in different sensoryterm areas suggests that the basic laminar structure and organization is similar across sensoryterm regions and deterministic of the early sensoryterm response, particularly in primary sensoryterm cortices (Douglas and Martin 2004 ; Barbour and Callaway 2008 ; Atencio and Schreiner 2010 ).

Although animalterm models provide insight into cell and circuit-level dynamics underlying sensoryterm evoked responses, determining if and how this information translates to macroscale humanterm signals, as measured with EEGterm and MEGterm, is a non-trivial problem. In recent years, computational neuroscience has begun to bridge the gap between macroscale humanterm brain signals and network-dynamics (Kiebel et al. 2009 ; Sanz Leon et al. 2013 ; Hagen et al. 2018 ). One approach to achieve this is proposed by the modeling software Humanterm Neocortical Neurosolver (HNNterm; Neymotin et al. 2020 ), namely to create a modelterm·mth of neuronal circuitry informed by invasive animalterm recordings and use this modelterm·mth to simulate humanterm macroscale brain signals based on their biophysical origin. The modelterm·mth underlying HNNterm consists of a canonicalterm·dom neocortical circuit, with individual excitatory and inhibitoryterm neurons across the cortical layers, and layer specific thalamocortical and cortico-cortical inputterm pathways. The primary electrical currents generating EEGterm and MEGterm signals are simulated from the intracellular current flow in the long and spatially aligned cortical pyramidalterm neuron apical dendrites (for a detailed description, see Methods , supplement S1, or visit hnnterm.brown.edu). HNNterm has been applied to study the cellular and circuit level neural mechanism underlying a number of commonly measured EEGterm and MEGterm signals, such as alpha and beta frequency oscillations (JonespersName et al. 2009 ; Ziegler et al. 2010 ; Sherman et al. 2016 ), gamma oscillations (Lee and JonespersName 2013 ), and somatosensory evoked fields (SEFterm; JonespersName et al. 2007 , 2009 ; Ziegler et al. 2010 ; Sliva et al. 2018 ). Here, we build from the prior literature and known commonalities in the cortical structure and information relay in sensoryterm areas to apply HNNterm to study the circuit mechanisms underlying MEGterm measured AEFs. We focus on interpreting the neural mechanisms generating (1) the AEFterm waveform in response to a simple auditory tone (ParviainenpersName et al. 2019 ), including the P50m-N100m-P200m sequence, (2) observed differences in AEFs in the right and left hemisphereterm, and (3) between contralateralterm and ipsilateralterm tone presentations. We hypothesized that the laminar organization and relay of sensoryterm information is preserved across sensoryterm areas, and that, as a result, AEFs could be simulated using similar cortical inputterm sequences as those shown to reproduce SEFs in our prior studies. Note that, while the laminar organization and exogenous drives simulated in HNNterm are based on animalterm work (see Discussion ), previous work has shown that the resulting simulations can be applied to humanterm data and support canonicalterm·dom inputterm sequences (JonespersName et al. 2007 , 2009 ; Ziegler et al. 2010 ; Sliva et al. 2018 ; Neymotin et al. 2020 ). Building from this hypothesisterm, we then investigated the observed phenomenon that the AEFterm recorded over the right hemisphereterm, and specifically the N100mterm component, is often larger in amplitudeterm compared to the left AEFterm (Peronnet et al. 1974 ; Mononen and Seitz 1977 ; Wolpaw and Penry 1977 ; Hine and Debener 2007 ; Howard and Poeppel 2009 ; Kimura 2011 ; Shaw et al. 2013 ). The biophysical origin of this difference is unknown, and we apply HNNterm to develop targeted predictions on neural mechanisms that may generate these differences.

Lastly, we examine the mechanisms underlying AEFterm differences that depend on the site of auditory stimulation, i.e. between contralateralterm and ipsilateralterm responses. In early AEFterm research, it was found that when simple sounds were presented monaurally, responses in the hemisphereterm contralateralterm to the sound presentation were more pronounced than responses in the ipsilateralterm hemisphereterm (Tunturi 1946 ; Rosenzweig 1951 ). This increased magnitude in contralateralterm auditory responses has since been confirmed in a number of experimental paradigms and recording modalities (Peronnet et al. 1974 ; Andreassi et al. 1975 ; Taub et al. 1976 ; Wolpaw and Penry 1977 ; Reite et al. 1981 ; Pantev et al. 1986 , 1998 ; Yoshiura et al. 1994 ; Loveless et al. 1994 ; Mäkelä et al. 1994 ; Jäncke et al. 2002 ; Devlin et al. 2003 ; Petkov et al. 2004 ; ParviainenpersName et al. 2019 ). In addition, the contralateralterm response has been described as not only larger in magnitude, but also as faster than its ipsilateralterm equivalent (Mononen and Seitz 1977 ; Wolpaw and Penry 1977 ; Mäkelä et al. 1994 ; Pantev et al. 1998 ). However, this temporal effect appears to be less clear than the effect on magnitude, as several studies failed to confirm any temporal differences (Tunturi 1946 ; Andreassi et al. 1975 ; Yoshiura et al. 1994 ).

While there is strong evidence suggesting that the contralateralterm dominanceterm effect is robust (although there is some debate, e.g. Hine and Debener 2007 ), few studies have focused on the biophysical mechanisms underlying this phenomenon. These studies guide our neural modelterm·mth investigation of observed contralateralterm AEFterm dominanceterm in our data. One explanation for contralateralterm dominanceterm is that, while auditory processing is not as lateralized as, for example, somatosensory processing, those pathways between ear and cortex which cross over nevertheless appear dominant, with greater numbers of fibers and faster transmission speeds (Rosenzweig 1951 ; Kimura 1961 ). This feature is most likely to account for temporal differences, as stronger pathways may lead to faster transmission of information, resulting in earlier contralateralterm AEFterm peaks. However, it is not immediately apparent that the integrity of fiber tracts alone necessarily leads to responses of greater magnitude. A second explanation states that larger areas of cortex are activated by contralateralterm stimulation (Rosenzweig 1951 ; Gross et al. 1967 ; Pantev et al. 1986 ), which would lead to greater amplitudeterm responses. Gross et al. ( 1967 ) found that the cortical area in which responses could be evoked was larger when tones were presented contralaterally as opposed to ipsilaterally. Importantly, these explanations are based on evidence almost exclusively from animalterm models, and it is unknown if they can account for the contralateralterm dominanceterm observed in humanterm macroscale AEFs. We therefore applied our HNNterm modeling framework to test the hypothesisterm that changes in modelterm·mth parameters that represent the speed of activation of the cortex, and number of cells contributing to the signal, could account for the observed differences in contralateralterm vs. ipsilateralterm MEGterm measured AEFs.

In summary, the goals of the current study were threefold. (1) We aimed to simulate an AEFterm waveform in HNNterm and hypothesized that the cortical inputs underlying the waveform share important features with those previously shown to be involved in other sensoryterm evoked responses, due to the canonicalterm·dom structure of sensoryterm neocortex. (2) We used HNNterm to explore which biophysical mechanisms underlie the commonly observed difference between right hemisphereterm and left hemisphereterm AEFs. (3) We tested the hypothesisterm that differences in the scale of the underlying network as well as the latencies of the inputs into the network could account for the previously reported dominanceterm of contralateralterm AEFs.

Methods

Participants

Participant recruitment, MEGterm recording and source-localization were performed for a previous study. For a more detailed description, see ParviainenpersName et al. ( 2005 , 2019 ).

Ten neurotypical participants (age 23–39; five females) were recruited for MEGterm recording.

Auditory Stimulation

Participants were presented with simple 1 kHz sine wave tones, 50 ms in duration with 10 ms fade-in and fade-out time, created in Sound Edit (MacroMedia, San Francisco, CA, USA). The tones were presented alternatingly to the left and right ear (ParviainenpersName et al. 2019 ). Tones were separated by inter-stimulus-intervals varying between 0.8 and 1.2 s and were presented at 60 dB above the subjective hearing level.

MEGterm Acquisition and Analysis

MEGterm was recorded using a helmet-shaped 306-channel whole-head system (Vectorview, Neuromag Ltd, Helsinki, Finland), band-pass filtered at 0.03–200 Hz and sampled at 600 Hz. Segments of − 200 ms to 800 ms relative to auditory stimulus onset were averaged off-line for each participant, separately for left and right ear sounds. Electro-oculogram (EOG) was recorded and epochs in which the EOG channel exceeded a threshold of 150 µV were considered contaminated by blinks or saccades, and were excluded from the averages. After artifact rejection, an average of 102 (± 4)/105 (± 4) (mean ± SD) epochs remained per subject for left/right ear sounds (see ParviainenpersName et al. 2005 , 2019 ).

The resulting averages were source-localized using equivalent current dipole (ECD)term·def modeling, where ECDs represent the average distribution of electric current within a cortical patch, giving an estimate of the location, strength, and direction of local current flow (Hämäläinen et al. 1993 ). For determining the ECD, the temporally varying field pattern was visually inspected between 80 and 120 ms relative to the auditory stimulus to identify symmetric dipolar fields, indicative of separable, active neuron population. The N100mterm peak visible at the sensor-level was always associated with salient dipolar field pattern (cf. Fig. 2 d, e). ECDs were determined for each participant (one dipoleterm per hemisphereterm) from a standard subset of 46 planar gradiometers that covered the 100 ms auditory field pattern (Fig. 2 ). The determined ECDs in each hemisphereterm were then used to account for the MEGterm signals by keeping ECD location and orientations fixed and varying only their amplitudeterm. For further analysis in this study, source-localized AEFs were segmented into 0 ms to 250 ms epochs, relative to the auditory stimulus, and averaged across participants. The same pair of ECDs, at an individual level, accounted for the activation patterns evoked by both ipsilateralterm and contralateralterm stimulation. Moreover, the ECD that was fitted to the 100 ms response (N100mterm) was also able to account for the preceding response at 50 ms (P50mterm). The current direction of the N100mterm peak was into the cortex, and the P50mterm and P200mterm have amplitudes in the opposite direction representing current out of the cortex.

Source localization is an important step since we intended to modelterm·mth the AEFterm waveforms using the Humanterm Neocortical Neurosolver software (HNNterm, see below), which is designed to study the origin of source localized signals. Inverse solution methods applied to sensor-level MEGterm (or EEGterm) estimate the primary current generator of the sensor data. These primary currents are estimated as current dipoles in units of current × distance (Ampere × meters) and known to be generated by intracellular post-synaptic current flow in the long and spatially aligned cortical pyramidalterm neuron dendrites (Ikeda and Shibasaki 1992 ; Hämäläinen et al. 1993 ; Okada et al. 1997 ; Murakami et al. 2003 ; Murakami and Okada 2006 ; Sacchet et al. 2015 ; Neymotin et al. 2020 ). As detailed below, HNNterm simulates these primary currents from the modelterm·mth pyramidalterm neurons and produces dipoleterm outputs in units of ampere–meters (Am), allowing for direct comparisons between simulated and empiricalterm AEFs. Currents that flow into the cortex (e.g. N100mterm) correspond to current flow down the pyramidalterm neuron dendrites in HNNterm, as detailed further below.

Statistical Analysis

In order to quantify the differences in the AEFterm waveform between left and right, as well as between contralateralterm and ipsilateralterm hemispheres, we focused on the analysis of the N100mterm response. Although we do not expect AEFterm differences to be expressed exclusively in the N100mterm, this component is typically the most prominent of the AEFterm components, and therefore lends itself to quantification. To this end, we conducted a total of four 2 × 2 repeated-measures ANOVAs with factors Tone Presentation (contralateralterm/ipsilateralterm) and Hemisphereterm (right/left) to determine differences in N100mterm amplitudeterm, N100mterm latencyterm, P50m-N100m slopeterm, and N100m-P200m slopeterm. The N100mterm trough was defined per participant average as the absolute signal maximum during the time interval from 80 to 120 ms relative to the auditory stimulus onset. The P50m-N100m, and N100m-P200m slopes were determined by fitting straight lines to each participant’s average waveformterm·stat between 70–90 and 110–130 ms respectively.

We applied the same ANOVAs to the modelterm·mth simulationterm·mth (see below), by running 10 simulations per modelled condition as a source of variabilityterm. While the reduced variabilityterm of simulations compared to humanterm MEGterm data means that these statistics are not directly comparable to those of the empiricalterm data, they nevertheless give insight into whether specific qualitative features, such as differences in slopes or latencies, are reproduced by the modelterm·mth.

Modelterm·mth

The Humanterm Neocortical Neurosolver (HNNterm)

We used the computational neural modeling software Humanterm Neocortical Neurosolver (HNNterm; Neymotin et al. 2020 ) to study the neural mechanisms generating the observed source localized, grand-average AEFterm waveforms. HNNterm is an open-source software (for tutorials and detailed descriptions of the modelterm·mth, please visit http://hnnterm.brown.edu), which simulates the primary electrical currents underlying macro-scale EEGterm and MEGterm data, based on their biophysical origin by modeling neocortical cellular and circuit-level activity. The modelterm·mth contains multi-compartment pyramidalterm neurons as well as single-compartment inhibitoryterm interneurons (basketterm cells) in supragranular layers (layer II/IIIterm) and infragranular layers (layer Vterm, see Fig. 1 ). The morphology of the pyramidalterm neurons was modelled based on cat visual cortex pyramidalterm neurons (Bush and Sejnowski 1993 ), and adjusted in accordance with anatomical findings in the humanterm brain (Geyer et al. 1997 ; Fischl and Dale 2000 ; Elston et al. 2001 ). The intracellular electrical current flow in the long and spatially aligned apical dendrites of the pyramidalterm neurons across the infragranular and supragranular layers are the main contributors to macroscale primary current dipoles that generate macroscale signals that can be observed on the scalp (Murakami and Okada 2006 ). The cells are connected with glutamatergic and GABAergic synapses, and each cell’s activity is simulated using Hodgkin-Huxley dynamics (Fig. 1 a). The primary current dipoleterm is calculated by summing the intracellular current flow across the network of pyramidalterm neurons (see red and green arrows in Fig. 1 c and d). Importantly, the current dipoleterm generated in HNNterm is expressed in units of ampere-meters (Am), the same units as those estimated by source localization methods of EEGterm and MEGterm data, enabling one to one comparison between modelterm·mth and empiricalterm data. For computational tractability, a reduced network size is simulated and a scalingterm factor is multiplied by the aggregate current dipoleterm to estimate the size of the network contributing to the recorded data, as detailed below. To assess the correspondence between averaged evoked responses and modelled dipoles, simulations are smoothed (30 ms Hamming window, see Table S2). There are many more sources of variabilityterm in the larger network contributing to the recorded humanterm data, such as spike variabilityterm across the network and individual subject differences. We assume high-frequency components created by this variabilityterm are more likely to average out in the larger network and multi-subject averages contributing to the recorded AEFs than in modelterm·mth simulations. We therefore apply smoothingterm·mth to modelterm·mth simulations to allow for a direct comparison between simulationterm·mth and data (see also Fig. S2). We also simulate N = 10 trials per example, each of which has some intrinsic variabilityterm, and average across trials.

The baseline network consists of 100 pyramidalterm neurons per layer (Fig. 1 b). A scalingterm parameterterm is then applied by multiplying the dipoleterm simulated by the baseline network by a constant. This constant represents the number of neurons contributing to a given recorded signal, assuming the signal represents the summed activity of a larger population of synchronous pyramidalterm neurons. Since a typical MEGterm/EEGterm response has a magnitude of 10-100nAm, and a single pyramidalterm neuron contributes approximately 0.2 pAm, between 50,000 and 500,000 cells are contributing to a typical macroscale signal, corresponding to a scalingterm factor of between 250 and 25,000 (Murakami and Okada 2006 ; Neymotin et al. 2020 ).

In order to simulate macroscale MEGterm/EEGterm signals, the network modelled in HNNterm is activated through exogenous excitatory synaptic inputs generated by predefined trains of action potentials that contact layer specific post-synaptic targets via two canonicalterm·dom inputterm pathways, which have previously been shown in animalterm models (Rockland and Pandya 1979 ; Friedman and JonespersName 1980 ; Kulics and Cauller 1986 ; Cauller and Kulics 1991 ; Douglas and Martin 2004 ). We refer to the two types of inputs used here as proximalterm or feedforwardterm and distalterm or feedbackterm inputs. Feedforwardterm drives represent signals that reach the cortex from the lemniscal thalamus to granular layers, which then propagate directly to supragranular and infragranular layers where they effectively target the proximalterm dendrites of the pyramidalterm neurons (Fig. 1 c). Feedbackterm drives represent inputs from non-lemniscal thalamus or cortico-cortical connections that target the distalterm dendrites in supragranular layers (Fig. 1 d). The level of detail included in HNNterm’s modelterm·mth enables simulationterm·mth of the macroscale current dipoles along with microscale circuit information, including layer specific spiking activity in individual cells. Detailed descriptions of the modelterm·mth underlying HNNterm can be found in the Supplementary Materials (S1: Supplemental Modelterm·mth Description).

Although the cortical columnterm modelled in HNNterm was originally generated to account for the anatomy and behavior of primary somatosensory cortex (JonespersName et al. 2007 , 2009 ), it is based on canonicalterm·dom features shared between different neocortical circuits and hence can be applied to interpret signals in other cortical areas (Neymotin et al. 2020 ). This is particularly the case for sensoryterm areas, as the relay of sensoryterm information has been shown to follow remarkably stereotypical patterns across modalities (Douglas and Martin 2004 ; Barbour and Callaway 2008 ).

Since HNNterm is a complex modelterm·mth with a large number of parameters, many of which interact, we cannot rule out that some alternative parameterterm sets could not reproduce similar results (see “ Discussion ” section). HNNterm was designed to be a hypothesisterm development and testing tool. The hypothesisterm testing here was motivated from our prior studies of sensoryterm evoked responses, which provided the baseline assumptions for the pattern of exogenous proximalterm and distalterm drives underlying an evoked response, and prior literature on neural mechanisms underlying hemispheric dominanceterm (see “ Introduction ” section). While we cannot show that any given modelterm·mth provides a uniqueterm solution, it is important to note that the dipoleterm shape produced by HNNterm depends crucially on the underlying assumptions and we can examine the dipoleterm response under alternative hypotheses. To illustrate this, we show models using alternative assumptions for two of our examples (see Fig. 5 and supplement S2, Fig. S1).

A Sequence of Feedforward-Feedback-Feedforward Activation of the Cortical Circuit Reproduces AEFterm in HNNterm

The tone evoked AEFs in Fig. 2 exhibit peak timings and polarities that are similar to source localized SEFs evoked by brief perceptual threshold level tactile stimuli observed in our prior studies (e.g. JonespersName et al. 2007 , 2009 ), where there is an initial small amplitudeterm positive peak (confirmed to correspond to current flow out of cortex), followed by a prominent negative peak (current flow into cortex), followed by a subsequent positive peak. Motivated by this consistency along with the homology of canonicalterm·dom cortical circuitry in sensoryterm areas built into HNNterm, we applied the inputterm sequence of the existing SEFterm modelterm·mth distributed with HNNterm as a starting point for our AEFterm simulationterm·mth (namely the ‘default.param’ parameterterm file). This sequence consisted of a proximalterm inputterm, followed by a distalterm inputterm, followed by a second subsequent proximalterm inputterm (JonespersName et al. 2007 ; Neymotin et al. 2020 ), and represents a canonicalterm·dom feedforwardterm, feedbackterm, feedforwardterm pattern of activation. This pattern of activation was able to account for rough features of the AEFterm waveform. To produce a closer fit to the AEFterm data we began by adjusting only the parameters defining the strength and timing of each inputterm (all inputterm parameters are displayed in Table 1 ), as well as the scale of the waveform. We first hand-tuned these parameters until there was visually close agreement between modelterm·mth and data, and subsequently used the automated parameterterm optimization feature in HNNterm to improve the modelterm·mth fit further. The optimization tool automatically adjusted parameters controlling inputterm timing and strengths to minimize the error (root mean squared error; RMSEterm·stat) between the simulated and empiricalterm dipoleterm waveforms (Neymotin et al. 2020 ). This was initially performed for the AEFterm recorded over the contralateralterm, right hemisphereterm. Briefly, the inputterm sequence affects the network in the following way: first, a feedforwardterm inputterm reaches the proximalterm dendrites, as well as inhibitoryterm interneurons of layer II/IIIterm and layer Vterm. This inputterm is thought of as feedforwardterm sensoryterm information reaching the auditory cortexterm via the lemniscal thalamus. It reaches the network around 47 ms after the onset of the auditory stimulus, strongly driving layer II/IIIterm cells (Table 1 ). This timing is broadly in line with previous findings, suggesting that AEFterm components occur after auditory signals reach the cortex at around 50 ms (Picton et al. 1974 ). This inputterm causes both pyramidalterm and basketterm cells in layer II/IIIterm to fire, with pyramidalterm firing quickly inhibited by the inhibitoryterm basketterm cells (Fig. 4 f). The spiking in the pyramidalterm neurons creates backpropagation of current flow up the pyramidalterm apical dendrites towards the surface of the cortex, leading to the P50mterm peak seen in the AEFterm (Fig. 4 e).

While the effects of the proximalterm inputterm are ongoing, a feedbackterm inputterm arrives at the distalterm dendrites at around 81 ms (although with a wider temporal distribution, Fig. 4 d), strongly activating layer II/IIIterm basketterm and pyramidalterm cells and layer Vterm pyramidalterm cells and inducing the N100mterm peak. This drive is presumed to represent inputs from either cortico-cortical, or non-lemniscal thalamocortical connections. The distalterm drive induces several changes in the network dynamics, the net effect of which is a downward deflecting current. Excitatory synaptic inputs on distalterm apical dendrites of layer II/IIIterm and layer Vterm pyramidalterm neurons push the current flow down (Fig. 1 d). At the same time, somatic inhibition mediated by activation of basketterm cells in both layers (note, the layer Vterm baskets cells are driven by the layer II/IIIterm excitatory connections to layer Vterm, Fig. 1 a) pulls current further down the pyramidalterm neuron dendrites. In addition, activation of calciumterm dynamics, triggered by layer Vterm pyramidalterm excitation pulls current down towards infragranular layers. The distalterm drive also generates spiking activity in the layer Vterm pyramidalterm neurons, which leads to backpropagation, i.e. current flow in the opposite direction up the dendrites. However, the size of this effect is smaller and not visible in averaged and smoothed data (see Fig. S2 and “ Methods ” section) and the overall effect is current flow down the dendrites, leading to a large N100mterm trough (Fig. 4 e).

Lastly, as the spiking activity in the network would begin to relax back to baseline after the distalterm drive, a second proximalterm inputterm reaches the network at around 151 ms, driving primarily layer II/IIIterm and layer Vterm basketterm cells, and some pyramidalterm cells, causing continuous basketterm firing, with burst-like behavior occurring at around 200 ms (Fig. 4 f). This activation of postsynaptic excitatory synapses on the basal dendrites of the pyramidalterm neurons again pushes current flow towards the surface of the cortex. Additionally, the layer II/IIIterm basketterm cells inhibit the distalterm dendrites of the layer Vterm pyramidalterm neurons (Fig. 1 a), helping to pull current flow up the dendrites toward the cortical surface. Together these effects create the P200mterm peak in the AEFterm.

Dividing the net current dipoleterm into its layer specific components shows that the shape of the AEFterm simulationterm·mth is largely driven by responses in layer Vterm, while layer II/IIIterm responses contribute only to the earliest component (see inserts in Fig. 4 e, and Fig. S2b).

We also calculated the simulated firing rates of each neuronal population by averaging the number of action potentials over trials and cells. We found the highest firing rates in basketterm cells (layer Vterm = 13.97 spikes/s; layer II/IIIterm = 13.46 spikes/s), followed by layer Vterm pyramidalterm neurons (10.60 spikes/s), and low firing rates in layer II/IIIterm pyramidalterm neurons (1.85 spikes/s, cf. Fig. 4 f). Although firing rates were not considered as part of the modelterm·mth fitting procedures, these firing rates appear to be within physiological ranges (Wallace and Palmer 2008 ; Atencio and Schreiner 2010 ), further supporting the model-derived predictions.

The targeted differences we aimed to create were the significantly smaller N100mterm amplitudeterm (Figs. 2 c and 3 ). Since distalterm layer Vterm pyramidalterm excitation pushes currents down the dendrites, creating the N100mterm trough, decreasing the excitatory inputs to these cells leads to a smaller N100mterm magnitude as seen in left hemisphereterm AEFs. We refer to the modelterm·mth in which only this parameterterm was changed to account for left AEFs as the ‘Manually Fitted Modelterm·mth’, and used automated parameterterm optimization to further improve the modelterm·mth fit. The differences that we targeted to reproduce in the modelterm·mth were the decreased N100mterm amplitudeterm, and smaller P50m-N100m and N100m-P200m slopes as observed in the empiricalterm data (Figs. 2 and 3 ). Since the latencies of all inputs were adjusted equally, this manipulation did not change the shape of the dipoleterm, but shifted it along the time axis. Although latencyterm differences between contralateralterm and ipsilateralterm AEFs were not statistically significantterm in our data, N100mterm latencies were slightly but consistently larger in ipsilateralterm AEFs (Fig. 3 ). Discussion

Auditory evoked fields are a commonly used measurement in humanterm studies of healthy cognitive processing and as biomarkers of neuropathology, yet the biophysical mechanisms underlying these scalp-recorded responses are not yet fully understood. While humanterm AEFterm studies and laminar recordings of the auditory cortexterm in animalterm models often proceed in relative isolation, here, we connected the information of the circuitry architecture, provided by animalterm models, with humanterm MEGterm recordings using biophysically principled computational neural modeling. We used the Humanterm Neocortical Neurosolver (HNNterm), a software whose foundation is a modelterm·mth of a canonicalterm·dom neocortical circuit, which allows users to define inputs to activate the network and simulates the primary electrical currents underlying scalp-recorded fields or potentials (JonespersName et al. 2007 ; Neymotin et al. 2020 ), to develop and test predictions on the neural mechanisms underlying two well-established characteristics in the AEFterm response, namely the lateralizationterm·dom effect in which responses recorded over the right hemisphereterm display stronger magnitudes in response to simple auditory stimuli than those recorded over the left hemisphereterm (Hine and Debener 2007 ; Howard and Poeppel 2009 ; Kimura 2011 ), as well as the phenomenon that AEFs in response to contralaterally presented tones show larger, and often faster, responses than ipsilaterally presented tones (Tunturi 1946 ; Rosenzweig 1951 ; Kimura 1961 ; Pantev et al. 1998 ).

We were able to modelterm·mth the AEFterm waveform elicited by a brief monaural contralateralterm tone presentation using the same inputterm sequence that has previously been shown to simulate somatosensory evoked responses (JonespersName et al. 2009 ), with parameters tuned to match the AEFterm data. According to this sequence, auditory responses can be accounted for by an initial excitatory thalamocortical feedforwardterm drive to layer II/IIIterm and V, via layer IVterm inducing the P50mterm, followed by a cortico-cortical or non-lemniscal thalamicterm feedbackterm inputterm to supragranular layers inducing the N100mterm, and a subsequent second feedforwardterm inputterm inducing the P200mterm. This finding is in line with evidence suggesting that all sensoryterm areas share the same basic structure as well as many defining features, such as the basic laminar organization and activation patterns (Douglas and Martin 2004 ). Specifically, the initial activation of cortical circuits by the thalamus, which follows a layer IVterm, layer II/IIIterm, layer Vterm information processing sequence, a simplified version of which is recreated here and generates the P50mterm using HNNterm, has been shown to be similar across sensoryterm modalities (Douglas and Martin 2004 ; Barbour and Callaway 2008 ; Atencio and Schreiner 2010 ).

It is important to note that the connectivity within the auditory cortexterm is not as well understood as in other sensoryterm regions, and that focusing solely on similarities between cortical regions is likely an oversimplified description of auditory cortical activation. There are known differences between auditory cortexterm and other sensoryterm regions, as for example, laminar differences are less prominent than in visual or somatosensory regions, and there is some evidence to suggest that the functional role of auditory layer IVterm may differ from those in other sensoryterm regions (Linden and Schreiner 2003 ; Barbour and Callaway 2008 ; Ji et al. 2016 ; Oviedo 2017 ). However, we show that a manually fitted neural modelterm·mth of a cortical columnterm with canonicalterm·dom layer specific inputterm patterns is able to account for scalp-recorded auditory responses, supporting the notion that the basic structure and activation patterns are similar across sensoryterm regions, and providing means to approach the biophysical basis of well-known characteristics and effects reported in humanterm AEFterm research.

Further, we found that simulations associated with AEFs recorded over left and right hemispheres were remarkably similar, not only in the inputterm sequence, but also in firing rates and dipoleterm waveforms (compare Fig. 4 a-c and d-f). Interestingly, the only parameterterm necessary to account for qualitative differences in the waveform shape was the strength of the distalterm inputterm into layer Vterm pyramidalterm neurons. In other words, a 100 ms distalterm feedbackterm inputterm drives layer Vterm pyramidalterm neurons more strongly in right than in left hemisphereterm AEFs (note that since parameterterm optimization was performed all inputterm parameters differed somewhat between left and right hemisphereterm models). These findings are in general agreement with research demonstrating functional asymmetries between left and right hemispheres. For example, right hemisphereterm dominanceterm has been shown especially in processing of pure tones, although the hemispheric balance seems to depend on the nature of the stimulus, as well as context (Peronnet et al. 1974 ; Wolpaw and Penry 1977 ; Schönwiesner et al. 2007 ; Howard and Poeppel 2009 ). It could be speculated that the relative specialization of the right hemisphereterm to process pure tones is associated with optimized connectivity, leading to stronger inputs. It would be interesting, in the future, to explore whether the diminished rightward asymmetry, or leftward asymmetry to speech sounds could be explained by similar underlying dynamics. However, the functional lateralizationterm·dom of auditory processing continues to be debated (e.g. Boemio et al. 2005 ), and its relation to biophysical mechanisms is unknown. Additionally, our interpretations are model-derived predictions, since we did not have clear pre-defined hypotheses regarding the mechanisms underlying the differences in left and right AEFs.

HNNterm is designed to be a hypothesisterm development and testing tool, however in its current form not all possible hypotheses can be explored. For example, macroanatomical differences not captured in HNNterm between left and right hemispheres could contribute to, if not fully account for, waveform differences observed in AEFs (Galaburda et al. 1978 ; Rademacher et al. 1993 ; Penhune et al. 1996 ; Howard and Poeppel 2009 ). In fact, Shaw et al. ( 2013 ) argued that what may appear as a functional asymmetry in auditory processing is, at least partially, caused by structuralterm asymmetries, as increased cortical foldingterm in the left auditory regions results in increased MEGterm/EEGterm signal cancelation. Here, we do not account for these anatomical differences since HNNterm has been developed to account for the microanatomical principles underlying electrophysiological recordings, and does not include cortical foldingterm in its parameters. As such, we cannot rule out the possibility that the parameterterm differences between left and right hemisphereterm AEFs we found in our modelterm·mth are simply compensating for the effect of structuralterm differences, and do not accurately reflect underlying functional mechanisms. However, we found that alternative modelterm·mth adjustments which manipulated local network features, instead of external inputterm characteristics to account for hemispheric AEFterm differences, led to a poor modelterm·mth fit, providing some support for our modelterm·mth. Ultimately, the predictions made with HNNterm can guide targeted experiments to validate or negate modelterm·mth results, and new data informs HNNterm expansion and improvement.

We also simulated the contralateralterm dominanceterm effect, i.e. the larger N100mterm amplitudeterm in the AEFterm waveform associated with contralateralterm compared to ipsilateralterm tone presentation. In the AEFterm literature, the mechanistic differences proposed to underlie the finding that increased AEFterm amplitudes are recorded in hemispheres contralateralterm to the auditory stimulus, are that auditory pathways that cross over are stronger and/or more numerous than ipsilateralterm ones (Tunturi 1946 ; Rosenzweig 1951 ; Kimura 1961 ), and that the cortical region activated by the auditory stimulus is larger in the contralateralterm hemispheres (Rosenzweig 1951 ; Gross et al. 1967 ). We tested these predictions using HNNterm. We expected the strength of a pathway to correlate primarily with the speed of the relay of information, which corresponds to the inputterm time parameters in HNNterm. The notion of differences in the size of the cortical area can be accounted for by HNNterm’s scalingterm parameterterm, which gives an estimate of the number of cells contributing to a given dipoleterm. We were able to recreate the differences between contralateralterm and ipsilateralterm AEFs, solely by changing the time and scalingterm parameters, supporting the hypotheses that these manipulations based on animalterm research can indeed account for the differences in the humanterm signal.

These simple manipulations led to greatly improved modelterm·mth fits to ipsilateralterm data, in both the left and the right hemisphereterm. The ipsilateralterm modelterm·mth fits the ipsilateralterm AEFterm less well than the contralateralterm modelterm·mth fits its corresponding data which is noticeable particularly in the P200mterm peak fit in the right hemisphereterm. However, note that the contralateralterm models were established by hand-tuning and then optimizing all 28 inputterm parameters (Table 1 ), while the ipsilateralterm modelterm·mth was generated by adjusting only four parameters, three of which by a fixed value. Importantly, the four parameters, as well as the directions in which to adjust them (i.e. increasing the time of each of three inputs by a fixed amount and decreasing the network scale), were chosen based on predictions from the literature, not on the patterns identified in our data, and no further steps were taken to fit the modelterm·mth simulations to the empiricalterm AEFs. In view of this procedure, we consider the ipsilateralterm models to reproduce the corresponding AEFs remarkably well.

We found that, compared to ipsilateralterm AEFs, contralateralterm AEFs can be simulated by increasing the scalingterm factor by 20% and decreasing the time of each inputterm by 5 ms. The difference of 20% found here is also approximately in line with predictions made by Rosenzweig ( 1951 ) who suggested that the size of ipsilateralterm presentations is around 25% smaller than contralateralterm representations. Although in our data, we were not able to demonstrate clear latencyterm differences between contralateralterm and ipsilateralterm AEFs, we found that a difference of 5 ms provided improved modelterm·mth fits. This small difference is in line with previous studies which have reported contralateralterm responses to occur between 4 and 14 ms faster than ipsilateralterm ones (Mononen and Seitz 1977 ; Mäkelä et al. 1994 ; Pantev et al. 1998 ). Since this is a fairly small difference in latencyterm, it may overlap with individually differing factors that influence AEFterm measures at these latencies, and may not be easily demonstrated. We speculate that this may explain why other studies, like our own, have not been able to confirm latencyterm differences between ipsilateralterm and contralateralterm AEFs (Yoshiura et al. 1994 ). Overall, we found that ipsilateralterm AEFs, compared to contralateralterm ones, can be simulated by delaying the activation of the cortical network and decreasing the size of the network, supporting previous hypotheses predicting that contralateralterm pathways are faster and activate larger regions of the cortex.

It is important to note that, since HNNterm is a large-scale modelterm·mth with thousands of differential equations and parameters, we cannot claim that any given waveform has a uniqueterm way in which to modelterm·mth it. With large numbers of parameters, many of which interact and trade off, HNNterm is primarily a hypothesisterm testing tool as it is not feasible to test the entire parameter spaceterm. However, a large proportion of the modelterm·mth, including cell morphologies and physiologies, and local connectivity patterns were fixed based on a large body of animalterm models, introducing biologically realistic constraints (JonespersName et al. 2007 ; Neymotin et al. 2020 ). Further, we drastically limited the number of parameters free to vary, by making the assumption that many basic features of the network are preserved across sensoryterm areas, which allowed us to fix the vast majority of parameters to values previously established for somatosensory response models (JonespersName et al. 2007 , 2009 ). Based on the same assumption, we also fixed the inputterm sequence which activates the network, but not the timing and strength of the inputs. This resulted in a modelterm·mth with 28 inputterm parameters and one scalingterm parameterterm, while all other parameters were fixed (seven additional parameters, representing calciumterm activity and inhibitoryterm connection strength, were adjusted to test alternative models, but were not used in the final models). In the case of interpreting the difference between left and right hemispheres, we attempted to narrow down the parameter spaceterm of interest by identifying which changes made the largest contribution to the difference in waveform shape. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that other parameterterm values could account for the finding in a different way. In the case of contralateralterm compared to ipsilateralterm AEFs on the other hand, we had clear hypotheses predicting not only the parameters of interest, but also the direction of their adjustment, enabling much clearer conclusions.

Additionally, it is important to note that HNNterm is designed to bridge the gap between humanterm macro-scale data and circuit-level findings from animalterm models. Many of the circuit features built into HNNterm, including those regulating inputterm drives into sensoryterm cortices, were based on animalterm studies in rodents and non-human primates and have not been directly demonstrated in humans (see Neymotin et al. 2020 ; JonespersName et al. 2007 , 2009 ). Although many basic structures and mechanisms seem to be shared across cortical regions and speciesterm (Douglas and Martin 2004 ), and HNNterm has been able to account for a variety of humanterm signals (JonespersName et al. 2009 ; Sliva et al. 2018 ; Neymotin et al. 2020 ), including those with remarkable homology between humans, rodents and non-human primates (Sherman et al. 2016 ; Shin et al. 2017 ) we cannot rule out the possibility that some cell- or circuit-level dynamics which are specific to humans are misrepresented in HNNterm.

Overall, we argue that biophysical modelling of humanterm macro-scale brain signals, using models such as HNNterm, is an important step to bridge the gap between humanterm cognitive neuroscience, and cell- and circuit-level insights provided by animalterm models. While we aimed to characterize commonly observed phenomena on a biophysical basis, future research may apply similar approaches to study similar scalp potentials, providing insights into mechanistic differences between developmental stages or clinical populations. Future research may also build on the current capabilities of HNNterm and incorporate sensitivity analyses to allow for an improved interpretation of parameterterm values.

In summary, we found that HNNterm, a modelterm·mth of a canonicalterm·dom neocortical circuit, can be used to simulate humanterm AEFs by activating the network using an inputterm sequence similar to one used to modelterm·mth other sensoryterm responses, supporting the notion that the basic structure and activation patterns are preserved across sensoryterm regions. We show that AEFterm differences between left and right, as well as contralateralterm and ipsilateralterm hemispheres, can be simulated by adjusting a small number of parameters representing network scale and inputterm characteristics. Our simulations establish a connection between scalp-recorded correlates of auditory processing and network-level insights from animalterm models, providing a first step to understand the mechanisms underlying this cognitively and clinically relevant biomarkerterm.
