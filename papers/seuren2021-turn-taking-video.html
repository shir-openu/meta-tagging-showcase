Introduction Research on video-mediated interactionterm·key has long recognized the problems that technology and a lack of shared physical space (the “fractured ecology”) pose for conversational participants ( Heath and Luff, 1993 ; Hindmarsh et al., 1998 ; Luff et al., 2003 ; Rintel, 2013 , 2015 ). In this article we are concerned with latencyterm·dom , the technology-generated transmission delay between when a participant produces an action and when the co-participant(s) perceive that action. The effects latencyterm·dom has on smooth turn-takingterm·key have featured centrally in a range of studies of social interaction ( Olbertz-Siitonen, 2015 ; Ruhleder and Jordan, 2001 ; Rusk and Pörn, 2019 ; Schoenenberg et al., 2014 ; Tang and Isaacs, 1993 ).

The problems participants experience with turn-takingterm·key in video-mediated interactionterm·key are of significant benefit to scholars of social interaction. The setting provides something of a natural breaching experiment ( Garfinkel, 1967 ): provided the latencyterm·dom is long enough to have a noticeable impact on the interaction—noticeable to the analysts, not the participants–participants find themselves in an environment where unbeknownst to them, their background assumption that turn production and turn perception occur simultaneously no longer applies ( Ruhleder and Jordan, 2001 ). As a result, they routinely have to solve basic interactional problems. Precisely because latencyterm·dom frustrates smooth turn-takingterm·key, we are provided with a treasure trove of phenomena and practices that are rare in instantaneous interaction.

In this paper, we provide a detailed analysis of how participants in video-mediated interactionterm·key manage turn-takingterm·key problems that happen as a result of latencyterm·dom. In doing so we not only document systematically how latencyterm·dom affects video-mediated interactionterm·key (i.e., its procedural consequentiality) ( Arminen et al., 2016 ; Schegloff, 1991 ), but also contribute to the study of turn-takingterm·key and the practices by which participants address problems.

Turn-takingterm·key in co-present interaction Turn-takingterm·key is a basic organizational principle of human social interaction ( Levinson, 2016 ; Sacks et al., 1974 ). While the particulars of the turn-takingterm·key system vary between linguistic cultures, the underlying rules as described by Sacks, Schegloff, and Jefferson hold large cross-cultural sway ( Dingemanse and Floyd, 2014 ; Stivers et al., 2009 ). Conversational participants organize their talk through sequences of actions in which the norm is one-speaker-at-a-time. The turn-takingterm·key system is arguably one of the few true universals of communication. Organizing talk in an orderly and systematic way is a basic condition for people to develop and maintain an intersubjectively shared understanding ( Moerman and Sacks, 1988 ; Sacks et al., 1974 ; Schegloff, 1992 ).

The turn-takingterm·key system, in combination with the principles of sequence organization ( Schegloff, 2007 ), has implications for our understanding of talk as well as our understanding of silenceterm·key at points where talk should occur (i.e., non-talkterm·dom) ( Lerner, 2019 ; Sacks et al., 1974 ). Whilst some silenceterm·key is tolerated ( Stivers et al., 2009 ), upon completion of a turn in which the speaker has selected a participant to produce a specific next action (e.g., a question making relevant an answer), silenceterm·key will often be understood as the noticeable absence of that action ( Bolden et al., 2012 ; Goodwin, 1979 ; Lerner, 2003 ; Pomerantz, 1984 ; Schegloff, 2007 ; Stivers and Rossano, 2010 ). Speakers may treat such a noticeable silenceterm·mth as, for example, a refusal by the recipient to produce a next action ( Schegloff, 2002 ) or as foreshadowing a dispreferred or non-straightforward response ( Davidson, 1984 ; Kendrick and Torreira, 2015 ; Robinson, 2020 ; Sacks, 1987 ). Even when no next-speaker has been selected, silencesterm·key between turns where talk should occur will be understood as the noticeable absence of talk: one of the participants should “self-select” to produce a turn ( Hoey, 2020 ).

The turn-takingterm·key system is also used to minimize overlapping talkterm·dom: to maintain the norm of “one-speaker-at-a-time” ( Sacks et al., 1974 ). That is not to say that overlapping talkterm·dom does not happen—it frequently does. Some forms of overlapterm·key are in fact highly affiliative, such as recognition overlapterm·key, which can be used to display strong agreement ( Vatanen, 2018 ). However, participants often treat overlapterm·key as deviant from the norm and engage in interactional work to resolve it ( Drew, 2009 ; Jefferson, 2004b ; Schegloff, 2000 , 2001 ).

Turn-takingterm·key and latencyterm·dom People acquire the turn-takingterm·key system in co-present environments, in which turns will be heard and seen at the same time they are produced ( Ruhleder and Jordan, 2001 ). However, when there is latencyterm·dom, action production and perception no longer co-occur, and this has implications for participants’ ability to manage turn-takingterm·key ( Olbertz-Siitonen, 2015 ; Rusk and Pörn, 2019 ; Schoenenberg et al., 2014 ; Tang and Isaacs, 1993 ).

Video communication technologies such as Skype inherently suffer from some latencyterm·dom. The length of this transmission delay varies: It can be anywhere in the order of tens to hundreds of milliseconds, depending on the quality of the network and each participants' local internet connection. These delays may seem short, but in social interaction where participants work with split-second timing ( Jefferson, 1973 ) they quickly present problems. Whilst recent studies have shown that participants are, at least on some occasions, aware of some of the technical problems that video-mediated interactionterm·key poses ( Rintel, 2013 , 2015 ; Rusk and Pörn, 2019 ), in our data they proceed under the same seen but unnoticed background assumptions characteristic of face-to-face interaction. There are two “non-mutual realitiesterm·dom” ( Ruhleder and Jordan, 2001 )—one for the nurse, and one for the patient—but both orient to their reality as a shared one.

Materials and methods We conducted secondary analysis of data that were collected between 2015 and 2018 in two separate studies on video-mediated consultations ShawpersName et al., 2018 . The consultations were recorded in (antenatal) diabetes, cancer, and heart failure services in the UK, and were conducted using either consumer Skype or FaceTime. Patients ranged in age between 21 and 87, with younger patients (n = 18, median = 26 years) prevalent in diabetes services and older patients (n = 19, median = 72 years) prevalent in cancer and heart failure services. Participants had a range of experience with video-mediated interactionterm·key, but due to the variation in latencyterm·dom across conversations, we could not analyze this systematically. Because we were interested in how latencyterm·dom affects the interaction, we only included consultations for which we had recordings from the patient's home and the clinic so we could compare each “reality”. Our final dataset consisted of 25 consultations, totaling 7 h and 57 min of interaction.

We transcribed the data according to conventions by Jefferson (2004a) and Mondada (2018) , and compared the two ends of each consultation to collect instances of noticeable, post-turn completion silenceterm·key and of overlapping talkterm·dom.

We examined silencesterm·key at each point where a speaker's turn came to possible completion (the transition-relevance placeterm·dom). However, silenceterm·key can occur at these points even without latencyterm·dom. We needed to isolate cases in which the speaker attempted to resolve a post-completion silenceterm·key, where that silenceterm·key was caused by latencyterm·dom. In other words, our focus was on cases where latencyterm·dom caused speakers to perceive a noticeable silenceterm·mth, when there was no such silenceterm·key for the recipient. We therefore excluded cases where the recipient did not take a turn (i.e., where there was an actual failure in turn transition). In these cases, latencyterm·dom would merely prolong the noticeable silenceterm·mth. We did not take the length of a silenceterm·key as a criterion for inclusion. A silenceterm·key becomes a noticeable silenceterm·mth when participants treat it as such, that is, when participants orient to that silenceterm·key as the noticeable absence of talk (e.g., by pursuing a response).

As with silenceterm·key, overlapping talkterm·dom can occur in any social interaction ( Jefferson, 2004b ; Schegloff, 2000 ). To make sure we only included cases where the emergence of overlapterm·key and its resolution were affected by latencyterm·dom, we excluded cases where one participant was making a competitive bid for the turn by launching into a turn when their co-participant's turn was recognizably not reaching completion ( Drew, 2009 ; Jefferson, 1984b ; Vatanen, 2018 ).

Our final collection consisted of 130 cases of noticeable silenceterm·mth and 172 cases of overlapterm·key. Since silenceterm·key resolution strategies almost invariably caused overlapping talkterm·dom, most cases of noticeable silenceterm·mth are also in the collection of overlapterm·key.

We analyzed these data using Conversation Analysisterm·key ( Ten Have, 2007 ), focusing on how latencyterm·dom affected the interaction turn by turn ( Jefferson, 2004b ; Schegloff, 2000 ). We analyzed the interactional practices by which participants: (a) treated silencesterm·key as noticeable silencesterm·mth, and (b) attempted to resolve these silencesterm·key. We subsequently examined all cases of overlapping talkterm·dom, and then investigated (a) where overlapterm·key began for each participant, (b) the strategies they used to try to resolve overlapterm·key, and (c) where and how latencyterm·dom caused problems for these strategies.

Results The turn-takingterm·key system provides for minimal silenceterm·key between turns and minimal overlapping talkterm·dom, and it has built-in repair mechanisms to address errors ( Jefferson, 2004b ; Sacks et al., 1974 ; Schegloff, 2000 ). However, the system is built for and applied in a (co-present) context in which recipients perceive turns at the same time as speakers produce them ( Ruhleder and Jordan, 2001 ). When latencyterm·dom is introduced, this causes two problems. First, at a transition-relevance placeterm·dom, even if the recipient takes a turn and produces a next action, latencyterm·dom causes this answer to be delayed and the speaker to perceive silenceterm·key. Second, when participants talk at the same time, their perception of where in their respective turns the overlapterm·key starts (i.e., the point of overlapterm·key onset) will differ.

Silenceterm·key where talk should occur In dyadic interaction, turn-transition at a possible completion point can either be normatively relevant , when the speaker has selected the recipient to produce a specific “type” of next action (e.g., by asking a question, the speaker selects the recipient to provide an answer), or it can be possible , when the speaker has completed their turn without sequentially implicating a specific type of next action ( Sacks et al., 1974 ; Schegloff, 2007 ). Participants treat silencesterm·key in these respective environments differently. When turn-transition should happen a gapterm·dom emerges (i.e., a failure by the recipient to take a turn in which to produce the projected action). When turn-transition could happen, a lapseterm·dom emerges (i.e., the recipient foregoes an opportunity to talk) ( Hoey, 2020 ; Sacks et al., 1974 ).

Silencesterm·key thus become problematic and are constituted as a specific type of silenceterm·key through the behavior of participants. In our data, both the practices used by participants to address silenceterm·key and the timing with which they implement these practices indicates they do not understand these silencesterm·key to be the result of latencyterm·dom, at least not initially. They overwhelmingly treat the silencesterm·key as failures with turn transition, 2 as silenceterm·key where either participant should or could have taken a turn. They thus do not orient to these silencesterm·key as silencesterm·key that routinely occur in video-mediated interactionterm·key as a result of latencyterm·dom.

Understanding and solving gapsterm·dom That latencyterm·dom affects turn-takingterm·key can best be seen when speakers treat a post-completion silenceterm·key as the noticeable absence of a projected next action: the recipient is not just heard to be not talking (cf. Hoey, 2020 ), but to be not responding at a point where a response was due ( Sacks et al., 1974 ; Schegloff, 2007 ). In all but three cases, we find that they use practices that provide the recipient a new opportunity to respond, that is, they reinstantiate the transition-relevance placeterm·dom. Throughout the post-completion silencesterm·key, the speaker of the last turn either maintains their gazeterm·key if they are looking at the screen, visibly awaiting a response, or they direct their gazeterm·key towards the screen after a few hundred milliseconds, before pursuing a response. By using these practices speakers orient to the silenceterm·key as the noticeable absence of a conditionally relevant response ( Schegloff, 2007 ).

There is a range of practices participants have at their disposal to address these silencesterm·key such as repeats (extract 1), response prompts (e.g., using a polar response particle such as yeah to pursue (dis)confirmation after a yes/no-type question) ( Heritage, 1984a ) (such as in extract (7) in supplementary material), or adjusting the preference organization of the question, orienting to the silenceterm·key as foreshadowing a dispreferred or non-straightforward response ( Davidson, 1984 ; Kendrick and Torreira, 2015 ; Robinson, 2020 ; Sacks, 1987 ) (e.g., by incrementally adding a tag question to a declarative question, such as in extract (8) in supplementary material).

In the overwhelming majority of our data, speakers correct or clarify (part of) their turn, that is, they perform self-initiated self-repairterm·dom ( Bolden et al., 2012 ). The speaker identifies a problem (or “trouble”) with their talk and provides the solution for that trouble ( Kitzinger, 2013 ; Schegloff et al., 1977 ). Take the following extracts from a diabetes consultation in which the doctor asks the patient to confirm that she has been prescribed NovaRapid, a quick-acting insulin that is to be taken with meals. Following syntactic completion of her turn, the doctor adds another constituent that continues that turn, in this case the noun phrase the pen type of insulin (i.e., she adds an “incrementterm·dom” to her initial question) ( Ford et al., 2002 ; Schegloff, 2016 ). With this incrementterm·dom she combines linguistic and embodied resources to depict how the NovaRapid is used. By producing the clarification as an incrementterm·dom, she re-completes her question and provides another opportunity for the patient to provide an answer, following the initial lack of a response. She thereby reveals her understanding that an answer was due, but missing. In all but three cases, speakers use practices that reinstantiate the transition relevance placeterm·dom. In these three exceptions, participants explicitly orient to the technology as the cause of the problem. However, in none of the three do they treat latencyterm·dom as the cause. Recurrently we find that participants in our data treat silenceterm·key at a point where turn transition is relevant, as a failure in the organization of turn-takingterm·key. They use practices to pursue a response, meaning they treat the response not as delayed due to latencyterm·dom, but as missing entirely. By treating these silencesterm·key as gapsterm·dom, they show that at least initially they are not aware of latencyterm·dom as the true source of the problem.

Solving lapsesterm·dom According to the rules of turn-takingterm·key, when a speaker reaches possible completion and has not selected a next speaker, turn transition is optional. Recipients will have primary rights to self-select, but they may forego this opportunity. The ensuing silenceterm·key will still be oriented to as silenceterm·key where talk should occur ( Hoey, 2020 ), but the turn-takingterm·key system provides for its resolution: if none of the recipients self-selects, then the current speaker may continue; and if the current speaker does not continue then “rounds of possible self-selection” ( Sacks et al., 1974 , p. 715) occur until the silenceterm·key is resolved (i.e., until either participant has self-selected).

We found that latencyterm·dom interfered with this system. At a point of possible completion where no next speaker has been selected, the current speaker may perceive a lapseterm·dom even though the recipient has already self-selected. The following example from a diabetes consultation illustrates how speakers perceive and deal with a lapseterm·dom where one does not occur. The patient has been talking for 1.5 min about a drug trial in which he took part. That story comes to potential completion in line 3, where he returns to describing the study and discusses the outlook ( Jefferson, 1978 ). 4 Image 5 The nurse receives the patient's story with a claim of understanding oh right ( Heritage, 1984b ) and subsequently uses okay to indicate her understanding that his story is done ( Beach, 1993 ). With this turn, she does not select the patient to produce a next turn, and so he can self-select. And indeed, the patient continues. He directs his gazeterm·key away from the screen, 5 and moves into a new TCU (4b, line 6) providing an acknowledgment token ( Jefferson, 1984a ) before continuing. However, due to latencyterm·dom the nurse perceives 0.5 s of silenceterm·key, during which she maintains her gazeterm·key towards the screen, perceiving the patient to be reorienting his gazeterm·key from left to right (i.e., to the screen and thus to her ( Luff et al., 2016 )), and then asks how the study affected the patient. She thus perceives a lapseterm·dom where one does not occur and resolves it by self-selecting.

The consequences of this perceived but not actual lapseterm·dom may seem small initially. There is some overlapping talkterm·dom before they resume one-speaker-at-a-time with the patient answering the nurse's question (4a, line 11). However, the patient does not return to the turn he had abandoned when the overlapterm·key happened. Instead, the talk moves away from the specifics of the study he was talking about. The opportunity to mention what he was going to mention does not arise afterwards and he does not create the opportunity for himself.

We identified 33 lapsesterm·dom that were caused by latencyterm·dom (extract (5) below provides a further example). Lapsesterm·dom provide a different problem for participants than gapsterm·dom. Latencyterm·dom causes speakers to perceive silenceterm·key at a point where recipients may self-select. This silenceterm·key is understood as the recipient foregoing the opportunity to take a turn, even when the recipient has in fact started talking. Because the solution provided by the turn-takingterm·key system is for the speaker to continue, the result consistently is overlapping talkterm·dom, which then has to be resolved by one of the participants dropping out. But that itself is not always straightforward. We now turn to how latencyterm·dom frustrates participants’ ability to accomplish overlapterm·key resolution.

Overlapping talkterm·dom The turn-takingterm·key system provides for minimal overlapping talkterm·dom ( Sacks et al., 1974 ). When overlapterm·key does happen, participants attempt to solve it quickly, applying an “Overlap Resolution Deviceterm·dom”: either one of the participants drops out after one or two beats (eg, syllables), or they use practices to compete for the turn (e.g., faster or louder talk) ( Jefferson, 2004b ; Schegloff, 2000 , 2001 ).

Overlapterm·key can occur at different structural positions in the construction of the turn ( Drew, 2009 ; Jefferson, 1973 , 1984b , 1986 , 2004b ; Vatanen, 2018 ): (i) when a next speaker comes in at a point where they recognize what the speaker is going to say (what is called recognitional onsetterm·dom ) 6 ; (ii) when a next speaker produces a turn at the same time as a current speaker continues ( transition space onset ); (iii) when a next speaker begins a turn just after current speaker has continued ( post-transition onset ); and (iv) when a next speaker begins a turn at a point where current speaker is recognizably not approaching completion ( interjacent onsetterm·dom ).

The implications of overlapterm·key happening at these various points can be markedly different. For example, when recognitional onsetterm·dom occurs, turn completion provides a natural point for current speaker to drop out and thereby to resolve overlapterm·key. However, when one participant launches a turn at an interjacent position, they are making an active claim to the turn when another already has the right, and this can require more competitive practices ( Schegloff, 2000 ). In video-mediated interactionterm·key, latencyterm·dom can cause participants to have different perceptions of the point of overlapterm·key onset and thereby of how to resolve it.

In the majority of our data (147 cases), overlapterm·key is resolved quickly (in the other 25 one participant concedes the turn after both compete for it). One of the participants drops out within a few beats of overlapping talkterm·dom. Extract (5) from a diabetes consultation illustrates this clearly. The nurse has just explained that while the patient had a slightly low potassium during her last blood test, there is no cause for concern. She then asks the patient for an update on her diabetes. She pursues a response by re-asking her question, using a full clause this time, treating the silenceterm·key as a gapterm·dom. The patient's response comes off in post-transition overlapterm·key, but the nurse continues undisturbed. After her response pursuit, the patient provides an answer and the sequence unfolds. The overlapping talkterm·dom here is a result of the nurse pursuing a response when, unbeknownst to her, one was already forthcoming. The trouble is quickly resolved. The patient quickly drops out, in line with what Schegloff (2000) finds for the resolution of overlapping talkterm·dom in face-to-face interaction. The majority of our cases runs off similarly: once overlapterm·key happens, one of the participants drops out within a few beats, often, as here, without either participant making a claim for the turn.

One problem that latencyterm·dom can cause for overlapterm·key resolution is that resolution practices are similarly delayed. This potentially leads to extensive sequences in which participants try to figure out who gets to talk. The nurse has now twice explicitly prompted the patient to continue his turn, and the patient has made a first attempt at doing so. Both have thus oriented to the patient as having the right to talk. The patient acknowledges the nurse's second prompt with yeah ( Jefferson, 2004b ) before restarting his turn for the third and final time. The above example is perhaps extreme: there were only 15 cases in our dataset with so many overlapsterm·key and repeated attempts to fix them. We have included it here, because it reveals in detail how latencyterm·dom causes problems for the basic mechanisms of organizing social interaction. In chronological time it does not take the participants long to fix the problem of whose turn it is. Between the first point of overlapterm·key onset and the patient resuming his turn, only 12.7 s have elapsed. However, in interactional time it takes a lot of work. There are multiple extensive silencesterm·key and each participant uses multiple practices to resolve the halt in progressivity, running into new problems each time. This reveals how fundamentally latencyterm·dom frustrates smooth turn-takingterm·key and participants’ ability to solve problems as they arise.

Discussion We demonstrated in this paper in detail how latencyterm·dom causes problems for turn-takingterm·key. Building on previous work on latencyterm·dom ( Olbertz-Siitonen, 2015 ; Ruhleder and Jordan, 2001 ; Rusk and Pörn, 2019 ; Schoenenberg et al., 2014 ; Tang and Isaacs, 1993 ), we explored how latencyterm·dom in video-mediated interactionterm·key matters for the talk by focusing on how participants manage silencesterm·key in transition relevance places and overlapping talkterm·dom. First, by causing a delay between turn-production and turn-perception, latencyterm·dom causes speakers to perceive silenceterm·key where talk should, and in fact does happen. This problem can be exacerbated by re-orientation of recipient gazeterm·key, which is also delayed and therefore can be understood differently (e.g., gazeterm·key can signal incipient speakership, whereas it was done to signal attention to a new action). Second, when participants talk in overlapterm·key, latencyterm·dom causes them to have different perceptions of where overlapterm·key starts and it delays the overlapterm·key resolution strategies they implement. Often these problems are quickly resolved, but sometimes they lead to extended sequences before participants manage to resume one-speaker-at-a-time.

These findings show that the participants in our data were generally not aware of latencyterm·dom: they used overlapterm·key and silenceterm·key resolution practices that treated these problems as turn-takingterm·key problems, not as latencyterm·dom problems. Latencyterm·dom is thus well and truly part of the context of video-mediated interactionterm·key ( Arminen et al., 2016 ): it has procedural consequences ( Schegloff, 1991 ). An upshot is that video-mediated interactionterm·key can provide a natural breaching experiment ( Garfinkel, 1967 ) for turn-takingterm·key. Participants are recurrently faced with a basic structural-organizational problem, providing us as analysts with an invaluable resource to understand how participants address these problems.

Note, however, that while latencyterm·dom is a clear problem in our data, we conducted secondary analysis on a small data set, meaning that the data were collected for a different purpose, and that they involved many older people who had limited experience with video-mediated interactionterm·key. A study designed to investigate latencyterm·dom specifically could provide a more detailed understanding of (a) how long a delay needs to be before it starts to disrupt turn-takingterm·key, and (b) to what extent the length of the delay matters for different types of actions and turn-transitions. Furthermore, the COVID-19 pandemic has led to an enormous increase in the use of video-mediated communication. Early indications are that people are now far more aware of the problems that latencyterm·dom can cause. Indeed, it seems to be one of the main frustrations. Whether and if so how this emerging experience re-shapes the turn-takingterm·key rules for video-mediated interactionterm·key is unclear. Participants may become better at addressing the problems, and develop new turn-takingterm·key conventions that are optimized for the medium.

Upshot for study of video-mediated interactionterm·key Video-mediated interactionterm·key has been a subject of research for decades, and much of our social interaction has moved online as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic and social distancing protocols. Nonetheless, the literature around turn-takingterm·key in video is scarce and primarily about institutional talk. Since latencyterm·dom is omni-present, inter-turn silencesterm·key will always be longer in video-mediated interactionterm·key than they are for in-person or telephone interaction. However, what kind of silencesterm·key are tolerable and when video-mediated interactionterm·key can be understood as “smooth” has so far not been investigated. Our data suggests that with latencyterm·dom around 100 ms, turn-takingterm·key is barely affected, whereas with 700 ms, participants struggle routinely. Studies need to be set up to systematically address the nature of video-mediated turn-takingterm·key, focusing on its vocal, embodied and technological components in both dyadic and multi-party interaction. Given the challenge posed by social distancing protocols for data collection, such studies may prove difficult during the pandemic, but with screen capture software it should be possible to start exploring these questions in a more systematic way.

Our analysis indicates that in order to get an adequate understanding of the sequential organization of video-mediated interactionterm·key, it is often necessary to have recordings of both ends of the conversation. However, this is not always practically feasible. Participants may be far apart geographically (e.g., Ekberg et al., 2019 ; Licoppe, 2017 ; Licoppe and Morel, 2012 ; Rintel, 2013 , 2015 ), and it places a significant demand on the research team to travel to multiple locations to make recordings. In addition there are methodological considerations: participants do not have access to each other's reality ( Luff et al., 2003 ) and in order to get am emic perspective, we should focus on the individual's life world ( Olbertz-Siitonen, 2015 ).

For many questions, however, it is crucial that researchers have access to each side of the conversation, both what each participant sees, hears and does. 9 Participants are continuously asking the question “why that now” ( Schegloff and Sacks, 1973 ), and while there is one answer for each participants, for us as analysts there are as many answers as there are participants. An analysis of how a sequence unfolds and how structure emerges cannot be conducted when we rely on one side of the interaction.

Conclusion Immediacy is a taken for granted background assumption of communication. We have demonstrated in this paper that in video-mediated interactionterm·key, time and again latencyterm·dom disrupts the structural organization of the interaction. These problems also happen in face-to-face and other forms of synchronous, instantaneous interaction. Indeed, it is precisely because these problems are familiar, that they are not easily recognized as technological problems: there is no way for participants to distinguish between silenceterm·key that indicates a withheld response, and silenceterm·key that indicates a delayed response. All silencesterm·key sound the same. That participants manage to overcome these problems at all is then a testament to the robustness of the turn-takingterm·key system. No matter how significant the impact of latencyterm·dom is on the interaction, participants manage to resume normal turn-takingterm·key at some point, even if it takes some effort and frustration.
