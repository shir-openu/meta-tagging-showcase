phenomenonspatial ability as a predictor of STEM achievement— “We were concerned with the interplay between cognitive abilities and success in specific topics during higher STEM education, and particularly with the role of SA.”
claim-typeprediction— “Math and physics achievements were best predicted by numerical, verbal and general reasoning abilities.”
method — evidence— “In a prospective cohort design, we assessed students from two STEM programs on multiple measures of SV, numerical and verbal reasoning abilities.”
object of study — evidence— “We investigated whether ability differences among students who entered higher STEM education were predictive of achievements during the first undergraduate year.”
mechanismAdvanced mathematics is carried out by translating spatial structures into abstract symbolic formalism rather than by visualizing them, so exceptional visualization ability adds nothing to math achievement— “Math courses may focus on translating complex spatial structures into abstract mathematical formalism, more than they require superb visualization of such structures.”
mechanismWhere spatial tests do correlate with math grades, the link runs through domain-general reasoning components embedded in those tests rather than through visualization itself— “Additionally, our finding that positive correlations between one type of SV tests (visualization of cross-sections) and some math achievements were mediated by factors common to other domains of reasoning suggests that factors other than visualization—which may be strongly present in some spatial tests—play a role when it comes to…
mechanismHigher prior domain knowledge in self-selected STEM entrants removes the need to recruit spatial ability, weakening the SV-physics link— “One possible explanation for these differences is that students in our sample may have had higher levels of prior-knowledge in science: first at entry level due to self-selection, and at the time of achievements assessment (i.e., course exams), as it reflected learning during an entire academic year.”
mechanismApplication-oriented versus proof-oriented teaching of the same mathematical content recruits different abilities (calculation efficiency vs. broader reasoning)— “For example, application-oriented mathematics may require high efficiency in utilizing domain-specific skills and knowledge, which may explain the almost exclusive relation between numerical reasoning ability and math in the engineering group.”
mechanismPrediction is domain specific on both sides: the ability that matters depends on what the course actually demands— “Thus, the predictions seem highly domain specific: differences in SV, even among the spatially talented, mattered when complex spatial tasks were in focus (technical drawing), whereas differences in numerical or verbal reasoning abilities among the mathematically talented mattered when math was in focus.”
mechanismThe proposed reading of the null: spatial ability has a threshold for mathematics that these students have already passed, so further differences buy nothing— “Apparently, when it comes to predicting mathematics achievements, SV seems to have a lower threshold than numerical and verbal abilities (i.e., a lower level is sufficient for success).”
mechanismSpatial tests are not purely spatial - performance on them depends on executive function and on analytic strategies that do as well as visualising— “For example, Miyake et al. [ 31 ] showed that performance on SA tests, and particularly on SV tests, highly depended on executive functions, whereas others found that analytical- and heuristic-based strategies are as common and at least as efficient as visualization per se, sometimes even preferable [ 9 , 30 , 32 , 33 ].”
mechanismSeparating domain-specific from domain-general variance weakens some effects and strengthens the general factor, so how the model is specified changes which story it tells— “However, when specific effects were estimated from the residual variances of the specific abilities (as in model B), some of these effects became weaker, and the direct effects of the general factor became stronger.”
mechanismIn a high-ability sample the abilities overlap less than usual, which limits how much prediction is available to find— “It should be noted that the overlap between specific abilities in our sample was likely lower than in more heterogeneous samples (this is often found in high-ability groups [ 67 ]), possibly limiting its predictive power.”
contradicts Lubinski and Benbow (2006)— “Our results seem inconsistent with findings on the unique predictive validity of SA among students of extremely high ability (e.g., the top 0.01% in Lubinski and Benbow, 2006).”
builds-on Uttal and Cohen [16]— “As suggested by Uttal and Cohen [ 16 ], SA may be particularly important when domain-knowledge is low.”
replicates Miller and Halpern [44]— “Miller and Halpern [ 44 ] found positive effects of SA training on physics but not math grades of high-ability STEM students, and concluded that SA training was not relevant to the content of math courses, which is consistent with our own conclusion.”
critiques the threshold view that cognitive abilities stop predicting within highly selected samples— “Overall, our data clearly showed that ability differences in this high-ability population are relevant to achievement prediction, corroborating previous findings against a ‘threshold’ view of cognitive abilities [ 34 , 44 , 48 , 49 ].”
critiques the assumption that spatial ability is a broad predictor of STEM success during the novice phase— “It therefore appears that rather than broadly determining STEM success during the novice phase, as would be expected based on prior research assumptions, SV emerged as a narrower STEM predictor relative to other abilities, which in turn predicted success on a broader range of core STEM subjects.”
extends Carroll's broad definition of spatial visualisation, adopted here as the working sense— “We use the term SV in the broader sense suggested by Carroll [ 24 ], namely referring to complex mental manipulations of objects, mostly in three dimensions, involving either rotation or other forms of transformation.”
critiques the argument linking women's underrepresentation in STEM to their poorer spatial test performance— “Although it is beyond the scope of this study to directly address this issue, the possibility that high spatial skills are not crucial for higher mathematics puts in a different light the often-made arguments linking women’s poorer performance in SA tests to this gender gap.”
sensespatial ability (SA) - the broad literature term, used loosely and often for measures that are actually narrower— “Very broadly, spatial ability (SA) refers to “the ability to generate, retain, and manipulate abstract visual images” [ 19 ].”
sensespatial visualization (SV) - the specific factor measured here, and the authors' stated convention is to say SV for their own work and SA when reporting prior research— “Since previous studies have often used the broad term SA even when measures were of SV, we use the term SA when referring to previous research in this field.”
sensethreshold - the general claim: above some ability level, further differences stop predicting anything— “Overall, our data clearly showed that ability differences in this high-ability population are relevant to achievement prediction, corroborating previous findings against a ‘threshold’ view of cognitive abilities [ 34 , 44 , 48 , 49 ].”
sensethreshold - per ability and per domain: each ability has its own level that suffices for a given subject, and spatial ability's is low for mathematics— “Apparently, when it comes to predicting mathematics achievements, SV seems to have a lower threshold than numerical and verbal abilities (i.e., a lower level is sufficient for success).”
sensewhat a spatial test measures - as named: spatial-visual processing— “The most studied type of SA in the context of STEM is spatial visualization (SV), which is the ability to “…apprehend a spatial form, shape or scene in order to match it with another spatial form, shape or scene, often with the necessity of rotating it in two or three dimensions one or more times” [ 24 ].”
sensewhat a spatial test measures - in practice: also executive function, and analytic strategies that substitute for visualising altogether— “Research showed that solving SA tests involves not only strictly spatial-visual processes, but also a variety of non-spatial components.”
