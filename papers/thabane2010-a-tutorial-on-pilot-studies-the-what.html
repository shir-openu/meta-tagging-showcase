Phase II trials provide preliminary evidence on the clinical efficacy of a drug or intervention. They may or may not be randomized. Phase III trials are randomized studies comparing two or more drugs or intervention strategies to assess efficacy and safety. Phase IV trials, usually done after registration or marketing of a drug, are non-randomized surveillance studies to document experiences (e.g. side-effects, interactions with other drugs, etc) with using the drug in practice. Pilot studies of phase III trials (dealing with common diseases) are not addressed in their proposal. It is therefore prudent to ask: Is it ethical to conduct a study whose feasibilityterm can not be guaranteed (i.e. with a high probability of success)? It seems unethical to consider running a phase III study without having sufficient data or information about the feasibilityterm. In fact, most granting agencies often require data on feasibilityterm as part of their assessment of the scientificterm validity for funding decisions. There is however one important ethical aspect about pilot studies that has received little or no attention from researchers, research ethics boards and ethicists alike. This pertains to the issue of the obligation that researchers have to patients or participants in a trial to disclose the feasibilityterm nature of pilot studies. This is essential given that some pilot studies may not lead to further studies. A review of the commonly cited research ethics guidelines - the Nuremburg Code [ 29 ], Helsinki Declaration [ 30 ], the Belmont Report [ 31 ], ICH Good Clinical Practice [ 32 ], and the International Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research Involving Human Subjects [ 33 ] - shows that pilot studies are not addressed in any of these guidelines. Canadian researchers are also encouraged to follow the Tri-Council Policy Statement (TCPS) [ 34 ] - it too does not address how pilot studies need to be approached. Conclusions Pilot or vanguard studies provide a good opportunity to assess feasibilityterm of large full-scale studies. Pilot studies are the best way to assess feasibilityterm of a large expensive full-scale study, and in fact are an almost essential pre-requisite. Conducting a pilot prior to the main study can enhance the likelihood of success of the main studyterm and potentially help to avoid doomed main studies. Pilot studies should be well designed with clear feasibilityterm objectives, clear analytic plans, and explicit criteria for determining success of feasibilityterm. They should be used cautiously for determining treatment effectsterm and variance estimates for power or sample size calculations. Finally, they should be scrutinized the same way as full scale studies, and every attempt should be taken to publish the results in peer-reviewed journals.
