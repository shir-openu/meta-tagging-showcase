findingHuber and Lapinski's 2006 study is the odd one out: it found no racial priming while a body of recent studies does find it. — “In a critique of Huber and Lapinski (in this volume) I argued that their 2006 study failed to find evidence of racial priming and that this failure stands out in the recent accumulation of studies that do find racial priming.”
findingThe author attributes that non-replication to defects in how Huber and Lapinski's research was conducted rather than to the absence of a real racial priming effect. — “I argued further that this failure to replicate is the result of deficiencies in Huber and Lapinski's research.”
findingAgainst their reply, the author argues that their own results show the study produced null findings, that flaws in the study caused those nulls, that her own research survives their criticism, and that the surrounding literature is relevant and shows how anomalous their nulls are. — “I show that 1) Huber and Lapinski's results demonstrate that their study produced null findings, 2) these null findings are caused by flaws in their study, 3) my research withstands their criticism, and 4) the relevant literature is in fact relevant and highlights the extent to which their null results are anomalous.”
findingBoth sides agree that racial predispositions shape policy views, that those predispositions can be primed by cues and messages, and that implicit racial messages prime them. — “There are, however, several points of agreement: 1) racial predispositions shape policy views, 2) these predispositions can be primed by cues and messages, and 3) these predispositions are primed by implicit racial messages.”
findingThe surviving disagreement is narrow: whether explicit racial messages have an effect. — “What remains at issue is the impact of explicit racial messages.”
