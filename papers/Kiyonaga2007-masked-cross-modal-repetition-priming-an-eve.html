phenomenonmasked cross-modal repetition priming in word recognition— “In the current study we examined ERP masked repetition priming both within and between modalities.”
claim-typemechanism— “These results therefore confirm the first prediction derived from the bi-modal interactive-activation model, namely that lexical-level pathways dominate cross-modal transfer.”
method — evidence— “We report three experiments that combine the masked priming paradigm with the recording of event-related potentials in order to examine the time-course of cross-modal interactions during word recognition.”
mechanismA visual prime activates whole-word orthographic representations, which pass activation on to the corresponding whole-word phonological representations, so cross-modal transfer travels the slower lexical route and shows up only in the N400.— “Activation engendered by a visual prime word reaches whole-word orthographic representations (giving rise to within-modality priming) and is sent on to corresponding whole-word phonological representations.”
mechanismLengthening prime exposure lets activation reach whole-word phonological units sooner and accumulate to a higher level, which shifts the cross-modal N400 effect earlier and makes it bigger.— “suggests that the longer prime duration allowed activation to reach whole-word phonological representations earlier and therefore to build up to higher levels than at the shorter prime duration”
mechanismAn inhibitory reset of modality-specific representations, triggered when prime and target are perceived as separate events, reduces within-modality priming at longer prime durations.— “One plausible mechanism for such inhibitory effects is that the word recognition system needs to be reset in order to correctly process new upcoming words.”
replicates Grainger et al. (2003) behavioural cross-modal masked priming result— “Experiment 1 successfully replicated the behavioral pattern of within and cross-modality masked repetition priming obtained in prior studies using the lexical decision task and similar stimulus presentation conditions (i.e., 50 ms visual primes, Grainger et al., 2003 ).”
builds-on Holcomb and Grainger (in press), the N250 component— “In line with the results of Holcomb and Grainger (in press) , in Experiment 1 we also found evidence for visual-visual repetition effects starting as early as 200–300 ms post-target onset that resulted in a clear negative-going peak at 250 ms (see Figure 4 ).”
contradicts the second prediction of the bimodal interactive-activation model (early, pre-N400 cross-modal effects at 67 ms primes)— “However, contrary to this prediction we failed to find any evidence for standard priming effects (i.e., reduced negativity in the related condition) in early ERP components (i.e., the N250) with auditory targets at the longer prime duration of Experiment 3.”
senseTHEY CONSIDER Holcomb2005'S DISTINCTION FOR THIS CASE AND DECLINE IT - AND Holcomb AND Grainger ARE AUTHORS ON BOTH PAPERS. The obvious extension of `pre-activation versus establishing connections` is that cross-modal priming, being slower and later, is the connection-building kind. `HOWEVER, WE WOULD ARGUE that while semantic priming may indeed require the conscious linking of related representations, SUCH A MECHANISM WOULD BE SUPERFLUOUS FOR CROSS-MODAL REPETITION PRIMING since the visual prime and auditory target presumably have THE SAME SEMANTIC REPRESENTATION.` The argument is that the distinction does not APPLY here, not that it is wrong - a author group declining to extend its own account into the case it most obviously fits— “However, we would argue that while semantic priming may indeed require the conscious linking of related representations, such a mechanism would be superfluous for cross-modal repetition priming since the visual prime and auditory target presumably have the same semantic representation”
senseAND THEY BACK THE REFUSAL WITH AN EMPIRICAL DISCRIMINATOR RATHER THAN LEAVING IT AS REASONING: the Holcomb et al. (2005) semantic effect `was mainly evident on the MIDDLE PORTION AND TRAILING EDGE of the N400, while cross-modal repetition priming was evident on the RISING EDGE of the N400 at the longest prime duration of the present study.` Different position within the same component, so the two effects are not one phenomenon wearing two names. That is a much harder claim to make than a verbal distinction, and it is the kind of evidence a corpus of definitions should be able to retrieve: two papers, one lab, one component, and a measurable difference in WHERE on the waveform each effect sits— “Furthermore, the semantic priming effect that emerged with longer prime durations in the Holcomb et al. (2005) study was mainly evident on the middle portion and trailing edge of the N400, while cross-modal repetition priming was evident on the rising edge of the N400 at the longest prime duration of the present study”
senseA METHODOLOGICAL CRITERION FOR WHETHER AN ERP COMPONENT EXISTS AT ALL, cited to a standard and RANKED BY STRENGTH: `THIRD, THE MOST COMPELLING EVIDENCE for component independence is when components show DIFFERENTIAL SENSITIVITY TO EXPERIMENTAL VARIABLES (Rugg & Coles, 1995).` The first two pieces of evidence are morphological - two peaks with a positivity between them, different scalp distributions - and the authors say plainly that the functional one beats them. **A component is real when something moves it that does not move its neighbour**, and that is a rule about how to individuate a measure, not a finding about words— “Third, the most compelling evidence for component independence is when components show differential sensitivity to experimental variables”
senseTHE PAPER'S OWN DISSOCIATION SUPPLIES THAT CRITERION, AND IT IS A DOUBLE ONE: `within-modality priming revealed an early N250 effect THAT WAS ABSENT in the cross-modality condition. On the other hand, priming effects in the region of the N400 appeared to be JUST AS STRONG cross-modalities as within-modalities in this experiment.` One variable moves the early component and not the late one; the other moves both. So the N250's null in the cross-modal condition is not a weak result - it is the evidence that the N250 is a component rather than a shoulder of the N400— “In the current series of studies such a dissociation occurred in the comparisons of within and across modality priming in Experiment 3 where within-modality priming revealed an early N250 effect that was absent in the cross-modality condition. On the other hand, priming effects in the region of the N400 appeared to be just as strong…
senseTHE MODEL IS TESTED ON TWO PREDICTIONS AND THE PAPER REPORTS BOTH OUTCOMES, INCLUDING THE FAILURE. The first holds - `The present results are compatible with the FIRST prediction of the bimodal interactive-activation model, that lexical-level pathways dominate cross-modal transfer.` The second does not: `HOWEVER, CONTRARY TO THIS PREDICTION WE FAILED TO FIND ANY EVIDENCE for standard priming effects ... in early ERP components (i.e., the N250) with auditory targets at the longer prime duration.` They then explain the failure within the model - the sublexical units get only one source of activation where whole-word units get two - and mark that explanation as owing a test: whether another small increase in prime duration would produce the effect `remains to be seen in further research`— “However, contrary to this prediction we failed to find any evidence for standard priming effects (i.e., reduced negativity in the related condition) in early ERP components (i.e., the N250) with auditory targets at the longer prime duration of Experiment 3”

limitationThe post-experiment mask-effectiveness test probably overstates how visible the primes were during the ERP experiment itself.— “Note, however, that as in Experiment 1, the mask-effectiveness test is likely to have over estimated the visibility of primes during the ERP experiment as it focused attention on detecting the masked items themselves ( Kahan, 2000 ).”

findingBehaviourally, masked visual primes speeded lexical decisions to both visual and auditory targets by roughly equal amounts, with no modality-by-repetition interaction.— “Simple effects ANOVAs revealed that the repetition effect was reliable for visual words (39ms effect, F(1,23) = 20.176, p < 0.001), and auditory words (40ms effect, F(1,23) = 14.957, p < 0.001), but no interaction between target modality and repetition (F(1,23) = 0.004, p < 0.947).”
findingIn the ERPs, within-modality repetition effects appeared early and spanned the N250 and N400, while cross-modal effects only emerged on the trailing edge of the N400 after the visual effect had ended.— “While the visual ERP repetition effects started early (200–300 ms) and continued through the traditional N400 epoch (300–500 ms), a comparable effect in the auditory modality was not significant until the offset phase of the typical N400 (550–750 ms), a time period by which the visual effect had completely dissipated.”
findingRaising the prime duration from 50 ms to 67 ms made the cross-modal N400 priming effect both earlier and substantially larger.— “Our between-experiment analyses showed an earlier and much larger modulation of N400 amplitude in cross-modal repetition priming in Experiment 3 compared to Experiment 2.”
findingThe model's prediction that a longer prime would produce cross-modal effects in pre-N400 components was not supported: no N250 priming appeared for auditory targets even at 67 ms.— “However, contrary to this prediction we failed to find any evidence for standard priming effects (i.e., reduced negativity in the related condition) in early ERP components (i.e., the N250) with auditory targets at the longer prime duration of Experiment 3.”
findingWithin-modality (visual-visual) priming became smaller, not larger, as prime exposure increased from 50 to 67 ms.— “Within-modality repetition priming actually diminished with increasing prime exposure across Experiments 2 and 3.”
