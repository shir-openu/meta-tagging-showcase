phenomenonsubliminal affective priming from facial expressions
claim-typeexistence
mechanismUnconsciously processed happy primes are proposed to shift attention toward the global configuration of the following face, which improves encoding and hence next-day recognition. — “In line with this reasoning, we speculate that happy primes may have led to better encoding and subsequent memory by preferentially engaging an attention focus on the global configuration of facial features, consistent with findings that happy mood can increase the use of global features to classify visual figures ( Bridge et al., 2009 ; Frederickson, 2004 ; Gasper and Clore, 2002 ).”
mechanismThe prime's expression is proposed to alter encoding and/or later rehearsal of the target face, rather than the memory difference arising from the conscious affective judgment the participant made about that face. — “Therefore, we suggest that the observed differences in memory were due to subliminal priming that led to differential processing at encoding and/or subsequent rehearsal as a function of facial expression.”
mechanismAffect elicited by a masked stimulus is assumed to be diffuse and therefore to spill over onto whatever stimulus follows it in time, producing assimilation of prime valence into the target judgment. — “Presumably, the affect from a subliminal stimulus is diffuse in the sense that it can spill over onto a temporally adjacent stimulus.”
relation — “In particular, our findings replicate and extend the findings of Li and colleagues (2008) , though several design features differed between these two studies.”

relation — “Our results differ from previous findings of better recognition memory among young adults for images of scenes depicting negative compared to positive affect ( Charles, Mather, & Carstensen, 2003 ).”
differs inlong-lasting → transient — HOW LONG THE EFFECT IS ENTITLED TO OUTLIVE ITS PRIME - the transient reading limits it to fleeting perceptual changes; this paper shows a bias surviving 24 hours with the prime absent at test. The whole dispute is a duration — “a transient phenomenon manifested only in fleeting perceptual changes”

replicationuntested
limitationPrime awareness may have gone undetected, so the strictly-subliminal grouping is not certain. — “Of course, it remains possible that prime awareness was missed by the subjective reports (e.g., because of forgetting), or that prime awareness occurred despite chance-level discrimination abilities”
limitationThe objective awareness check may have had too few trials per participant to estimate discrimination ability well. — “Of course, for any of the 43 participants the number of trials may have been suboptimal for assessing discrimination abilities.”
limitationThe 24-hour gap before the subjective awareness questions may have affected the answers given. — “The 1-day delay may have influenced the nature of the answers provided, but these questions nonetheless provided indications that some participants were aware of primes on Day 1.”
limitationA single study cannot cover the range of effects that different facial expressions have on behaviour and cognition. — “A single investigation cannot address the great variety of influences that facial expressions of various emotions have on behavior and cognition.”

content-observations: finding · quantity · variable · definition · key-term — grounded in the full text
findingSurprise faces preceded by masked happy primes were rated more positively than those preceded by fearful primes, but only among participants classified as strictly subliminal. — “Happy face primes led to more positive ratings than fearful face primes in the SS group [ t (14) = 2.58, p < .05], but not in the NSS group [ t (27) = 0.65, n.s. ].”
findingA day later, recognition of the same surprise faces was better for those that had been happy-primed than for fearful- or neutral-primed faces, with no fearful-versus-neutral difference. — “Mean hit rate was significantly higher for happy-primed surprise faces than for fearful-primed surprise faces [ t (14) = 2.95, p < .05], and for neutral-primed surprise faces [ t (14) = 2.28, p < .05], but did not differ between fearful-primed and neutral-primed surprise faces [ t (14) = 1.28, n.s. ].”
findingIndependently of priming, faces a participant had rated more negatively on Day 1 were better recognised on Day 2. — “Surprise faces rated more negatively were remembered better than surprise faces rated more positively.”
findingThe awareness grouping moderated the memory effect: prime expression changed recognition in the strictly subliminal group but not in the not-strictly-subliminal group. — “Furthermore, the finding that memory results varied with prime status (fearful versus happy) in the SS group but not in the NSS group was confirmed by a significant interaction between prime status and group [ F (1, 41) = 4.52, p < 0.05].”
findingAffective priming was reliable only in the first half of the priming phase, weakening across blocks. — “The magnitude of affective priming, defined as the mean rating difference between surprise faces primed by happy faces and those primed by fearful faces, was high in the first three blocks [ M = 0.24, SD = 0.31, t (14) = 2.99, p < .01], whereas affective priming in the second three blocks was non significant [ M = 0.11, SD = 0.32, t (14) = 1.31, n.s. ].”
findingHow strongly a participant was primed was unrelated to how well that participant could objectively discriminate the prime expressions. — “Magnitude of affective priming was not associated with the degree of objective discrimination ability for participants within either the SS group [ r = -0.40, p = .13] or the NSS group [ r = -0.11, n.s. ].”
findingThe authors conclude that subliminal affective priming is not a transient perceptual effect but persists for at least a day. — “We conclude that behavioral biases induced by masked emotional expressions are not ephemeral, but rather can last at least 24 hours.”
quantityForty-three — number of participants tested — “Forty-three undergraduate students at Northwestern University gave informed consent to participate for course credit.”
quantity15 of the 43 — size of the strictly subliminal subgroup, the only group showing both key effects — “The SS group was composed of 15 of the 43 participants tested, and it was only the SS group that demonstrated both key effects.”
quantity30 ms — duration of the affective prime face before backward masking by the surprise face — “Each trial began with the central presentation of a fixation cross for 500 ms followed by an affective prime for 30 ms, immediately backward-masked by a surprise face for 300 ms ( Figure 1 ).”
quantity21-27 hrs (mean = 23.9 hrs) — delay between the priming session and the surprise memory test — “The second session occurred 21-27 hrs after Day 1 (mean = 23.9 hrs).”
quantity2.64, 2.87, and 2.70 — mean confidence-weighted memory scores in the strictly subliminal group for fearful-, happy- and neutral-primed faces — “Mean memory scores in the SS group were 2.64, 2.87, and 2.70 for fearful, happy, and neutral conditions, respectively.”
quantityM = 0.24, SD = 0.31, t (14) = 2.99, p < .01 — size of the happy-minus-fearful rating difference over the first three blocks — “The magnitude of affective priming, defined as the mean rating difference between surprise faces primed by happy faces and those primed by fearful faces, was high in the first three blocks [ M = 0.24, SD = 0.31, t (14) = 2.99, p < .01], whereas affective priming in the second three blocks was non significant [ M = 0.11, SD = 0.32, t (14) = 1.31, n.s. ].”
quantityM = -0.22, SD = 0.30, t (42) = 4.88, p < .01 — mean per-participant regression slope relating affective rating to memory score, negative meaning better memory for more negatively rated faces — “Collapsing across data from all three priming conditions and both groups, the mean slope was negative and significantly different from zero [ M = -0.22, SD = 0.30, t (42) = 4.88, p < .01], demonstrating better memory for more negatively rated surprise faces.”
quantity23 participants — number showing above-chance objective discrimination of the prime expressions — “Above-chance levels of expression discrimination were indicated by significant χ 2 ( p < .05) for 23 participants.”
