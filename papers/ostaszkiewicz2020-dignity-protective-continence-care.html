Method Concept analysis is a well-established method that has been used to analyse many concepts in health and social care (for example - human dignityterm·key [ 43 ], critical health literacy [ 53 ], cultural competence [ 54 ] and poor care [ 44 ]. Concept analyses are integral to examining concepts that are often simply seen as a task or a series of tasks [ 55 , 56 ]. By using a structured and objective process, researchers can identify critical elements of a given concept, including antecedentsterm, attributes and consequencesterm [ 55 , 57 ]. Defining the key conceptual elements assists in the development of theory that may be useful in practice, education or further research [ 58 ].

The benefit of conducting a concept analysis is that it offers a method to develop a set of defining characteristics that articulates “what counts” as the concept [ 57 ]. Specifically, it allows the researcher to (a) formulate a clear, precise theoretical and/or operational definition to be used in the study; (b) choose measurement instruments that accurately reflect the defining characteristics of the concept to be measured; (c) determine if a new instrument is needed (if no extant measure adequately reflects the defining characteristics); and (d) accurately identify the concept when it arises in clinical practice or in qualitative research data [ 59 ]. Following the clarification of key concepts, operational definitions can be developed to guide education and clinical practice and to measure the core attributes; in this case, the attributes of dignity-protective continence careterm·dom.

The goal of this concept analysis was to define ‘dignity in continence careterm·dom’ based on attributes or “essences” that capture the meaning of the concept under study [ 56 ]. There are a number of different concept analysis methods available [see for example, [ 55 , 56 , 60 – 62 ]. Rodgers [ 56 ] highlights that it is the attributes that serve as the true definition of the concept under study and that the definition of the key attributes or the concept is the “primary accomplishment of a concept analysis” [ 57 ], p. 91]. In order to define the key concepts, the first four steps of Rodgers [ 57 ] evolutionary method of concept analysis were used.

Identification and naming of the concept of interest and its surrogate terms, Identification and selection of an appropriate sample for the data collection, Collection of data relevant to identifying attributes and contextual bases of the concept, Analysis of the data to identify key characteristics of the concept.

An inductive processes of analysis which included the definition of the concept of dignityterm·key in relation to continence careterm·dom as well as identifying its alternative statements and terms was used. This approach allowed for the consideration of concepts as dynamic [ 57 ] which provided a comprehensive understanding of the concept.

We also drew on findings from a Grounded theory study that involved 88 h of field observations in two RACH in Australia, in-depth interviews with 18 nurses and care assistants, and an analysis of 87 accreditation reports about the quality of continence careterm·dom [ 32 , 72 , 73 ]. Other evidence derived from a mixed methods study to explore dementia care practitioners’ and service managers’ opinions about the challenges they countered in upholding the dignityterm·key of older people with dementia in hospitals, care homes and community settings in the UK. The researchers conducted a two-hour workshop to prompt discussion on vignettes about ethically challenging care decisions, including decisions about continence careterm·dom [ 71 ]. Information about dignity-protective continence careterm·dom was also located in a systematic review of systematic reviews for the management of UIterm·dom and the promotion of continence with conservative behavioural approaches in older people in care homes [ 76 ].

Evidence for the concept analysis was further informed by two theoretical papers that represented a synthesis of findings from prior qualitative research with contemporary biomedical understandings about incontinenceterm·key with theoretical concepts from the disciplines of nursing, psychology, and sociology [ 30 , 33 ]. One such paper describes a conceptual model of possible associations between incontinenceterm·key, care dependence and elder abuse [ 33 ]. The other describes a theoretical framework termed ‘The Dignity in Continence Careterm·dom Framework designed to improve choice, autonomyterm·key, and dignityterm·key for people who require assistance to maintain continence or manage incontinenceterm·key [ 30 ]. Using a biopsychosocial approach, the framework promotes the dignityterm·key of the person as the overarching goal of care; empathic continence careterm·dom; acknowledgement of personhood; therapeutic communicationterm·key; authentic partnership in continence careterm·dom; and acknowledgement of the effects of stigma. Consequencesterm of undignified continence careterm·dom Discussion The purpose of this concept analysis was to identify the essential attributes, antecedentsterm’ and consequencesterm of dignity-protective continence careterm·dom for older people who are care dependent who require long-term formal care and support for day-to-day living. Our analysis demonstrates that dignified continence careterm·dom happens when key attributes of care including privacyterm·key, respect, autonomyterm·key, empathy and trust, communication are the focus of the care encounter. Whilst the concepts identified in the current study are similar to previous concept analyses-including: privacyterm·key, autonomyterm·key, respect [ 41 – 46 ], stigmna [ 45 ] and the impact of the cultural/environmental context on the provision of care [ 47 ], prior examinations of dignityterm·key as a central concept in the provision of continence careterm·dom in long-term residential or inpatient formal care settings are lacking.

A key antecedentterm for dignified care was the development of a therapeutic relationship between the care provider and the care recipient. Sometimes referred to as “person-centred careterm·dom” this approach is based on a philosophy of care that promotes personal choice and autonomyterm·key for people in receipt of care [ 81 – 85 ]. The ability to deliver person-centred careterm·dom depends in turn on knowing the person [ 81 , 82 ]. Our analysis of dignityterm·key identified six key domains for the provision of dignified continence careterm·dom including; respect, empathy, trust, privacyterm·key, autonomyterm·key and communication. These findings align well with the findings of a systematic review by Kogan, Wilber, & Mosqueda [ 83 ] who identified six key domains of person-centred careterm·dom for older adults: (a) holistic or whole-person care, (b) respect and value, (c) choice, (d) dignityterm·key, (e) self-determination, and (f) purposeful living or encouragement of continued social roles (p. e1). Whilst there are many similarities between the domains of person-centred careterm·dom and dignity-protective continence careterm·dom there are some key differences relating to privacyterm·key, empathy and communication.

The findings of this study revealed several practices that could uphold the dignityterm·key of older people who are care dependent who require continence careterm·dom. In practice, nursing home staff engage in several practices that result in residents’ incontinenceterm·key being contained and thus concealed, with incontinenceterm·key products, ostensibly to protect residents’ dignityterm·key [ 32 ]. Whilst the desire to protect residents’ dignityterm·key is commendable, the findings of this concept analysis suggest that concealment is only one of several aspects of dignity-protective care.

The use of humour in the care encounter was identified as a key part of therapeutic communicationterm·key and can be dignity-protective or dignity-violating [ 86 , 87 ]. Caregivers may use humour judiciously to alleviate fears and protect dignityterm·key [ 86 ] however they also need to aware of its potential to humiliate people [ 87 ].

Dignity-protective continence careterm·dom is care that is delivered with compassion and respect. Previous studies have highlighted the importance of dignified continence careterm·dom at end of life [ 78 ] however, it is important to consider the subjective nature of dignityterm·key that varies by person and throughout life [ 20 ]. Receiving continence careterm·dom may challenge a person’s sense of dignityterm·key but for some this may be relatively insignificant compared to other problems (eg: pain or other symptoms) [ 78 ]. Dignityterm·key may not be as big a concern at the end of life as is it is for a healthy individual [ 77 ] and studies suggest that here a loss of dignityterm·key may be accepted as a “trade-off” [ 78 ]. Matiti and Trorey used the term perceptual adjustmentterm·dom to describe a process by which a person forecasts the potential indignities that he or she expects to suffer whilst in care, “mentally analyses the situation and adjusts to a level that he or she feels comfortable enough to accept” [ [ 88 ] p. 741]. In relation to continence careterm·dom, fears might relate to being ridiculed or humiliated [ 22 , 75 ], being naked [ 78 ], invasion of personal space, loss of self-control [ 78 ] and loss of independence [ 88 ].

The concept analysis highlighted a number of consequencesterm of undignified continence careterm·dom for patients, carers and organizations, including shame, humiliation, embarrassment (for patients), stress, burnout and feeling emotionally and ethically challenged (for care staff) and financial and staffing pressures (for organizations). Whilst incontinenceterm·key itself is not a life threatening illness, it has been viewed by some as a state worse than death [ 89 ]. It is difficult to ascertain if it is the incontinenceterm·key or the care dependence and subsequent loss of dignityterm·key that incites fear and dread. We hypothesize that the provision of dignified continence careterm·dom may mitigate this fear.

Although some international guidelines identified dignityterm·key as an important part of care, few attempted to define or give examples of what dignityterm·key in relation to continence careterm·dom might look like. One exception to this is the ‘Dignity in Continence Careterm·dom Framework’ [ 30 ] that promotes the dignityterm·key of the person as the overarching goal of care – i.e. dignityterm·key as the person perceives it, rather the perspective of the person delivering care. By defining the attributes of dignity protective continence careterm·dom, our concept analysis builds on, and operationalises the framework. Based on the findings of this analysis, we suggest dignity in continence careterm·dom (dignity-protective continence careterm·dom) is operationalised through practices that promote respect, empathy, trust, privacyterm·key, autonomyterm·key and communication.

Many of the key organizational antecedentsterm to dignified care identified from the analysis suggest that providing dignified continence careterm·dom requires time and resources. As the population ages, the acuity and complexity of care required in long-term careterm·key settings will also increase. Coupled with this, the sector faces severe constraints on budgets, staffing, time and resources, all of which affect the provision of dignified care [ 26 ]. By clarifying the concept dignityterm·key, and exploring how it is manifested in continence careterm·dom, the findings of this concept analysis can support reflection on the identified antecedentsterm and attributes as part of appropriate staff education and training [ 76 , 77 ].

The purpose of this analysis was to develop a better understanding of dignity-protective continence careterm·dom, i.e. to describe and explain the concept of dignityterm·key as it relates to continence careterm·dom for older people in organisations that provide long-term formal care. Whist literature was retrieved from a variety of sources, using a structured search process, there are some limitations. Studies in languages other than English were not accessed and a significant body of literature may therefore have been excluded. Jacelon [ 20 ] makes the point that understandings of dignityterm·key are not only historically and politically relative, they are also culturally dependent. The fact that the search yield information from five Western countries is an important limitation.

Moreover, in a seminal article about dignity-conserving care for people at the end of life, Harvey Chochinov pointed out that “If the preservation of dignityterm·key is to be a targeted goal of palliation, the patient’s sense of dignityterm·key must first be thoroughly understood” [ 5 ]. Our search yielded only seven studies about dignity-protective continence careterm·dom from the perspectives of people with continence careterm·dom needs. Further research about their experiences and preferences for care is required to deepen our understandings of how to define and thus operationalise dignity-protective continence careterm·dom. A broader search may have produced a more comprehensive definition of dignityterm·key. Additionally, employing an alternative method for the concept analysis may have produced a different outcome (eg Avant & Walker’s).

Conclusion This study reveals that although dignityterm·key is subjectively experienced, the key attributes of dignity-protective continence careterm·dom are privacyterm·key, respect, autonomyterm·key, empathy, trust, and communication, in the caregiving encounter. An understanding of the essential attributes of dignity-protective continence careterm·dom could allow caregivers and healthcare professionals to challenge practices that violate dignityterm·key, and recognize caring opportunities for protecting the dignityterm·key of vulnerable and care-dependent older citizens. It could also inform the development of an instrument to evaluate the whether continence careterm·dom is delivered in a way that protects the person’s dignityterm·key.
