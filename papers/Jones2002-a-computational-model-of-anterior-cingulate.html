phenomenonresponse conflict monitoring in anterior cingulate cortex
claim-typemechanism
mechanismRepeated high-frequency stimuli build residual activation (an expectancy) in one stimulus-response pathway, so a low-frequency stimulus must overcome that preactivation, and the resulting competition is what registers as conflict in ACC. — “Specifically, in the model, repeated presentations of high-frequency stimuli create an expectancy for continued future repetitions, which results in residual activity in the expected stimulus– response pathway.”
mechanismConflict at the decision layer produces ACC-indexed signal that lowers strategic priming, which raises control (slower, more accurate responding) on subsequent trials. — “The role of the conflict detector was to increase control by decreasing strategic priming in response to conflict.”
mechanismBecause the incorrect nontarget decision unit in one-response tasks is not connected to a response execution unit, transient wrong-channel activation never becomes an overt error, so the task difference arises downstream of conflict detection rather than at it. — “The difference in behavioral profiles across the two tasks is captured in the model by the simple fact that premature activation of the incorrect nontarget decision layer unit does not result in an overt behavior under a one-response task situation.”
relation — “The present model extends this previous work by adding the conflict-control loop (Botvinick et al., 2001) into the Cho et al. model.”
relation — “As was mentioned above, the basic architecture was developed in Usher and McClelland (2001) and was extended by Botvinick et al. (2001) and Cho et al. (2002).”
relation — “The neuroimaging data used for the present study were originally reported in Braver et al. (2001).”
replicationuntested
limitationThe trial-by-trial fit of the model to individual subjects' behaviour is low, which the authors attribute partly to unsimulated human noise processes. — “Although these numbers indicate a somewhat low fit, it is important to note that noise processes in humans (which are poorly understood) may make a significant contribution to trialby-trial behavioral performance.”
limitationThe imaging reanalysis testing the sequence prediction was hampered by hemodynamic overlap in rapid event-related fMRI. — “This analysis was complicated by our use of rapid event-related fMRI and the slow evolution of the hemodynamic response.”
limitationThe model covers only a specific type of control, not cognitive control in general. — “The present study was not intended to provide a complete account of the myriad processes that contribute to cognitive control.”

content-observations: finding · quantity · variable · definition · key-term — grounded in the full text
findingThe simulated conflict measure was higher on low-frequency trials in every task condition, matching the ACC imaging result. — “There was significantly greater conflict for low- versus high-frequency events in all task conditions (all ps , .001).”
findingThe model reproduced the puzzling exception that accuracy to low-frequency targets in the oddball (one-response) task is near perfect and not degraded by low frequency. — “In this condition, low-frequency responses were nearly perfectly accurate and were not significantly different from responses to high-frequency [F(1,63) = 0.297, p = .588] or equal-frequency [F(1,63) = 0.133, p = .717] events.”
findingSimulated conflict tracked not only global stimulus frequency but the fine-grained five-trial sequential history of stimuli. — “The high correlation observed between conflict and empirical data behavioral performance (accuracy, r = .934; RT, r = .891) indicates that conflict not only tracks global frequency but also the local sequential history of stimuli.”
findingA reanalysis of the imaging data confirmed the model's novel prediction that ACC activity fluctuates with local sequence even under globally equal frequencies. — “the empirical data from the ACC corresponds rather well with the conflict predictions obtained from the model (r = .856, p , .001), accounting for 73% of the variance present in the sequential pattern of ACC activation”
findingTrials whose four preceding stimuli were repetitions had the fastest reaction times and fewest errors, in both human and simulated data. — “In fact, the four categories ending in RR had the four lowest RTs and error rates in both the behavioral and the simulated data.”
findingRemoving the conflict-monitoring control loop degraded the model's ability to predict trial-by-trial performance, showing the loop makes a real contribution. — “The performance of the model with no conflictmonitoring control loop was slightly less effective at predicting performance, thus indicating a contribution of this component of the model.”
findingSubjects showed speed-accuracy adjustments after slow (high-conflict) trials as well as after errors, exactly as the intact conflict-control model predicted. — “There were significant adjustments in RT following errors [t(60) = 6.50, p , .001], in error rate following slow RT trials [t(64) = 25.76, p , .001], and in both RT and error rate following fast RT trials [RT, t(64) = 22.6, p , .01; error rate, t(64) = 4.81, p , .001].”
quantity65 subjects / 14 subjects — sample sizes of the behavioral and the neuroimaging studies whose data the simulations were fitted to — “There were 65 subjects (47 females and 18 males; mean age, 19.3 years; range, 17 –25 years) in the behavioral study and 14 subjects (5 males and 9 females; mean age, 22.9 years; range, 18–27 years) in the neuroimaging study.”
quantity17%, 50%, 83% — the three target-frequency conditions used in each version of the task — “Each version of the task (one-response, two-response) had three frequency conditions: low-frequency target/R1 (target = 17%), equalfrequency (target = 50%), and low-frequency nontarget/ R2 (target = 83%).”
quantityr = .856 ... 73% — correlation between simulated conflict and reanalyzed ACC activity across matched sequence pairs, and the share of variance it explains — “the empirical data from the ACC corresponds rather well with the conflict predictions obtained from the model (r = .856, p , .001), accounting for 73% of the variance present in the sequential pattern of ACC activation”
quantity16.6% / 6.8% — variance in trial-by-trial reaction time and in error explained by the full model — “the model accounts for 16.6% of the variance present in the RT and 6.8% of the variance present in error”
quantity2.5% / 1.5% — the small but reliable extra variance attributable specifically to the conflict-control loop — “Thus, although the conflictcontrol effect was rather small—accounting for 2.5% of the total variation in the subjects’ RT scores and 1.5% of variance in error rates—it was highly reliable.”
quantity64 out of 65 — subjects for whom adding the conflict-control loop improved the model's RT prediction — “This partial correlation was computed separately for each subject and was positive for 64 out of 65 subjects in the RT analysis (mean partial correlation = .141; 99% confidence”
quantityF(1,63) = 9,143 — test that the low-vs-high frequency conflict difference exceeded the difference under equal frequency — “the conflict difference between low- and high-frequency stimuli was significantly greater than the difference between stimulus types under equalfrequency conditions [F(1,63) = 9,143, p , .001].”
variablestimulus frequency (low / equal / high) (independent) — “we suggest that the tasks tap a common factor, stimulus frequency, that modulates the degree of conflict present on any given trial.”
variabletask type (one-response go/no-go and oddball vs two-response 2AFC) (independent) — “The subjects performed two types of speeded response tasks: one-response (go/no-go/oddball) and two-response (2AFC).”
variablelocal sequential history (repetition/alternation category over the previous five trials) (independent) — “Analyses of data were conducted after categorizing each stimulus as a function of the previous five trials”
variablereaction time and error rate (dependent) — “mean RT and percentage of error were calculated across subjects (both actual and simulated) for each frequency condition”

variablesimulated conflict index (dependent) — “Mean simulated conflict for each stimulus was also calculated as a function of frequency and task condition for comparison against imaging data from the ACC”
variablelesioned model (strategic priming fixed at its mean, conflict-control loop removed) (control) — “The first was with the intact model, whereas the second was with the strategic priming mechanism (which modulates control as a function of conflict) lesioned.”
definitionresponse conflict (as simultaneous coactivation of incompatible responses) — “Conflict can be thought of as the simultaneous coactivation of incompatible responses, such as trying to name the ink color of the word “red” written in green ink in the classic Stroop task.”
definitionresponse conflict (origin in constraints on response generation) — “Response conflict arises because of either physical or task-induced constraints on response generation.”
definitionrepetition priming and alternation priming — “This priming came in two forms: (1) repetition priming (facilitation of a given stimulus after that stimulus had previously been observed) and (2) alternation priming (facilitation of the stimulus opposite to that last observed, following a previous alternation).”
definitionconflict (computational measure based on the Hopfield energy) — “Conflict was computed as in previous simulations (Botvinick et al., 2001) and corresponded to a simplified form of the well-known Hopfield energy measure (Hopfield, 1982).”
