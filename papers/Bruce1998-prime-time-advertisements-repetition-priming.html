phenomenonrepetition priming of face recognition
claim-typeexistence
mechanismRepeated exposure strengthens the connections between face recognition units and person identity nodes, so later familiarity decisions are reached faster — “According to Burton et al.'s (1990) interactive activation (structural) account, repetition priming of faces arises as a result of the strengthening of connections between/ace recognition units (nodes responding to particular individual faces) and person identity nodes (multimodal nodes re sponding to faces, voices, etc.), the proposed locus offa miliarity decisions.”
mechanismThe same link-strengthening process that produces priming is the process by which new faces are learned and stored representations of familiar faces are updated by new exemplars — “On this account, then, repetition priming taps the same pro cess that underlies the perceptual learning of faces and is the same mechanism by which new faces are learned and representations of familiar faces are modified through encounters with new exemplars.”
mechanismPriming reflects the ongoing operation of a system that develops and updates the representations used to identify faces, rather than retrieval of the priming episode — “For the moment, however,we maintain our current hypothesis that priming does reflect the operation of a system mediating the development and updating of the representations used to identify faces.”
relation — “The sim ilar performance on same and different exemplars of fa miliar faces replicates Bruce (1982) and probably reflects a ceiling effect.”
relation — “The results presented here suggest that the longevity of face priming (cf. Flude, 1993)does notarise because ofthe maintenance of (at least superficial aspects of) environ mental context.”
relation — “However, the interactive activation account, as currently articulated, would not readily accommodate the effects of changes in environ mental context, and, to the extent that priming is, in gen eral, robust from the posters, this supports the model.”
replicationreplicated
limitationExposure duration and frequency for poster faces were uncontrolled, so absolute priming magnitudes for poster versus lab faces are not directly comparable. — “This means that we must be cautious about comparing the absolute size ofthe priming obtained from the lab and poster faces.”
limitationExperiment 3 could not counterbalance items across conditions, leaving only four items per cell and an underpowered items analysis. — “As there were only four items in each cell and counterbalancing was not possible, the items analysis for this experiment lacks power and is not reported.”
limitationThe poster-versus-lab comparison in Experiment 1 was severely underpowered. — “However, the power of this comparison within this exper iment was very low (0.22, assuming a medium effect size, as defined by Cohen, 1977).”
limitationThe contextual manipulation used cues known to matter for unfamiliar-face memory, but may not have changed the functional context relevant to identifying familiar faces. — “However, some caution should also be exercised over these findings, since, although the contextual manipulation in these experiments encompassed changes found to in fluence memory for unfamiliar faces, it is not clear to what extent similar variations capture the functional aspects of the context that are important for the identification of or memory for familiar faces.”

findingFaces first encountered on subject-recruitment posters outside the laboratory produced measurable repetition priming of laboratory familiarity decisions, even when the poster never mentioned face recognition. — “Here we show that gains in face recognition in the laboratory are found from faces initially seen in a rather different context on subject recruitment posters, even when the advertisements make no specific mention of experi ments involving face recognition.”
findingPoster priming was reliable across all four experiments but smaller in magnitude than priming from faces seen in the laboratory. — “1. Repetition priming can be demonstrated for faces shown on advertising posters (Experiments 1-4), although it was reduced in magnitude, as compared with that found for faces encountered in the lab (Experiments 2-4).”
findingPriming survived fully incidental exposure, where participants were never required to look at or name the poster faces. — “2. Repetition priming was found even when the faces were presented quite incidentally and subjects were not obliged to look at them (Experiments 2 and 3).”
findingChanging the pictured exemplar between prime and test reduced priming by about the same amount in the poster and laboratory conditions, so exemplar change and context change acted additively rather than interactively. — “3. Changing the pictorial exemplar between the prime and test phases affected priming similarly in lab and on the poster (Experiments 3 and 4). Thus, the effect ofreduced priming after a change ofview is additive with reductions observed after a change in the environmental context within which the faces were primed.”
findingChanging the background colour behind the face between prime and test had no detectable effect on the size of the priming effect. — “5. A change in the background color on which the faces were presented had no measurable effects on the amount of priming (Experiment 4).”
findingExplicit episodic memory for the poster faces was very poor: they neither interfered as distractors nor were recognised at anything like the rate of laboratory faces. — “6. Episodic memory for the faces shown on the poster was weak (Experiments 3 and 4). These faces did not give rise to a sense of familiarity that interfered with their re jection as distractors in a test of memory for faces seen in the lab, and they were recognized very much less well than faces seen in the lab when instructions requested subjects to consider both lab and poster faces as old.”
findingThe authors conclude that face priming does not require reinstating the environmental context of the priming episode, since medium, venue, experimenter presence and task all differed. — “At a theoretical level, we have demonstrated convinc ingly that repetition priming of faces does not require the reinstatement of the environmental context specifically associated with the experiment itself. The faces on the posters were seen on different material (on paper), in a dif ferent venue, without the experimenter present, and, in some cases (Experiments 2 and 3), with different task de mands from those that were encountered in the laboratory, yet priming was observed in these circumstances.”
findingA methodological consequence drawn is that researchers testing famous-face recognition must screen out participants who saw those faces in any earlier, apparently unrelated study. — “Although we cannot conclude that priming will transcend changes in all kinds of context (as we discuss below), our observation of substantial priming even from the posters presenting faces incidentally suggests that anyone whose research tests recognition of famous faces should be care ful to exclude subjects who may previously have seen any of the faces in any other experiments, however unrelated.”
quantity622 msec / 611 msec / 737 msec — Experiment 1 mean familiarity-decision times for poster-primed, lab-primed and unprimed famous faces — “The mean times to make familiarity decisions to the three sets of items in the test phase were as follows: Group 1 (poster), 622 msec (SD= 88 msec); Group 2 (lab), 611 msec (SD= 85 msec); and Group 3 (unprimed), 737 msec (SD= 167 msec).”
quantity773 msec / 678 msec / 822 msec — Experiment 2 mean response times for incidentally poster-primed, lab-primed and unprimed faces, showing significant but weaker poster priming — “Faces that had been previously seen incidentally on posters were responded to at test with a mean of773 msec (SD= l44msec), compared with 678 msec (SD= 112msec) for faces seen and named initially in the lab and 822 msec (SD = 190 msec) for unprimed faces.”
quantityF(l,46) = l7.45, p < .001 — Experiment 3 effect of same versus different pictorial exemplar on the amount of priming — “showed significantly greater priming for same than for different exemplars [F(l,46) = l7.45,p < .001] and a trend for greater priming in the lab condition than in the poster condition [F(l,46) = 3.11, p = .085] but no other significant effects or interactions (all other ps > .15).”
