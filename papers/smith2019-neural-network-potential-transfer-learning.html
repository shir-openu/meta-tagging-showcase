Approaching coupled cluster accuracy with a general-purpose neural network potentialterm·key through transfer learningterm·mth Abstract Computational modeling of chemical and biological systems at atomic resolution is a crucial tool in the chemist’s toolset. The use of computer simulations requires a balance between cost and accuracy: quantum-mechanical methods provide high accuracy but are computationally expensive and scale poorly to large systems, while classical force fields are cheap and scalable, but lack transferability to new systems. Machine learningterm·mth can be used to achieve the best of both approaches. Here we train a general-purpose neural network potentialterm·key (ANI-1ccxterm·key) that approaches CCSD(T)term·key/CBS accuracy on benchmarks for reaction thermochemistry, isomerization, and drug-like molecular torsionsterm·key. This is achieved by training a network to DFTterm·mth data then using transfer learningterm·mth techniques to retrain on a dataset of gold standard QMterm·mth calculations (CCSD(T)term·key/CBS) that optimally spans chemical space. The resulting potential is broadly applicable to materials science, biology, and chemistry, and billions of times faster than CCSD(T)term·key/CBS calculations.

Subject terms: Physical chemistry, Computational chemistry Introduction The central questions in modern chemistry relate to the identification and synthesis of molecules for useful applications. Historically, discoveries have often been serendipitous, driven by a combination of intuition and experimental trial and error 1 , 2 . In the modern age, the computer revolution has brought about powerful computational methods based on quantum mechanicsterm·dom (QMterm·mth) to create a new paradigm for chemistry research 3 , 4 . At great computational expense, these methods can provide accurate chemical properties (e.g., energies, forces, structures, reactivity, etc.) for a wide range of molecular systems. Coupled-cluster theoryterm·mth systematically approaches the exact solution to the Schrödinger equation, and is considered a gold standard for many quantum chemistry applications 5 – 7 . When CCSD(T)term·key (coupled cluster considering single, double, and perturbative triple excitations) calculations are combined with an extrapolation to the complete basis set limit (CBS) 8 , 9 , even the hardest to predict non-covalent and intermolecular interactions can be computed quantitatively 10 . However, coupled-cluster theoryterm·mth at the level of CCSD(T)term·key/CBS is computationally expensive, and often impractical for systems with more than a dozen atoms.

Since the computational cost of highly accurate QMterm·mth methods can be impractical, researchers often seek to trade accuracy for speed. Density functional theoryterm·mth (DFTterm·mth) 11 – 13 , perhaps the most popular QMterm·mth method, is much faster than coupled-cluster theoryterm·mth. In practice, however, DFTterm·mth requires empirical selection of a density functional, and so DFT-computed properties are not as reliable and objective as coupled-cluster techniques at guiding experimental science. Even stronger approximations can be made to achieve better efficiency. For example, classical force fields are commonly employed to enable large scale dynamical simulation such as protein folding 14 , ligand-protein docking 15 , or the dynamics of dislocations in materials 16 . These models are often fragile; a force field fit to one system may not accurately model other systems 17 . An outstanding challenge is to simultaneously capture a great diversity of chemical processes with a single linear-scaling model potential.

Machine learningterm·mth (MLterm·mth) methods have seen much success in the last decade due to increased availability of data and improved algorithms 18 – 20 . Applications of MLterm·mth are becoming increasingly common in experimental and computational chemistry. Recent chemistry related work reports on MLterm·mth models for chemical reactions 21 , 22 , potential energy surfaces 23 – 27 , forces 28 – 30 , atomization energies 31 – 33 , atomic partial charges 32 , 34 – 36 , molecular dipoles 26 , 37 , 38 , materials discovery 39 – 41 , and protein-ligand complex scoring 42 . Many of these studies represent important and continued progress toward MLterm·mth models of quantum chemistry that are transferable (i.e., applicable to related, but new chemical processes) and extensible (i.e., accurate when applied to larger systems). These advances aim to revolutionize chemistry through applications to chemical and biological systems. Since molecular dynamics simulations underpin much of computational chemistry and biology, transferable, accurate, and fast prediction of molecular energies and forces is particularly important for the next generation of linear-scaling model potential energy surfaces.

Transferable and extensible MLterm·mth potentials often require training on very large data sets. One such approach is the ANI class of methods. The ANI-1 potential aims to work broadly for molecules in organic chemistry 43 . A key component of this potential is the ANI-1 data set, which consists of DFTterm·mth energies for 22M randomly selected molecular conformations from 57k distinct small molecules 44 . This vast amount of data would be impractical to generate at a level of theory more accurate than DFTterm·mth 45 . However, advances in machine learningterm·mth methodologies are greatly reducing the required data set sizesterm·dom. The ANI-1x data set, constructed using active learningterm·mth, contains DFTterm·mth data for 5M conformations of molecules with an average size of 15 atoms 25 . Active learningterm·mth iteratively adds new QMterm·mth calculations to the data set for specific cases where the current MLterm·mth model cannot make a good prediction. Despite the much smaller size of the ANI-1x data set, potentials trained on it vastly outperform those trained on the ANI-1 data set, especially on transferability and extensibility benchmarks. Even with the success of the ANI-1x potential, its true accuracy is still reliant upon the accuracy of the underlying DFTterm·mth data.

A remaining challenge is to develop ML-based potentials that reach coupled-cluster-level accuracy while retaining transferability and extensibility over a broad chemical space. The difficulty is that data sets with CCSD(T)-level accuracy are very expensive to construct and therefore tend to be limited in chemical diversity. Previous studies have trained on high-quality QMterm·mth data for small molecules at equilibrium conformations 46 , 47 and for non-equilibrium conformations of a single molecule 48 . A limitation is that MLterm·mth models trained on data sets which lack chemical diversity are not expected to be transferable or extensible to new systems. The present work uses transfer learningterm·mth 49 , 50 to train an MLterm·mth potential that is accurate, transferable, extensible, and therefore, broadly applicable. In transfer learningterm·mth, one begins with a model trained on data from one task and then retrains the model on data from a different, but related task, often yielding high-accuracy predictions 51 – 53 even when data are sparsely available. In our application, we begin by training a neural network on a large quantity of lower-accuracy DFTterm·mth data (the ANI-1x data set with 5 M non-equilibrium molecular conformations 25 ), and then we retrain to a much smaller data set (about 500k intelligently selected conformations from ANI-1x) at the CCSD(T)term·key/CBS level of accuracy. Such a high-quality and diverse data set is a first of its kind for training machine learning-based potentials. The resulting general-purpose potential, ANI-1ccxterm·key, and data set exceeds the accuracy of DFTterm·mth in benchmarks for isomerization energies, reaction energies, molecular torsionterm·key profiles, and energies and forces at non-equilibrium geometries, while being roughly nine orders of magnitude faster than DFTterm·mth. The ANI-1ccxterm·key potential is available on GitHub ( https://github.com/isayevpersName/ASE_ANI ) as a user-friendly Python interface integrated with the Atomic Simulation Environment 54 package (ASE; https://wiki.fysik.dtu.dk/ase/ ).

To test transferability and extensibility, we employ four benchmarks to appraise the accuracy of molecular energies and forces, reaction thermochemistry, and the computation of torsional profiles on systems consisting of CHNO. The GDB-10to13 benchmark 25 is designed to evaluate relative energies, atomization energies, and force calculations on a random sample of 2996 molecules containing 10–13 C, N, or O atoms (with H added to saturate the molecules). The GDB-10to13 molecules are randomly perturbed along their normal modes to produce between 12 and 24 non-equilibrium conformations per molecule. HC7/11 55 is a benchmark designed to gauge the accuracy of hydrocarbon reaction and isomerization energies. The ISOL6 benchmark 56 (a subset of the ISOL24/11 benchmark) measures isomerization energies for organic molecules. Finally, we test on the Genentech torsion benchmark 57 , which contains 62 diverse organic molecule torsion profiles (45 containing only CHNO).

Methods An efficient and accurate CCSD(T)term·key/CBS approximation Recalculating even 10% of the ANI-1x data set (i.e., 500k molecules) with conventional CCSD(T)term·key/CBS would require enormous computational resources. Therefore, we developed an approximation scheme (herein referred to as CCSD(T)term·key*/CBS) that allows highly accurate energy calculations in a high-throughput fashion.

Our CCSD(T)term·key*/CBS method is a computationally efficient approximation of CCSD(T)term·key/CBS energies that takes advantage of the linear-scaling domain-localized DPLNO-CCSD(T) method developed by Neese et al. 58 which is implemented in the ORCA software package 59 . It provides an affordable alternative capable of achieving near CCSD(T)term·key accuracy at a fraction of the computational cost. The DLPNO approximation relies on the MP2 method to estimate energy contributions from interacting electron pairs and effectively reduce the active orbital space. Table 3 provides accuracy and timing benchmarks, clearly showing our CCSD(T)term·key*/CBS approximation provides accurate energies vs. the CCSD(T)-F12 level of theory 60 in a computationally efficient way. S66 and W4-11 are standard benchmarks for interaction and atomization energies of small molecules 61 , 62 . See Supplementary Table 1 for a more detailed comparison. Details of our CCSD(T)term·key*/CBS scheme plus additional benchmarks are given in supplemental information Section S1.1.

Computational cost and accuracy of our coupled-cluster approximation Using active learningterm·mth for CCSD(T)term·key*/CBS data set curation The existing ANI-1x active learningterm·mth generated data set 25 is used to train an initial DFTterm·mth (to the ωB97X/6-31G* model chemistry 63 ) potential, likewise dubbed ANI-1x. The ANI-1x data set consists of 5M conformations from 64k small molecules and complexes of molecules containing only CHNO atoms. All model and training procedures are detailed in the ANI-1 work 43 . Section S1.2 provides details of the architecture, selection of hyperparameters, and held out test set errors. To reduce variance and increase accuracy, all ANI results presented in this work are the ensembleterm·key prediction of eight ANI neural networks, i.e., the ANI-1x potential used in this work is an ensembleterm·key of eight ANI-1x neural networks trained to different splits of the ANI-1x data set and the ANI-1ccxterm·key network is built by transfer learningterm·mth from the eight ANI-1x networks 25 . The disagreement between predictions of ensembleterm·key members can be used as a proxy to the prediction error, enabling rapid identification of molecular conformations where the current ANI model fails.

Despite the efficiency of our CCSD(T)term·key*/CBS extrapolation scheme, optimal curation of the coupled-cluster data set is still essential since we can only perform a limited number of these calculations. As a source of structures for CCSD(T)term·key*/CBS data generation, we choose to systematically subsample the existing data set with 5M molecules, since this data set already provides a pool of highly diverse molecular configurations and conformations. We begin with an initial random subsample of 200k data points, then iteratively we select new data for coupled-cluster calculations according to maximal ensembleterm·key disagreement (i.e., query by committee 64 ). Through three iterations of coupled-cluster data generation using active learningterm·mth, we grow the coupled-cluster data set to about 480k molecules. To further improve the ANI potential’s description of torsion profiles, we also perform 20 iterations of active learningterm·mth 25 on random molecular torsionsterm·key from small and drug-like molecules to enhance ANI-1x with about 200k new DFTterm·mth calculations. The ANI driven torsion sampling technique is detailed in Section S1.3. Of these torsion conformations, we randomly select 10% for CCSD(T)term·key*/CBS calculations. The result is an enhanced ANI-1x DFTterm·mth data set containing 5.2M data points and a high-accuracy CCSD(T)term·key*/CBS data set containing about 500k data points.

Training to high-accuracy data using transfer learningterm·mth Here we describe the transfer learningterm·mth methodology (depicted schematically in Fig. 4 ) used to create ANI-1ccxterm·key. First, an ANI potential is trained to the DFTterm·mth data set with the new active learningterm·mth torsion data added, yielding a potential equivalent to the ANI-1x potential 25 . Note, a single ANI potential is composed of multiple ANI neural network models. We then retrain each ANI-1x model to the CCSD(T)term·key*/CBS data with 65,280 of the 325,248 optimizable neural network parameters held constant for each ANI model in the ensembleterm·key. Training a single ANI model to the original 5.2 million molecule data set takes ~4 h on a NVIDIA Titan V GPU, while retraining to the 500k molecule CCSD(T)term·key*/CBS data set takes around 30 min. Neural network parameters are organized into a set of hidden layers. The ANI models trained in this work contain four hidden layers; we leave two hidden layers to be optimized during the transfer learningterm·mth process, while the other two layers are left fixed to reduce the number of optimizable parameters during the training process and thus avoid overfitting to the smaller CCSD(T)term·key*/CBS data set. Details of ANI-1ccxterm·key’s performance on its test set are given in Supplementary Table 3 . An alternative to transfer learningterm·mth is Δ-learningterm·mth 46 . With Δ-learningterm·mth, one trains a new model to correct for the difference between CCSD(T)term·key/CBS and the existing model pretrained on DFTterm·mth data. Although Δ-learningterm·mth yields similar accuracy to transfer learningterm·mth, it needs to evaluate the neural networks twice to make inferences. More information on Δ-learningterm·mth and its accuracy is provided in Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 4 .
