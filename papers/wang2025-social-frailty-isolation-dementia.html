The overlap in current measurement tools for social frailtyterm·dom and social isolationterm·dom is noted, but in the context of dementiaterm·key, there is no evidence of how these concepts are understood or what precise interrelationship exists.

Methods Aim This simultaneous concept analysisterm·mth aimed to examine the concepts of social frailtyterm·dom and social isolationterm·dom within the context of dementiaterm·key. This analysis identified distinguishing, overlapping and similar attributes, antecedents and consequences for each concept and explored their interrelationships simultaneously.

Study Design This study employed simultaneous concept analysisterm·mth (SCA) using a framework developed by Haase et al. [ 33 , 34 ], based on Rodgers’ evolutionary concept analysis methodology [ 35 ]. This simultaneous concept analysisterm·mth was registered on the Open Science Framework [ 36 ]. The analysis involved nine steps [ 33 ], which are described in detail below: Step 1‐Development of the Consensus Group The consensus group comprised three researchers (Z.W., D.C., D.S.), whose research focuses on dementiaterm·key and social frailtyterm·dom. The core consensus group (Z.W., D.S.) met biweekly to discuss the concept analysis process including the development of the SCA strategy, refined definitions of each concept, identification of the specific elements of each concept, and their sequence of occurrence and clarification of interrelationships. The full consensus group met every 6 weeks to review the process over 7 months.

Step 2‐Selection of Concepts to be Analysed With the aim of this simultaneous concept analysisterm·mth, two concepts, named social frailtyterm·dom and social isolationterm·dom, were selected following comprehensive discussions between three researchers (Z.W., D.C., D.S.). The analyses were conducted for each concept simultaneously in the context of dementiaterm·key.

Step 3‐Refinement of the Concept Clarification Approach A scoping review of the literature was performed. Specific elements of this approach are described below: Data Source The literature searches were performed on six databases: Ovid Medline, CINAHL, PsycINFO, Scopus, Embase and Cochrane Library.

Literature Search The literature search followed the guidelines for scoping review [ 37 ]. A pilot search on PubMed was conducted to identify all terms related to the two concepts of social frailtyterm·dom and social isolationterm·dom as well as dementiaterm·key. The MeSH terms ‘Social Isolationterm·dom’, ‘Dementiaterm·key’ and ‘Alzheimer Disease’ were identified. However, there were no relevant MeSH terms for ‘social frailtyterm·dom’. The final literature search strategy was refined with the assistance of a librarian. The final search was conducted in December 2023. All searches were limited to English, without a time limit. Detailed search strategies for all databases and preliminary search results are presented in Supporting Information S1 : Appendix 1.

Eligibility Criteria According to the PRISMA‐ScR [ 37 ] guidelines, the publications were screened using the following inclusion criteria (1) provide conceptual or operational definitions or descriptions of social frailtyterm·dom, or social isolationterm·dom, (2) address the topic within the context of dementiaterm·key, (3) reporting from the perspectives such as various contexts including different care settings (e.g., community, residential care or acute care), diverse populations, and professional background of authors, (4) published in peer‐reviewed journals following any study design including review articles, editorials, and commentaries and (5) published in English. Following a semantic approach, publications were included if they defined the terms social frailtyterm·dom or social isolationterm·dom. Publications excluded were those that (1) include social frailtyterm·dom or social isolationterm·dom but did not provide a definition or description, (2) were not in the context of dementiaterm·key, (3) were not published in peer‐reviewed journals, (4) were only conference paper, (5) were not available in English, (6) focussed on social frailtyterm·dom or social isolationterm·dom only in caregivers of people with dementiaterm·key considering that this is not the scope of this SCA, (7) were protocol registrations on an online platform or (8) were not available in full‐text.

Literature Selection The search results were imported to Endnote, and afterwards to Rayyan for de‐duplication and systematic screening. A random selection of publications (10% of the search results) was piloted by three researchers for the screening of titles and abstracts (Z.W., D.S., H.Z.). The conflicts between the researchers were resolved by discussions and consensus. This ensured the clarification and refinement of the inclusion and exclusion criteria. After that, two researchers (Z.W. and H.Z.) completed the title and abstract screening for the remaining publications. For the full text screening, a random selection of 10% of the publications were screened by two researchers (Z.W., D.S.), and the remaining publications were screened by one researcher (Z.W.). In case of unclarity regarding inclusion of a full text publication, discussions were held between two researchers (Z.W., D.S.) and decisions were made by consensus.

Data Extraction Data extraction was carried out following the data extraction form developed by Z.W. and D.S., including (1) title, the year of publication, author (s)’ names and their disciplines; (2) design of the publication; (3) aims of the publication; (4) population/participant characteristics, setting, assessment tools (if any); (5) conceptual definitions, operational definitions and narrative descriptions by people with dementiaterm·key as reported; (6) factors identified as antecedents of the concepts; (7) identified consequences of the concepts; (8) concepts identified by authors as relevant to the concepts of interest; (9) ‘alternative terms’ used by authors to refer to the concepts; (10)any examples (such as cases) of social frailtyterm·dom or social isolationterm·dom provided by authors; (11) information reported as the primary or secondary source; (12) other notes. Data was extracted from the included publications by Z.W. and the data was reviewed by DS for accuracy.

Step 4‐Clarification of Individual Concepts According to Haase et al. [ 33 ], the purpose of data analysis is not to develop a new, complete or finished definition of concepts, but rather to provide clarification of each concept. A thematic analysis was performed as suggested by Haase et al. [ 33 ] to examine the concepts of social frailtyterm·dom and social isolationterm·dom to strengthen the characterisation and clarification of each concept. Braun and Clarke's six steps [ 38 , 39 ] were followed for the thematic analysis. The extracted data was categorised into the groups of conceptual definitions, operational definitions and narrative descriptions. The data was first read and coded by Z.W. [ 33 , 40 ]. As part of this process, separate coding manuals were developed for attributes, antecedents and consequences. Another researcher (D.S.) then reviewed this coding and consensus was reached on the coding. The codes were collated and categorised into draft constructs for each concept by Z.W. and D.S. The constructs, which represent themes in the thematic analysis process, were then compared with each other to identify the common factors and distinctive attributes, antecedents, and consequences of both concepts. The term ‘distinctive’ was used to describe an attribute, antecedent or consequence that is present in only one of the two concepts but not both, and therefore played a crucial role in differentiating one concept from another.

Step 5‐Development of Validity Matricesterm·mth Validity matricesterm·mth were constructed for attributes, antecedents and consequences (Z.W.) through the identification of common factors and an examination of the connections between concepts to attain theoretical coherence. During this iterative process, Z.W. and D.S. interrogated each element, identified inconsistencies and gaps and made revisions as needed until all the elements were identified within the context of dementiaterm·key.

Steps 6 and 7‐Revision of Individual Concept Clarification and Re‐Examination of Validity Matricesterm·mth The consensus group convened to review the previous classifications of concepts and make any required revisions. Additionally, the group engaged in discussions, examinations and re‐evaluations of the concept analyses and validity matricesterm·mth. Taking into account each element, considering others through constant comparisons to clarify the interrelationships and differences between the two concepts.

Step 8‐Development of a Process Modelterm·mth After a consensus on attributes, antecedents and consequences were more clearly delineated a process modelterm·mth was developed [ 33 ]. The model examined the similarities and differences across concepts, and the interrelationships across both concepts and processes throughout the model.

Discussion This simultaneous concept analysisterm·mth (SCA) examined conceptual definitions, operational definitions and narrative descriptions of concepts of social frailtyterm·dom and social isolationterm·dom. It also explored similarities and differences between antecedents, attributes and consequences of two concepts, and analysed their relationship in the context of dementiaterm·key. The detailed exploration of social frailtyterm·dom and social isolationterm·dom enhanced the understanding of the conceptual framework for social healthterm·dom of people with dementiaterm·key [ 8 ], where social healthterm·dom is both an outcome and vital part of managing dementiaterm·key, emphasising the need for addressing the social healthterm·dom risks of people with dementiaterm·key.

Although the number of publications on the two concepts of social frailtyterm·dom and social isolationterm·dom included in this SCA is quite varied, similarities and differences exist in the definitions, descriptions, attributes, antecedents and consequences of each concept. In this SCA, only 10 publications were included for the analysis of social frailtyterm·dom while 57 publications were included for analysing the concept of social isolationterm·dom. Social frailtyterm·dom, which is suggested as a relatively recent concept within dementiaterm·key research, has drawn little academic interest and has only started to appear in the literature since 2017 [ 12 ]. In contrast, social isolationterm·dom has been extensively explored and well‐studied both for definitions [ 24 ] and measurement tools [ 43 , 87 ]. Other studies on social isolationterm·dom such as influencing factors [ 84 ], associations with dementiaterm·key incidence [ 63 ] and intervention programmes and their impact on individuals living with dementiaterm·key [ 72 ] also support the extensive body of research on this concept. This disparity indicates that social frailtyterm·dom is a less mature concept than social isolationterm·dom, which requires further investigation in the context of dementiaterm·key specifically regarding its application and social healthterm·dom outcomes in dementiaterm·key populations.

The publications included in this SCA highlighted the interlinked nature of the two concepts of social frailtyterm·dom and social isolationterm·dom, demonstrating different facets of each concept. Both concepts overlap in several aspects of their attributes, primarily in ‘limited or less social activities, engagement or interactions’ both on the conceptual and operational definitions of the two concepts [ 16 , 17 , 54 , 76 ]. Social frailtyterm·dom uniquely includes operationalised items such as ‘less frequently going out’ [ 54 ], while ‘engagement in social activities’ is a definition and measurement item of social isolationterm·dom [ 86 ]. Furthermore, social isolationterm·dom has also been referred as a degree of social disconnection [ 84 ] and inadequate quality and quantity of social connectednessterm·dom [ 57 ]. Social interactionsterm·dom refer to the direct acts of communication or engagement with others [ 104 ], while social connectednessterm·dom is a broader term that includes living arrangementsterm·dom, size of social networks, and engagement in social activities [ 105 ]. This suggested the concept of social frailtyterm·dom focuses only on the quantity of engaging in social activities or interactions, whereas social isolationterm·dom encompasses a broader range of measurements, focussing on both the quantity and quality of social connectednessterm·dom not only interactions.

Publications included in this SCA suggest that social frailtyterm·dom involves a one‐way reduction or absence in the attribute ‘visiting friends less’ [ 95 ] indicating that the number of visits to friends is reduced due to the reasons related to the person with dementiaterm·key. Studies suggested that such people with social frailtyterm·dom have more impaired ability in terms of verbal fluency [ 106 ] and have issues with hearing impairment [ 107 ], which might affect their desire, interest, or capacity to visit friends. This reflects another unique attribute ‘less talk’ [ 54 ], which was also an item in most measurement tools for social frailtyterm·dom. In contrast, social isolationterm·dom is suggested to refer to more of a reduction or absence in two‐way activities, for example, both visiting friends and being visited by friends [ 63 ]. This highlights a much broader impact extending beyond the socially isolated individuals' actions. It might also reflect the distinctive attribute ‘lack of social networks and social relationships’ [ 15 ]. Individuals experiencing social isolationterm·dom reported that being ignored by others contribute to reduced interactions with their social networks, leading to fewer visits by others [ 73 ]. Additionally, having ‘smaller social networks’ [ 69 ] is identified as a distinctive antecedent of social isolationterm·dom, suggesting that attributes and antecedents may overlap within the concept of social isolationterm·dom.

Although both concepts involve less or an absence of social support from friends, families or relatives, the attributes of social frailtyterm·dom distinctly reflect an absence of broader social resourcesterm·dom and financial difficultyterm·dom [ 16 , 17 , 95 ]. Social resourcesterm·dom refer to resources or services that individuals with dementiaterm·key and their family caregivers can apply for, such as day care, home help services, responsive care and home care, which can provide additional support to them [ 108 , 109 ]. Socially frail people with dementiaterm·key might retire early from their employment due to dementiaterm·key symptoms [ 110 ], which could to economic challenges and lack of access to healthcare or other essential services they need. The lack of social resourcesterm·dom might often intersect with economic challenges, highlighting how financial difficultyterm·dom directly affects an individual's ability to engage with and access required social resourcesterm·dom. Limitations with the welfare system might also lead socially frail individuals to financial difficulties [ 111 ]. This reflects the distinctive antecedent ‘impact of socio‐economic welfare’ of social frailtyterm·dom [ 95 ]. Financial difficultyterm·dom as one of the distinctive attributes of social frailtyterm·dom can be an important consideration under the socio‐economic factors [ 95 ] when developing or adapting measurement tools for social frailtyterm·dom and informing the development of prevention programmes for social frailtyterm·dom in the dementiaterm·key population.

‘Decreased access to social resourcesterm·dom’ [ 84 ] and having ‘lower social well‐being’ [ 90 ] are identified as distinctive consequences of social isolationterm·dom. People with dementiaterm·key who are experiencing social isolationterm·dom are more likely to experience poorer health outcomes than those who are not [ 112 ], possibly due to increasingly lacking access to essential social resourcesterm·dom and having reduced social well‐being. As social isolationterm·dom advances, the reduced access to social resourcesterm·dom and lower social well‐being potentially contribute to social frailtyterm·dom. This indicates that attributes of social frailtyterm·dom and consequences of social isolationterm·dom overlap, showing while both concepts are related, each has its distinct pathways and impacts. The relationship between this attribute of social frailtyterm·dom and consequence of social isolationterm·dom might suggest that social frailtyterm·dom and social isolationterm·dom are interrelated and mutually influence each other. Our results highlight the importance of early management of dementiaterm·key and social frailtyterm·dom by enhancing social resourcesterm·dom and decreasing the risks and effects of social isolationterm·dom. Based on the available literature, ‘cognitive declineterm·dom’ and ‘dementiaterm·key symptoms and diagnosis’ act as antecedents only for the concept of social isolationterm·dom. This suggests that the onset of cognitive declineterm·dom and dementiaterm·key might lead to individuals' social isolationterm·dom. Cognitive declineterm·dom may reduce an individual's ability to engage in social interactionsterm·dom due to memory loss [ 67 ], and lead to challenges in communication [ 68 , 74 ]. Similarly, the diagnosis of dementiaterm·key might contribute to social isolationterm·dom due to changes to social networks [ 72 ], potentially linking with stigma or discrimination from public [ 69 , 73 ]. On the other hand, ‘cognitive declineterm·dom’ and ‘increased risk for dementiaterm·key’ are recognised as consequences of both social frailtyterm·dom and social isolationterm·dom [ 54 , 61 , 65 , 99 ]. This indicates a relationship between both concepts, due to overlapping antecedents and consequences related to dementiaterm·key risk, symptoms and diagnosis (Figure 3 ). The concepts of social frailtyterm·dom and social isolationterm·dom have been used interchangeably in previous research [ 9 , 19 , 20 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 ], and this SCA identified some overlaps between these concepts; however, although they are interrelated, they are not identical. This suggests that managing social frailtyterm·dom might prevent individuals' cognitive declineterm·dom and reduce the risk of dementiaterm·key. It could also be possible to both prevent social isolationterm·dom by managing cognitive declineterm·dom and to prevent further progression of cognitive declineterm·dom and decrease the risk for dementiaterm·key by managing social isolationterm·dom. This can be addressed by development of prevention and management programmes targeting both social frailtyterm·dom and social isolationterm·dom in the dementiaterm·key population and those at risk. Additionally, it may help the recognition of the unique challenges faced by those with early onset dementiaterm·key, who may experience social frailtyterm·dom or social isolationterm·dom at a younger, potentially more active stage in their lives. Managing social frailtyterm·dom and social isolationterm·dom in early‐onset dementiaterm·key might also potentially slow the progression of their disease. Understanding the unique challenges experienced by this population can aid in developing more targeted care strategies and services for people with dementiaterm·key. This approach could help maintain the quality of life and improve health outcomes of people with dementiaterm·key, potentially reducing inequalities in dementiaterm·key population.

Examples of other concept analyses of social frailtyterm·dom [ 113 ] and social isolationterm·dom [ 114 ] in older adults have been noted. A recent concept analysis of social frailtyterm·dom in the context of older adults [ 113 ] reported that low cognitive function, including dementiaterm·key, is one of the psychological consequences of social frailtyterm·dom in older adults. This aligns with our analysis of ‘cognitive declineterm·dom’ as a consequence of social frailtyterm·dom in the context of dementiaterm·key. It suggests that social frailtyterm·dom should be managed in older adults to delay cognitive declineterm·dom in order to prevent dementiaterm·key onset. Another concept analysis, which was about social isolationterm·dom in older adults [ 114 ] also described dementiaterm·key and decline in cognition as possible causes of social isolationterm·dom in the older population, which also supports our results identifying ‘cognitive declineterm·dom’ and ‘increased dementiaterm·key risk’ as consequences of social isolationterm·dom in the context of dementiaterm·key. Our SCA further added to the existing knowledge by simultaneously analysing both concepts of social frailtyterm·dom and social isolationterm·dom and specifically exploring these concepts in the context of dementiaterm·key.

Potential limitations to this SCA include interpretive bias during the categorisation of conceptual and operational attributes of each concept, however, this simultaneous concept analysisterm·mth process followed guidelines outlined by Méndez‐Sánchez [ 115 ] and was reviewed and discussed by the consensus group members through regular meetings. The common factors and distinctive attributes, antecedents, or consequences of both concepts in this SCA are also limited by the quantity of publications, particularly concerning social frailtyterm·dom. It is important to note that the disparate number of publications on social frailtyterm·dom and social isolationterm·dom could have influenced the identification of distinctive attributes, antecedents or consequences. It is also recognised as a limitation that non‐English publications were excluded, thereby potentially omitting some relevant evidence. Another limitation is that people with dementiaterm·key and clinical practitioners were not directly involved in the development of this SCA. However, they were part of the process by providing feedback on the preliminary and final results.

Despite the limitations listed above, this SCA has several strengths. Following a comprehensive review and synthesis of the literature, this SCA is the first to identify the distinct and overlapping attributes, antecedents, and consequences of both social frailtyterm·dom and social isolationterm·dom in the context of dementiaterm·key, thereby enhancing the clarity of definitions and elucidating their interrelationships. Additionally, this SCA is the first to explore both the differences and similarities between these two important concepts. This SCA also included publications that were longitudinal studies including baseline populations without dementiaterm·key and their results contributed to the development of antecedents, attributes, and consequences of both concepts. We focussed on both of these population groups to identify the interrelationships in the literature and present the broader picture of social frailtyterm·dom and social isolationterm·dom in populations at pre and post diagnosis stages of dementiaterm·key. The results of this SCA will inform the development of more targeted and effective strategies to address social frailtyterm·dom and social isolationterm·dom in people with dementiaterm·key, contributing significantly to both academic and practical advancement in the field of dementiaterm·key care. Future studies should evaluate the results of this simultaneous concept analysisterm·mth using relevant primary data collection methods [ 33 ]. This SCA also highlighted the need for more publications particularly concerning social frailtyterm·dom in dementiaterm·key.

Conclusion This publication supports the use of SCA and the development of more comprehensive social frailtyterm·dom and social isolationterm·dom measurement tools for use with people with dementiaterm·key. Furthermore, the findings from this study can be used to better identify people with dementiaterm·key at risk of social frailtyterm·dom or social isolationterm·dom as well as guide future researchers and policymakers to develop social frailtyterm·dom or social isolationterm·dom prevention and management programmes, thereby improving the social healthterm·dom of people with dementiaterm·key.
