object of study — evidence — “The anatomic term “prefrontal” means different things to different people. These researchers often use variations of the term anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) to refer to agranular areas in the medial frontal cortex. By contrast, researchers studying rodents tend to use the term PFC to refer to the same medial frontal areas that are often called ACC in primates. Confusion can arise when the same anatomic term applies to different areas in different species. Anatomic terms should mean the same thing across species.”
research question — evidence — “Here, we attempt to address this issue by asking a simple question: What, if anything, is rodent PFC? The phrase “if anything” emphasizes the idea that we are not sure if there is a clear answer to our question. In particular, we do not wish to dispute the idea that rodents have a PFC. However, it may be that a standard set of anatomic terms, based on established cross-species homologies in the frontal cortex ( Vogt and Paxinos, 2014 ), should be used to describe the “rodent PFC.” These terms might or might not include the word “prefrontal” without implying that the area in question is not a prefrontal area.”
contribution — evidence — “Prefrontal cortex (PFC) means different things to different people. In recent years, there has been a major increase in publications on the PFC, especially using mice. However, inconsistencies in the nomenclature and anatomical boundaries of PFC areas has made it difficult for researchers to compare data and interpret findings across species. We conducted a meta-analysis of publications on the PFC of humans and rodents and found dramatic differences in the focus of research on these species. In addition, we compared anatomical terms and criteria across several common rodent brain atlases and found inconsistencies among, and even within, leading atlases. To assess the impact of these issues on the research community, we conducted a survey of established PFC researchers on their use of anatomical terms and found little consensus.”

PFCterm Nomenclatureterm in Rodents

Rodentterm PFCterm is agranular in most speciesterm and there is evidence for homologies, based on cytoarchitecture, among medial parts of the PFCterm, especially the anterior cingulateterm cortex (ACCterm), in rodents, humans, and monkeys ( Vogt and Gabriel, 1993 ; Vogt et al., 2013 ; Vogt and Paxinos, 2014 ). There have been numerous publications on the medial PFCterm in humans (3652 publications according to a PubMed search in June 2018, with some researchers referring to the area as ventromedial prefrontalterm or ventromedial frontal cortex) and rodents (4811 publications). Yet, confusionterm and controversy about the relevance of these rodentterm studies has persisted. We think that controversy about rodentterm PFCterm has persisted for three reasons, all of which reflect confusionterm about anatomicterm nomenclatureterm and not actual physical or physiologic issues. First, the core areas that compose the rodentterm PFCterm are characterized using two different nomenclatures (mPFCterm and ACCterm). Second, multiple terms and acronyms are used in prominent brain atlases, and the boundaries for the named areas are inconsistent across atlases. Third, hybridterm terms, such as prelimbicterm medial prefrontalterm cortex, have recently emerged in the literature, which are not used in humanterm or non-human primateterm studies. We discuss these issues below and argue that these issues have led to limited collaborationterm between rodentterm and humanterm researchers, evidenced by the small number of publications done in the same behavioral task across speciesterm.

Dual citizenship

Rodentterm studies are considered to be “prefrontalterm” when they report data from three cytoarchitectonically defined parts of the frontal cortex: the prelimbicterm, infralimbicterm, and anterior cingulateterm areas. Studies in humans and monkeys consider these regions to be part of the mPFCterm, which also includes Walker’s area 10 ( Walker, 1940 ) and the medial parts of area 9, which do not exist in rodents ( Semendeferi et al., 2001 ). A second scheme for cortical nomenclatureterm considers the three rodentterm medial frontal areas as part of the anterior cingulateterm cortex ( Vogt and Gabriel, 1993 ). As such, two distinct anatomicterm terms, ACCterm and mPFCterm, can be used to describe the same three cortical regions. Previous versions of stereotaxic atlases ( Paxinos and Watson, 1982 ; Zilles, 1985 ) referred to the more dorsal and caudal cingulateterm areas as being the cingulateterm cortex, while using the terms “prelimbicterm” and “infralimbicterm” to describe the rostral medial wall regions of the cortex, which may explain why some rodentterm studies report functional differences between the ACCterm and mPFCterm ( Seamans et al., 1995 ). As our surveyterm·mth described below revealed, it seems that some rodentterm PFCterm researchers are aware of, or seemingly prefer, one naming scheme and not the other. This issue has been a major factor in the lack of clarity about the rodentterm PFCterm, and it is confusing to non-PFC researchers.

Alphabet soup/lines in the sand

Brain atlases have not made it any easier to define the rodentterm PFCterm. Anatomists have used cytoarchitectural criteria to describe medial parts of the rodentterm frontal cortex as “cingulateterm cortex” in leading brain atlases ( Zilles, 1985 ; Paxinos and Watson, 1986 , 1998 ; SwansonpersName, 1992 ). However, a wide range of terms and acronyms have been used to describe these areas. For example, three different acronyms have been used to describe the prelimbicterm cortex: PL, PrL, and Cg3 ( Fig. 4 ). Similar to our comments above about the use of the word “prefrontalterm” across speciesterm and dual membership in the mPFCterm and ACCterm nomenclatures, it would be ideal if a single set of anatomicterm terms, and associated acronyms, were used to define these cortical regions. The most recent version of the commonly used Paxinos and Watson (2014) rat atlasterm stated “The nomenclatureterm of the rodentterm cingulateterm cortex and related areas has been a major problem for some decades” (page XV). Both mouse ( Paxinos and Franklin, 2012 ) and rat ( Paxinos and Watson, 2014 ) atlases were revised in collaborationterm with a leading cingulateterm cortex anatomist, Brent Vogt ( Vogt and Gabriel, 1993 ; Vogt et al., 2013 ; Vogt and Paxinos, 2014 ), to use slightly different anatomicterm boundaries along the medial wall cortex and label areas with Brodmann’s numbers, e.g., area 32term instead of PL and area 24 instead of Cg1 ( Fig. 4 ). The idea behind these changes, according to the authors, was to make the anatomy more in line with studies in primates (humans and monkeys). Unfortunately, a PubMed search reveals that the latest editions of the Paxinos atlases ( Paxinos and Franklin, 2012 ; Paxinos and Watson, 2014 ) have not been used in many recent studies, and labs still use terms from earlier versions of the Paxinos atlases (e.g., 279 publications with “prelimbicterm” and four papers with “area 32term” in the title of studies using rats or mice, based on a PubMed search in October, 2018).

Obfuscation, beclouding, and abstrusity

The three terms in the subheading of this section are synonyms for a lack of clarity in writing. At least they are single words. In recent years, two sets of hybridterm terms have emerged in the recent literature (since 2005) for denoting prefrontalterm regions in rodents. These terms combine the names of the cortical fields, prelimbicterm and infralimbicterm, with the term prefrontalterm cortex, e.g., “prelimbicterm prefrontalterm cortex” or “prelimbicterm medial prefrontalterm cortex.” Together with the various terms and acronyms described above for the Paxinos and SwansonpersName atlases, it is clear that there are too many terms being used to describe the prefrontalterm parts of the rodentterm cortex. We hope that these alternative terms can be eliminated from future publications, as they are redundant, do not add clarity, and make it more difficult to track new findings and incorporate them into the existing literature.

Why can’t we be friends?

The anatomicterm issues described above have made it difficult for researchers with expertise on different speciesterm to compare their data and, with only a few exceptions ( Narayanan et al., 2013 ; Parker et al., 2015 ; Hyman et al., 2017 ), rodentterm research groups have not collaborated with humanterm or monkey labs to understand medial PFCterm function. When such collaborations have been done, it has been difficult to agree on how to characterize the rodentterm areas in relation to medial areas of the humanterm mPFCterm. For example, in a study done through collaborationterm between rodentterm and humanterm researchers ( Narayanan et al., 2013 ), data from the prelimbicterm area were characterized as “medial frontal cortex” instead of the ACCterm or mPFCterm. Medial frontal cortex has not been commonly used to describe this area in the literature, with just 48 papers (<1% of all medial PFCterm papers) published using the term for rodentterm studies.

In summary, the question “What, if anything, is rodentterm prefrontalterm cortex?” is not easy to address due to major confusionterm about the anatomicterm terms that are commonly used to define the prefrontalterm cortex in rodents. These issues should be resolved as open-source brain atlases, such as the Allen Brain Atlasterm, are being developed and are becoming the dominant mode of reporting anatomicterm results for studies on the rodentterm (mouse) brain.

These acronyms are used to describe data from the dorsal and ventral parts of the mPFCterm. There may be a differing perception of the anatomicterm extent of rodentterm mPFCterm on a rostral to caudal basis. The area immediately dorsal and lateral to the classic mPFCterm/ACCterm area Cg1 has been called several names (medial precentral cortex, PrCm; medial agranular cortex, AGm, and secondary motor cortex, M2 or MOs). Some researchers have been careful to omit the “pre” from prefrontalterm when describing this area ( Siniscalchi et al., 2016 ; for review, see Barthas and Kwan, 2017 ). Suggestions for Future Studies on the Rodentterm PFCterm

We would like to close this perspective with some suggestions for improving the clarity, rigor, and reproducibilityterm of rodentterm PFCterm research. First, we recognize the difficulty in convincing an entire field to change their preferred terminologyterm for rodentterm PFCterm. To reduce confusionterm rodentterm researchers should at least clarify the precise anatomicterm location (e.g., when reporting data from neural recordings, use precise terms such as “prelimbicterm” instead of the more generic “ACCterm” or “mPFCterm” and/or estimated distances from landmarks such as bregma, e.g., 3.2 mm) of their data in publications on rodentterm prefrontalterm cortex, should always report specific coordinatesterm used in surgery (along with along with information on the strain, sex, and age of subjects), and should always provide images showing locations of experimental probes and viral fields for single animals and in group summaries. Second, hybridterm terms such as “infralimbicterm medial prefrontalterm” should not be used, and the community (especially journalterm editors and manuscript reviewers) should speak up when such unnecessary and confusion-generating terms appear in a preprint or manuscript that is under consideration for publication. Third, and most important, there is a serious need for community engagement to address the lack of clarity and resulting confusionterm generated by rodentterm PFCterm researchers not having adopted strict standards for anatomicterm details. This might best be addressed through a dedicated workshop to foster consensusterm on the use of precise anatomicterm terms for the rodentterm PFCterm, involving leading rodentterm PFCterm research labs, expert neuroanatomists, funding partners (NIH), foundations with a vested interest in rodentterm neuroanatomy (e.g., Allen Brain), and editors from leading neuroscience journals.

Bibliographies were created for each set of search terms using lists of PMIDs, imported into Zotero using the “Add items by identifier” tool, and saved in CSV (comma-separated values) formats.
