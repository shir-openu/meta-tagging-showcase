Deep learning enables accurate clusteringterm·key with batch effect removalterm·mth in single-cell RNA-seq analysis Abstract Single-cell RNA sequencingterm·mth (scRNA-seqterm·mth) can characterize cell types and states through unsupervised clusteringterm·key, but the ever increasing number of cells and batch effectterm·dom impose computational challenges. We present DESC, an unsupervised deep embeddingterm·key algorithm that clusters scRNA-seqterm·mth data by iteratively optimizing a clusteringterm·key objective function. Through iterative self-learning, DESC gradually removes batch effectsterm·dom, as long as technical differences across batches are smaller than true biological variationsterm·key. As a soft clusteringterm·key algorithm, cluster assignmentterm·key probabilities from DESC are biologically interpretable and can reveal both discrete and pseudotemporalterm·key structure of cells. Comprehensive evaluations show that DESC offers a proper balance of clusteringterm·key accuracy and stability, has a small footprint on memory, does not explicitly require batch information for batch effect removalterm·mth, and can utilize GPU when available. As the scale of single-cell studies continues to grow, we believe DESC will offer a valuable tool for biomedical researchers to disentangle complex cellular heterogeneity.

Subject terms: RNA sequencing, Machine learning, Software, Statistical methods Introduction A primary challenge in scRNA-seqterm·mth analysis is analyzing the ever increasing number of cells, which can be thousands to millions in large projects such as the Human Cell Atlas 1 . Identifying cell populations is challenging in large datasets because many existing scRNA-seqterm·mth clusteringterm·key methods cannot be scaled up to handle them. Large scRNA-seqterm·mth datasets often include cells that are easy to cluster, and it is desirable to learn expression patterns from these cells because they provide valuable information on cluster-specific gene expression signatures. These cells can further help improve clusteringterm·key of cells that are hard to cluster. As the number of cells grows in scRNA-seqterm·mth studies, another major challenge in analysis is batch effectterm·dom, which is systematic gene expression difference from one batch to another 2 . Batch effectterm·dom is inevitable in studies involving human tissues because the data are often generated at different times and the batches can confound biological variationsterm·key. Failure to remove batch effectterm·dom will complicate downstream analysis and lead to a false interpretation of results.

ScRNA-seqterm·mth clusteringterm·key and batch effect removalterm·mth are typically addressed through separate analyses. Commonly used approaches to remove batch effectterm·dom include canonical correlation analysisterm·mth (CCAterm·mth) 3 , mutual nearest neighborsterm·mth (MNNterm·mth) approach 4 , and the combination of MNNterm·mth and CCAterm·mth as implemented in Seurat 3.0 5 . After batch effect removalterm·mth, clusteringterm·key analysis is performed to identify cell clusters using methods such as Louvainterm·key’s method 6 , Infomap 7 , shared nearest neighbors 8 , or consensus clusteringterm·key with SC3 9 . However, some studies might deplete or enrich certain cell types, which can lead to cell-type-specific batch effectterm·dom 10 . Even when processed together, some cell types might be more vulnerable to batch effectterm·dom than others. Haghverdi et al. 4 found that consideration of cell-type-specific batch effectsterm·dom rather than a globally constant batch effectterm·dom for all cells leads to improved batch effect removalterm·mth.

Methods such as CCAterm·mth, MNNterm·mth, and Seurat 3.0 are based on pairwise analysis in which cells from two batches are considered at a time. For data with more than two batches, the first batch in order will be used as the reference batch to correct cells in the second batch, and the corrected values of the second batch are then added to the reference batch. This procedure is repeated until cells in all batches are corrected. As a pairwise procedure, the order in which batches are corrected will affect the final results. Scanorama 11 is also pairwise based, although it does not require a reference batch. Other methods such as scVI 12 and BERMUDA 13 explicitly incorporate batch information in analysis, thus can jointly analyze cells from all batches simultaneously.

Since clusteringterm·key and batch effect removalterm·mth are interrelated, an ideal approach for batch effect removalterm·mth should be performed jointly with clusteringterm·key. It is also desirable to have a method that can simultaneously include cells from all batches in the analysis. Here we present DESC, an unsupervised deep learning algorithm that iteratively learns cluster-specific gene expression representation and cluster assignmentsterm·key for scRNA-seqterm·mth analysis. DESC gradually removes batch effectterm·dom over iterations, as long as technical differences across batches are smaller than true biological variationsterm·key (e.g., between cell types). We compared DESC with many state-of-the-art methods for scRNA-seqterm·mth analysis, including CCAterm·mth, MNNterm·mth, Seurat 3.0, scVI, BERMUDA, and scanorama. Through comprehensive analyses of datasets with various degrees of complexities, we show that DESC is able to remove complex batch effectterm·dom, preserve biological variationsterm·key, and can reveal both discrete and pseudotemporalterm·key structure of cells.

Using a deep neural network, DESC initializes parameters obtained from an autoencoderterm·key and learns a nonlinear mapping function from the original scRNA-seqterm·mth data space to a low-dimensional feature space by iteratively optimizing a clusteringterm·key objective function. This iterative procedureterm·mth moves each cell to its nearest cluster centroid, balances biological and technical differences between clusters, and gradually reduces the influence of batch effectterm·dom. DESC also enables soft clusteringterm·key by assigning cluster-specific probabilities to each cell, which facilitates the clusteringterm·key of cells with high confidence. The resulting maximum probabilities for cluster assignmentterm·key also provide valuable information on cell transition for cells that originate from a genuinely continuous process.

Application to macaque retina data with complex batch effectterm·dom To evaluate the performance of DESC, we analyzed a scRNA-seqterm·mth dataset that includes 21,017 foveal and 9285 peripheral bipolar cells from retina in four macaques 10 . Since 80% of total cells in peripheral retina are rod photoreceptors, rods were depleted (anti-CD73) and retinal ganglion cells were enriched (CD90 + ) from peripheral samples before profiling. This dataset is complex in that there are three levels of batch effectterm·dom: animal level (four macaques), region level (fovea and periphery), and sample level (30 samples across macaques and regions). Peng et al. 10 found that CCAterm·mth and MNNterm·mth cannot completely remove batch effectterm·dom, collapsed distinct cell types, or have varying impact on different cell classes. For this complex dataset, DESC is effective in removing batch effectterm·dom and yields high clusteringterm·key accuracy (adjusted Rand index (ARI) 0.919–0.970) (Fig. 1b–e ). The cells are mixed well regardless whether sample, region, or animal was used to define batch in gene expression standardization, indicating the robustness of DESC. In contrast, CCAterm·mth, MNNterm·mth, Seurat 3.0, scVI, BERMUDA, and scanorama are all sensitive to batch definition, and the cells are separated by sample when region or animal was used to define batch in analyses (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Note 2 ).

In many studies, particularly those involving human tissues, batch effectterm·dom might be confounded with biological conditions. To reduce the risk of removing true biological variationsterm·key, it is desirable to have a method that is agnostic to batch. To this end, we further analyzed the data without using any batch information. Among all methods we evaluated, only scVI allows the analysis without the specification of batch in analysis. Figure 3a shows that DESC yields high clusteringterm·key accuracy (ARI 0.920) with the cells well mixed when batch information was not utilized in the analysis. However, cells from different samples are completely separated in scVI when batch information was not provided (Fig. 3b ), and the ARI dropped to 0.242 (Fig. 1e ), indicating its strong reliance on how batch is defined in the analysis.

Application to pancreatic islet data from four protocols Next, we tested whether DESC can remove batch effectterm·dom for data generated through different scRNA-seqterm·mth protocols. We combined four publicly available datasets on human pancreas generated using Fluidigm C1 14 , SMART-seq2 15 , CEL-seq 16 , and CEL-seq2 17 . Figure 4a shows that cells in these four datasets mixed well in DESC representation, and DESC yields the highest ARI value (0.945), which is much higher than MNNterm·mth (0.629), scVI (0.696), BERMUDA (0.484), and scanorama (0.537) (Fig. 4b ). Although Seurat 3.0 also has high ARI (0.896), its classification accuracies for α and β cells are only 92.1% and 88.1%, respectively, whereas the accuracies are 96.5% for α cells and 98.3% for β cells in DESC, respectively, (Supplementary Note 3 ). We also note that 2.32% of the α cells and 11.3% of the β cells were misclassified as a single cluster in Seurat 3.0, even though α and β cells are known to have distinct gene expression profiles (Supplementary Fig. 5 ). Seurat 3.0 relies on anchor cells between pairs of datasets, which are hypothesized to originate from the same biological state, to remove batch effectterm·dom. However, misidentification of anchors from different batches might have led to reduced accuracy for the α and β cells’ classification.

The main idea of DESC is to use “easy-to-cluster” cells to guide the neural network to learn cluster-specific gene expression features, while ignoring other unwanted noises such as batch effectterm·dom. Specifically, the auxiliary distribution P gives cells near the cluster centroid, i.e., easy-to-cluster cells, higher probabilities, in the process of optimizing the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between distributions P and Q . By doing this, DESC ignores information unrelated to cell clusteringterm·key by adjusting the network weight using the gradient descent algorithm, and learns cluster-specific information. Since DESC learns information on cell clusters from those “easy-to-cluster cells,” while ignoring other irrelevant information by constructing the auxiliary distribution P and optimizing the KL divergence between P and Q , thus as long as technical differences (e.g., between batches) are smaller than biological differences (e.g., between cell types), DESC can remove batch effectterm·dom successfully. A similar assumption has been made by MNNterm·mth 4 .

To illustrate that DESC gradually removes batch effectterm·dom over iterations, we generated t-SNE plots using the representation obtained from the bottleneck layer in DESC every two epochs for the combined pancreatic islet data. As shown in Fig. 4d, e , in the initial clusteringterm·key step (before iteration starts), there is significant batch effectterm·dom according to the t-SNE plot. However, during the training of the network, DESC removes batch effectterm·dom gradually over iterations. Finally, after the algorithm converges, DESC returns desired result with batch effectterm·dom removed in the clusteringterm·key.

Application to PBMC data with external stimulus To demonstrate that DESC preserves true biological variationsterm·key, we considered an even more complex situation in which technical batches were completely confounded with biological conditions. This is inevitable in disease studies involving human tissues where samples need to be processed immediately to maintain cell viability, resulting in the preparation of normal and diseased samples in different batches. For data generated in such complex settings, it is desirable to remove technical batch effectterm·dom while maintaining true biological variationsterm·key so that disease specific subpopulations can be identified. We analyzed a dataset that includes 24,679 human PBMCs from eight patients with lupus 18 (Supplementary Note 4 ). The cells were split into a control group and a matched group stimulated with interferon-beta (INF-β), which leads to a drastic but highly cell-type-specific response. This dataset is extremely challenging because removal of technical batch effectterm·dom is complicated by the presence of biological differences, both between cell types under the same condition and between different conditions for the same cell type.

Since batch completely confounds with biological condition, to reduce the risk of removing true biological variationsterm·key, we analyzed the data without using batch information in DESC. Figure 5a, b shows that DESC randomly mixed cells between the control and the stimulus conditions for all cell types except CD14 + monocytes. Results are similar when gene expression standardization is performed within batch (Supplementary Fig. 8a ). Differential expression (DE) analysis revealed a marked change in gene expression after INF-β stimulation for CD14 + monocytes (Fig. 5c ). The number of differentially expressed genes and the magnitude of DE, as measured by p value and fold change, are several orders more pronounced than the other cell types. This is consistent with previous studies showing that CD14 + monocytes have a greater change in gene expression than B cells, dendritic cells, and T cells after INF-β stimulation 19 , 20 . These results suggest that DESC is able to remove technical batch effectterm·dom and maintain true biological variationsterm·key induced by INF-β (Supplementary Figs. 9 – 13 ). Figure 5d shows the KL divergences calculated using all cells and using non-CD14 + monocytes only. The KL divergence here was used to measure the degree of batch effect removalterm·mth (see “Methods” for evaluation metric for batch effect removalterm·mth). The decreased KL divergence of DESC when CD14 + monocytes were eliminated indicates that technical batch effectterm·dom was effectively removed in the absence of CD14 + monocytes. The KL divergences of all other methods are larger than DESC when CD14 + monocytes were eliminated, indicating that they might be less effective in removing technical batch effectterm·dom than DESC.

DESC embedding preserves pseudotemporalterm·key structure DESC was designed to identify discrete cell clusters, but we also evaluated its performance for cells that originate from a genuinely continuous process. We analyzed a scRNA-seqterm·mth dataset generated from mouse bone marrow myeloid progenitor cells 21 . Since this dataset does not have batch effectterm·dom, we compared DESC with scVI only, which is also deep learning based. DESC embedding removes batch effectterm·dom for monocytes We further evaluated whether DESC is able to remove batch effectterm·dom for cells that originate from a continuous process. We analyzed a scRNA-seqterm·mth dataset generated from monocytes derived from human peripheral blood mononuclear cells by Ficoll separation followed by CD14- and CD16-positive cell selection. This dataset includes 10,878 monocytes collected from a single healthy subject. The cells were processed in three batches from blood drawn on three different days, sequentially 77 and 33 days apart. Although based on surface markers, monocytes can be classified as classical (CD14 ++ /CD16 − ), intermediate (CD14 ++ /CD16 + ), and nonclassical patrolling (CD14 − /CD16 ++ ) subpopulations, clusteringterm·key analysis based on scRNA-seqterm·mth data indicates that these cells show continuous characteristics, making it difficult to identify discrete cell clusters.

Therefore, we used the low-dimensional representation obtained from DESC as input for Monocle3 22 , to reconstruct the trajectory in this dataset. Comparison of running time and memory usage To verify DESC is scalable for large dataset, we analyzed a 1.3 million mouse brain cell dataset generated by 10X Genomics. Discussion In summary, we have presented a deep learning based algorithm that clusters scRNA-seqterm·mth data by iteratively optimizing a clusteringterm·key objective function with a self-training target distribution. Through iterative learning, DESC removes complex batch effectterm·dom and maintains true biological variationsterm·key. As a soft clusteringterm·key based algorithm, the cluster assignmentterm·key probabilities from DESC are biologically interpretable and can reveal both discrete and pseudotemporalterm·key structure of cells. We have performed comprehensive evaluations and compared with many state-of-the-art methods for scRNA-seqterm·mth analysis. Figure 10 shows a summary of the performance of each evaluated method. Overall, DESC achieved high clusteringterm·key accuracy across all datasets and showed stable results under a wide range of scenarios.

ScRNA-seqterm·mth has emerged as a revolutionary tool in biomedical research. In the last few years, studies employing scRNA-seqterm·mth technique have grown exponentially. Despite its promises, many computational challenges must be overcome before we can fully reap the benefit of scRNA-seqterm·mth. A critical first step in many scRNA-seqterm·mth studies is to cluster cells into biologically meaningful entities. Effective removal of technical batch effectterm·dom is important to ensure the validity and interpretability of clusteringterm·key results. Through comprehensive evaluations with many datasets, we have shown that DESC offers a proper balance of clusteringterm·key stability and accuracy, small footprint on memory usage, does not explicitly require batch definition for batch effect removalterm·mth, and can speed up computation by utilizing GPU when available. As the scale of single-cell studies continues to grow, we believe that DESC will be a valuable tool for biomedical researchers to better disentangle complex cellular heterogeneity, and will facilitate the translation of basic research findings into clinical studies of human disease.

Methods The DESC algorithm Analysis of scRNA-seqterm·mth data often involves clusteringterm·key of cells into different clusters and selection of highly variable genes for cell clusteringterm·key. As these are closely related, it is desirable to use a data driven approach to cluster cells and select genes simultaneously. This problem shares similarity with pattern recognition, in which clear gains have resulted from joint consideration of the classification and feature selection problems by deep learning. However, for scRNA-seqterm·mth data, a challenge is that we cannot train deep neural network with labeled data as cell-type labels are typically unknown. To solve this problem, we take inspiration from recent work on unsupervised deep embeddingterm·key for clusteringterm·key analysis 23 , in which we iteratively refine clusters with an auxiliary target distribution derived from the current soft cluster assignmentterm·key. This self-learning process gradually improves clusteringterm·key as well as feature representation. As shown by our comprehensive evaluations across a wide range of datasets, this procedure also gradually removes batch effectterm·dom over iterations.

Overview of DESC : The DESC procedure starts with parameter initialization, in which a stacked autoencoderterm·key is used for pretraining and learning low-dimensional representation of the input gene expression matrix. The corresponding encoder is then added to the iterative clusteringterm·key neural network. The cluster centers are initialized by the Louvainterm·key’s clusteringterm·key algorithm 6 , which aims to optimize modularity for community detection. This clusteringterm·key returns data in a feature space that allows us to obtain centroids in the initial stage of the iterative clusteringterm·key. Below, we describe each component of the DESC procedure in detail.

Parameter initialization by stacked autoencoderterm·key : Let X ∈ R n × p be the gene expression matrix obtained from a scRNA-seqterm·mth experiment, in which rows correspond to cells and columns correspond to genes. Due to sparsity and high dimensionality of scRNA-seqterm·mth data, to perform clusteringterm·key, it is necessary to transform the data from high dimensional space R p to a lower dimensional space R d in which d ≪ p . Traditional dimension-reduction techniques, such as principal component analysis, operate on a shallow linear embedded space, and thus have limited ability to represent the data. To better represent the data, we perform feature transformation by a stacked autoencoderterm·key, which have been shown to produce well-separated representations on real datasets.

The stacked autoencoderterm·key network is initialized layer by layer with each layer being an autoencoderterm·key trained to reconstruct the previous layer’s output. After layer-wise training, all encoder layers are concatenated, followed by all decoder layers, in reverse layer-wise training order. The resulting autoencoderterm·key is then fine-tuned to minimize reconstruction loss. The final result is a multilayer autoencoderterm·key with a bottleneck layer in the middle. After fine tuning, the decoder layers are discarded, and the encoder layers are used as the initial mapping between the original data space and the dimension-reduced feature space, as shown in Fig. 1a .

Since the number of true clusters for a scRNA-seqterm·mth dataset is typically unknown, we apply the Louvainterm·key’s method, a graph-based method that has been shown to excel over other clusteringterm·key methods, on the feature space Z obtained from the bottleneck layer. This analysis returns the number of clusters, denoted by K , and the corresponding cluster centroids { μ j : j = 1 , … , K } , which will be used as the initial clusteringterm·key for DESC.

Iterative clusteringterm·key : After cluster initialization, we improve the clusteringterm·key using an unsupervised algorithm that alternates between two steps until convergence. In the first step, we compute a soft assignment of each cell between the embedded points and the cluster centroids. Following van der Maaten and Hinton 24 , we use the Student’s t distribution as a kernel to measure the similarity between embedded point z i for cell i and centroid μ j for cluster j where z i = f W x i ∈ Z corresponds to x i ∈ X after embedding, and α is the degree of freedom of the Student’s t distribution. We also evaluated the performance of Gaussian distribution as a kernel, but found it to be less robust than the t distribution (Supplementary Note 3 ).

In the second step, we refine the clusters by learning from cells with high confidence cluster assignmentsterm·key with the help of an auxiliary target distribution. Specifically, we define the objective function as a KL divergence loss between the soft cell assignments q i and the auxiliary distribution p i for cell i as where the auxiliary distribution P is defined as The encoder is fine-tuned by minimizing L in Eq. Given that the target distribution P is defined by Q , minimizing L implies a form of self-training. Also, p i j gives the probability of cell i that belongs to cluster j , and this probability can be used to measure the confidence of cluster assignmentterm·key for each cell. Because α is insensitive to the clusteringterm·key result, we let α = 1 for all datasets by default.

Optimization of the KL divergence loss : We jointly optimize the cluster centers { μ j : j = 1 , … , K } and the deep neural network parameters using stochastic gradient descent. The gradients of L with respect to feature space embedding of each data point z i and each cluster center μ j are These gradients are then passed down to the deep neural network and used in standard backpropagation to compute the deep neural network’s parameter gradient. We use Keras to train our model. During each iteration, i.e., when loss is not decreasing or the epoch number threshold is reached, we update the auxiliary distribution P , and optimize cluster centers and encoder parameters with the new P . This iterative procedureterm·mth stops when the proportion of cells that changes cluster assignmentterm·key between two consecutive steps is less than tol . Specifically, tol is calculated as tol = # Y curr ≠ Y prev n , where Y curr is the cluster id obtained by the maximum cluster assignmentterm·key probability in the current step, Y prev is the corresponding cluster id in the previous step, n is the total number of cells, and # Y curr ≠ Y prev is the number of cells in which Y curr does not agree with Y prev . We let tol = 0.005 by default.

Architecture of the deep neural network in DESC : Depending on the number of cells in the dataset, we suggest different numbers of hidden layers and different numbers of nodes in the encoder. Supplementary Table 2 gives the default numbers of hidden layers and nodes in DESC.

DESC allows users to specify their own numbers of hidden layers and nodes. We recommend using more hidden layers and more nodes per layer for datasets with more cells so that the complexity of the data can be captured by the deep neural network. We use ReLU as the activation function except for the bottleneck layer and last decoder layer, in which tanh is used as the activation function. The reason why we use tanh is that we must guarantee the values in feature representation and output of decoder range from negative to positive. The default hyperparameters for the autoencoderterm·key are listed in Supplementary Table 3 .

Data normalization and gene selection The normalization involves two steps. In the first step, cell level normalization is performed, in which the UMI count for each gene in each cell is divided by the total number of UMIs in the cell, multiplied by 10,000, and then transformed to a natural log scale. In the second step, gene level normalization is performed in which the cell level normalized values for each gene are standardized by subtracting the mean across all cells and divided by the standard deviation across all cells for the given gene. When batch information is provided, gene expression standardization is performed across cells in each batch separately. Highly variable genes are selected using the filter_genes_dispersion function from the Scanpy package 25 ( https://github.com/theislab/scanpy ).

Evaluation metric for clusteringterm·key For published datasets in which the reference cell-type labels are known, we use ARI to compare the performance of different clusteringterm·key algorithms. Larger values of ARI indicate higher accuracy in clusteringterm·key. The ARI can be used to calculate similarity between the clusteringterm·key labels obtained from a clusteringterm·key algorithm and the reference cluster labels. Given a set of n cells and two sets of clusteringterm·key labels of these cells, the overlap between the two sets of clusteringterm·key labels can be summarized in a contingency table, in which each entry denotes the number of cells in common between the two sets of clusteringterm·key labels. Specifically, the ARI is calculated as where n j j ′ is the number of cells assigned to cluster j based on the reference cluster labels, and cluster j ′ based on clusteringterm·key labels obtained from a clusteringterm·key algorithm, a j is the number of cells assigned to cluster j in the reference set, and b j ′ is the number of cells assigned to cluster j ′ by the clusteringterm·key algorithm.

Evaluation metric for batch effect removalterm·mth We use KL divergence to evaluate the performance of various single-cell clusteringterm·key algorithms for batch effect removalterm·mth, i.e., how randomly are cells from different batches mixed together within each cluster. The KL divergence of batch mixing for B different batches is calculated as where q b is the proportion of cells from batch b among all cells, and p b is the proportion of cells from batch b in a given region based on results from a clusteringterm·key algorithm, with ∑ b = 1 B q b = 1 and ∑ b = 1 B p b = 1 . We calculate the KL divergence of batch mixing by using regional mixing KL divergence defined above using 100 randomly chosen cells from all batches. The regional proportion of cells from each batch is calculated based on the set of K nearest neighboring cells from each randomly chosen cell ( K can be set differently according the number of batches, we suggest K = 5 × the number of batches). The final KL divergence is then calculated as the average of the regional KL divergence. We repeated this procedure for 200 iterations with different randomly chosen cells to generate box plots of the final KL divergence. Smaller final KL divergence indicates better batch mixing, i.e., more effective in batch effect removalterm·mth.
