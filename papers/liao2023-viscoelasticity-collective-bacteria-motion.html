Introduction Bacteria are the most abundant species on Earth. They compose human and animal microbiota and are sources of many infectious diseases ( 1 ). Suspension of motile bacteriaterm·dom is an important class of active matterterm·dom: nonequilibrium systems transducing energy from the environment into mechanical motion. Concentrated bacterial suspensions often exhibit large-scale turbulent-like motion (so-called bacterial turbulence) ( 2 , 3 ). While significant knowledge is accumulated on the collective dynamics of bacterial suspensions in Newtonian liquids, complex fluids are mostly “terra incognita” ( 4 ).

Bacterial habitats are not limited to Newtonian fluids such as water. Bacteria thrive in non-Newtonian environments ( 4 ) exemplified by lyotropic liquid crystals ( 5 , 6 ), mucusterm·key ( 7 , 8 ), DNA solutions ( 9 , 10 ), blood ( 11 ), saliva ( 12 ), biofilm matrices ( 13 ), or mammalian extracellular matrices ( 14 ). Unlike the simple viscous response in a Newtonian liquid, the non-Newtonian rheology of biological viscoelastic fluid can drastically alter the bacterial collective motionterm·key ( 15 , 16 ). However, there is no consensus even on the level of individual motility of microorganisms in viscoelastic fluids. For example, some studies predicted that viscoelasticityterm·key hinders self-propulsion ( 17 , 18 ). On the contrary, other investigations suggested that the microswimmers propel faster or slower in the viscoelastic fluid but slower in a shear-thinning fluid ( 19 , 20 ). Alternatively, computational modeling ( 21 ) predicted increased bacterial swimming speed up to 60% in polymer solutions due to a nonuniform distribution of polymer molecules around a bacterium. Also, computational studies revealed that viscoelasiticity significantly affect collective motionterm·key of bacteria-like rod-shaped swimmers (pushers) but has a small effect on a suspension of algae-like swimmers (pullers) ( 15 ). Thus, examination of collective and individual dynamics of bacteria in viscoelastic fluids may produce new insights into active matterterm·dom in complex environments.

Among biological fluids, mucusterm·key, a gel-like viscoelastic substance lining the exposed organism tract, is a crucial habit of bacterial communities. The dominant glycan protein, mucin, forms a mesh network and hence endows mucusterm·key elasticityterm·dom ( 22 ). Mucusterm·key demonstrates non-Newtonian dynamics: shear-thinning, stress relaxation, yielding behavior ( 23 ), and even some liquid crystallinity ( 7 , 24 ). Most studies focus on the penetration of individual bacteria or sperm across mucusterm·key. The individual motility of bacteria such as Helibactor pylori , Escherichia coli , and Bacillus subtilisterm·key in mucusterm·key has been studied to better understand both the defensive mechanism of mucusterm·key and the invasion strategy of bacteria ( 7 , 8 , 25 ). The motility of individual sperm in cervical mucusterm·key has also been investigated to understand the mechanisms of infertility ( 26 ). However, the collective states of concentrated bacteria in mucusterm·key, which is relevant in the context of emerging antibiotic resistance ( 27 , 28 ) and colonization of mucosal surfaces in intestinal infection ( 29 ), is mostly unexplored.

Here, we examine the collective motionterm·key of concentrated bacteria Bacillus subtilisterm·key in synthetic porcine gastric mucusterm·key and natural cow cervical mucusterm·key. We contrasted the collective behavior in viscoelastic mucusterm·key with essentially Newtonian polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP360term·key) polymer solution at a wide range of concentrations. We have found that the velocity correlation lengthterm·mth of bacterial collective locomotion increases monotonously with the increase of mucin concentration in both synthetic mucusterm·key and natural mucusterm·key. The elevation of mucin concentration increases both storage and loss modules which is consistent with a previous observation ( 23 ). On the contrary, the correlation lengthterm·mth of bacterial motion in the PVP360term·key solution remained practically unchanged regardless of PVP360term·key concentration, consistent with the correlation lengthterm·mth predictions for Newtonian fluid ( 30 ). The study implies that the spatial correlation of bacterial active matterterm·dom is primarily affected by fluid elasticityterm·dom rather than viscosityterm·dom. The experimental results are supported by the computational analysis of phenomenological model for bacterial collective motionterm·key in viscoelastic fluids. The result sheds light on manipulating active matterterm·dom by modifying the elasticityterm·dom of the suspending fluid.

Discussions Our experimental study revealed a distinct effect of viscoelasticityterm·key: while the medium viscosityterm·dom results in the overall slowdown of collective motionterm·key, the elasticityterm·dom increases the correlation lengthterm·mth of collective motionterm·key. By examining individual bacterial dynamics, one may come to the conclusion that an increase in the correlation lengthterm·mth is an outcome of the straightening of bacterial trajectories ( 33 , 37 ). However, this issue is more subtle, and the curvature reduction of individual bacteria trajectories does not necessarily translates into the increase of the collective motionterm·key scale, as shown by our study, see Fig. 1 C–E. Straightening of the bacterial trajectories could be an interplay of multiple factors: suppression of run-and-tumble behavior, bundling of multiple flagella in a single tight bundle due to polymer interaction, effect of the lift force near solid surface, or alignment of bacterial bodies with the polymer network. In our situation, the rise in the correlation lengthterm·mth is due to the stretching and realigning of mucusterm·key elastic polymer network by bacteria, a phenomenon similar to that in liquid crystals ( 5 , 6 ). However, we do not anticipate the onset of long-range liquid crystalline order for the mucin concentrations used in our experimentsterm·mth. Here, the competition between the mucusterm·key network’s elastic deformations and the bacteria’s hydrodynamic torques increases spatial correlation lengthterm·mth L .

The collective bacterial flow has a scale of 80–250 μ m. It exceeds the mucin network’s mesh size (2 μ m). The recoil during the mesh network distortion can effectively slow the bacterial flow. Besides increasing viscosityterm·dom, this slowdown may explain the increased temporal correlation in mucin solution with increased concentrations. The Deborah numberterm·dom (Determ·dom) describes the effect of viscoelastic relaxation of flow patterns. It is defined as Determ·dom = λ a / λ b , where λ a is the fluid relaxation time and λ b is the observation time of fluid flow. Depending on the concentration, the characteristic relaxation time of mucin solution is around λ a ∼ 1 − 10 s. The observation time of fluid flow λ b associated with the collective bacterial flow is l c / U c , where l c is the length of a bacterium and U c is the bacterial flow speed. Thus, λ b is ∼0.7–3 s and the Deborah numberterm·dom Determ·dom ∼ 0.6 − 14 . Therefore, flow elasticityterm·dom can be essential in the onset of collective motionterm·key.

Conclusion We have shown that the viscoelasticityterm·key of suspending media profoundly affects the collective behavior of bacterial active matterterm·dom. Namely, the elasticityterm·dom of the solution increases spatial correlation lengthterm·mth, whereas the viscosityterm·dom mainly results in motion slowing down and, in turn, increasing the correlation time. This statement is supported by the correlation measurements in the viscoelastic mucusterm·key solution and contrasted with the study of essentially Newtonian PVP360term·key polymer suspension. We anticipate an onset of a global vortex when the correlation lengthterm·mth becomes of the order of system size, similar to ( 10 , 47 ). However, whether this global vortex exhibits spontaneous direction reversal, as in Ref. ( 10 ) could depend on subtle properties of the viscoelastic medium, e.g. the ratio between the relaxation time of the medium and the correlation time of the bacterial locomotion, nonlinearity of the stress–strain relation, etc.

The increased correlation lengthterm·mth L in mucusterm·key with elasticityterm·dom E is the consequence of the reorientation of the mucin network by collectively swimming bacteria. The elastic deformations of mucin network “flattens” small-scale bacterial collective motionterm·key and lead to the increased size of bacterial vortices and streams. A similar effect occurs in liquid crystals where the correlation lengthterm·mth increases with the elastic constant ( 5 , 6 ). A corresponding model for mucusterm·key requires a set of highly nonlinear equations coupling the local orientation and polymer extension and therefore is challenging. Here, we adopted a simplified framework where the alignment effect described by the polymer’s elastic response factor ϕ ( τ ) q in Eq. 5 . In this approach, the dependence results from pure physical interaction between swimmers and viscoelastic medium; therefore, neither bacteria- nor media-specific. We anticipate that our results provide insights into a broad class of active matterterm·dom composed of viscoelastic suspending medium (liquid crystals, polymers, biological fluids) and self-propelled agents, both biological (bacteria) and synthetic. The synthetic realizations could include Janus microswimmers energized by chemical reactions, light, or ultrasound ( 48 ). Our results also provide insights into bacteria behavior on mucosal surfaces with possible implications for human and animal health. The enhancement of large-scale collective motionterm·key of bacteria by mucusterm·key viscoelasticityterm·key may result in accelerated bacteria penetration and, in turn, faster bacteria colonization of internal tissues.
