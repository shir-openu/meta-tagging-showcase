phenomenoncorpus-based typology— “The core idea of corpus-based typology is to take languages as populations of utterances and to systematically investigate text production across languages in this sense.”
claim-typeexistence— “Corpus-based studies have become increasingly common in linguistic typology over recent years, amounting to the emergence of a new field that we call corpus-based typology.”
method — evidence— “In this article we describe some of the key insights that systematic investigations of language usage—as undertaken in corpus linguistics—contribute to our understanding of diverse and universal aspects of the near 7,000 human languages in the world.”
mechanismConventionalised orders minimise total dependency length, and because a dependent tree then sits closer to the higher head it hangs from, harmonic (cross-domain aligned) word order falls out as a by-product.— “The general conclusion is that DLM as observable in corpora provides an overall explanation for harmonic word order correlations observed in GBT, because head dependency trees occur overall closer to the higher-level heads of which they are dependents.”
mechanismKeeping a head adjacent to the head of its dependent lets the hearer recognise higher-order constituency sooner, which is why languages converge on harmonic orders.— “propose that head adjacency as sketched above is preferable for language processing because it allows a recipient to recognize the constituency of higher-order phrases more immediately”
mechanismNouns anchor referring expressions and so open a wider set of lexical and referential choices, making them harder to plan, which shows up as slower speech and pausing just before them.— “They explain this finding in terms of greater planning difficulties associated with nouns, which serve as the core of referring expressions and whose production thus entails a larger range of lexical and referential choices (Arnold 2008) than is common with verbs.”
builds-on Haig et al. (2011)— “We term the joint advances of corpus-based studies in linguistic typology corpus-based typology (CBT) (Haig et al. 2011) and contrast it with grammar-based typology (GBT).3”
contradicts Du Bois (1987) discourse ergativity— “Investigating a larger range of spoken-language original text corpora from Multi-CAST, Haig & Schnell (2016) and Schnell et al. (2021) do not find support for discourse ergativity”

Individual language corpora are thus samples of utterance populations, each associated with different linguistic conventions.”

findingCorpus-based typology largely reproduces the classifications of grammar-based typology, but adds finer-grained, quantitatively explicit detail and robustness.— “Thus, CBT findings largely confirm GBT findings but lend more empirical detail and robustness to observations of regularities.”
findingIn the authors' own comparison, long noun phrases are far commoner in the written Universal Dependencies corpora (about 36% of NPs at least four dependencies long) than in the spoken Multi-CAST corpora (about 12%).— “with approximately 36% of NPs [standard deviation (SD) = 15%] being l ≥ 4 dependencies in length in the former and only approximately 12% (SD = 9%) in the latter”
findingBecause spoken texts contain too few phrases long enough for reordering to matter, dependency length minimisation cannot be what drives word order variation in speech.— “these initial observations suggest that spoken texts fail to exhibit the prerequisite for DLM effects, namely phrases/local dependency trees of sufficient length”
findingAcross 12 spoken corpora, subjects inside a same-subject reference chain are about 15-20% likelier to be realised as zero than subjects elsewhere, regardless of the language's overall zero rate.— “chain-medial subjects are approximately 15–20% more likely to be zero than other subjects, irrespective of how common zero subjects are in the language…
findingNearly every language with a zero-subject rate above 70% has fully grammaticalised, transparent subject agreement, the exceptions being Mandarin and Jinghpaw.
findingDu Bois's discourse-ergativity hypothesis is not supported by spoken original-text corpora: intransitive subjects (S), not transitive objects (P), show the lowest rate of new-referent and full-NP expression.
findingSpeakers slow their articulation and pause more often before nouns than before verbs, a tendency found across nine typologically diverse spoken corpora.
