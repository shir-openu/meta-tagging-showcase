mechanismSupervised learning first certifies that a pattern exists at all (better-than-chance prediction), then attribution techniques localise which input features carry it, so the mathematician's search space is narrowed before any conjecture is formed— “The framework helps guide the intuition of mathematicians in two ways: by verifying the hypothesized existence of structure/patterns in mathematical objects through the use of supervised machine learning; and by helping in the understanding of these patterns through the use of attribution techniques.”
mechanismGradient saliency assigns importance by measuring how much the learned function's prediction shifts under small perturbations of each input component— “These techniques attribute importance to the elements of X ( z ), by calculating how much f ˆ changes in predictions of Y (z) given small changes in X ( z ).”
mechanismaccuracy above chance is used as evidence that a relationship EXISTS, before anyone knows what it is - the model detects presence, not content— “If f ˆ is more accurate than would be expected by chance, it indicates that there may be such a relationship to explore.”
mechanismattribution turns an opaque learned function into something a person can reason about, by showing which inputs it is sensitive to— “Attribution techniques can be used to understand which aspects of f ˆ are relevant for predictions of Y ( z ).”

findingThe proposed machine-learning framework produced genuinely new mathematics in two unrelated fields: a knot-theoretic connection between algebraic and geometric invariants, and a candidate resolution of a long-open conjecture in representation theory.
findingA feed-forward network predicted knot signature from geometric invariants far better than chance, which was taken as evidence that a real relationship exists.
findingAttribution pointed to the cusp shape, and the derived 'natural slope' turned out to capture linearly all the information about the signature that the full geometric model had extracted.
findingThe resulting theorem is among the first to link algebraic and geometric knot invariants and has further consequences for Dehn surgeries and surface genus.
findingThe first conjectured inequality, though supported by large sampled datasets, was false: specially constructed braids furnished counterexamples and forced a corrected theorem.
findingSalient-subgraph analysis in the representation-theory case led to the discovery that a Bruhat interval decomposes into a hypercube plus a graph isomorphic to an interval in the smaller symmetric group.
findingThis decomposition yields a proved theorem: every Bruhat interval admits a canonical hypercube decomposition from which the Kazhdan-Lusztig polynomial can be computed directly.
findingComputational testing suggested the stronger claim that any hypercube decomposition, not just the canonical one, recovers the correct KL polynomial.
findingEdge labels absent from the input were nonetheless reflected in the network's saliency pattern, since extremal reflections were overrepresented in salient subgraphs.
findingThe framework produced two genuinely new mathematical results - a connection between algebraic and geometric invariants of knots, and a candidate algorithm for the combinatorial invariance conjecture.
findingEarlier conjecture-generating systems split into two failure modes: useful but not generalisable, or general but not yet useful.
findingThe computational cost is small - hours on a single GPU - so the constraint is mathematical judgement, not compute.
findingAI had already been used in mathematics for counterexamples, calculation and symbolic solutions; what was missing was help with discovery itself.
