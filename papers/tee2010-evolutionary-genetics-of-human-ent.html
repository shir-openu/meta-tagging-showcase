findingThe common ancestor of human enterovirus 71 is dated to about 1941, after which the virus split into genogroups B, C and the now-extinct genogroup A. — “We estimated that the common ancestor of human EV-71 likely emerged around 1941 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1929 to 1952), subsequently diverging into three genogroups: B, C, and the now extinct genogroup A.”
findingEach subgenogroup of genogroups B and C circulated undetected in humans for up to five years before it caused a large hand, foot and mouth disease outbreak. — “Genealogical analysis revealed that diverse lineages of genogroup B and C (subgenogroups B1 to B5 and C1 to C5) have each circulated cryptically in the human population for up to 5 years before causing large HFMD outbreaks, indicating the quiescent persistence of EV-71 in human populations.”
findingThe VP1 gene evolves under strong purifying selection, with mean dN/dS between 0.029 and 0.075 in every subgenogroup except B3. — “Maximum-likelihood analyses showed that the evolution of EV-71 is driven by strong purifying selection, with estimates of mean dN / dS values ranging from 0.029 to 0.075 for all subgenogroups (except B3, for which the mean dN / dS is significantly higher) (Table 2 ).”
findingIn subgenogroup C1 the trunk lineages have a lower dN/dS (0.030) than the terminal lineages (0.064), the opposite of the pattern expected under antigenic-drift-style positive selection along the trunk. — “Lineage-specific codon-based analyses of subgenogroup C1 VP1 genes revealed a significantly lower dN / dS ratio for the trunk lineages (0.030 ± 0.009) than for the terminal lineages (0.064 ± 0.008) ( P = 0.055).”
findingNo homologous recombination was detectable in the capsid-encoding VP1 region, so the phylogenetic and dating analyses are not confounded by recombination. — “Additionally, rigorous recombination analysis showed a lack of recombination in the capsid-encoding VP1 region, consistent with previous analyses on human enterovirus species ( 73 ).”
