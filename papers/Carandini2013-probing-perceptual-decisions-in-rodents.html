findingGo/no-go designs, the most common choice in rodent work, confound perceptual ability with the animal's motivation and response criterion. — “A key issue with go/no-go tasks is that they are highly vulnerable to changes in the animal's motivation and criterion.”
findingRequiring a response on every trial removes the confound of a changing willingness to respond, though not the confound of a changing criterion. — “Unlike a go/no-go task, however, this design is immune to changes in a subject's willingness to respond, because the subject must report a decision on every trial.”
findingPresenting two stimuli and asking which carries the attribute is the only design immune to an unknown, drifting decision criterion, because the subject cannot be biased toward presence or absence. — “in a task with two stimulus presentations subjects cannot have a bias for the presence or absence of a stimulus attribute: in each choice they must assign presence to a location and absence to the other.”
findingTesting at only one stimulus strength, as rodent studies commonly do, makes a change in sensitivity indistinguishable from a change in bias or in lapse rate, so causal manipulations cannot be interpreted. — “Obtaining behavioral data at only a single intensity (as is often done in rodent studies) would not allow one to distinguish the effects of sensitivity, bias, and lapse rate.”
findingThe widespread assumption that mice are nocturnal is an artefact of unlimited laboratory food, and their eyes lack the anatomical signature of nocturnal animals. — “Mice, however, are reliably nocturnal only if food is unlimited, as is typical in the laboratory; when food is scarcer, they sleep part of the night to lower body temperature and conserve energy”
findingRodent eye anatomy provides independent evidence against the nocturnality assumption. — “Accordingly, the eyes of rats and mice lack the reflective tapetum that would be expected in nocturnal animals.”
