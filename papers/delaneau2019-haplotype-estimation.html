On the one hand, cost reduction of single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) arrays allow genotyping hundreds of thousands of individuals resulting in data sets such as the UK Biobankterm·key (UKB), which regroup genotype data for half a million samples 2 . Data sets consisting of millions of samples are now being generated by projects such as the Million Veteran Program 8 or by commercial companies such as 23andMe, which has now genotyped more than 5 million customers so far. In this context, it is unclear if the scaling with sample sizeterm·var offered by these methods is able to conveniently process genotype data on that scale. On the other hand, high-throughput sequencing now enables exhaustive screening of millions of genetic variants within tens of thousands of individuals such as in the Haplotypeterm·key Reference Consortium data set 9 . Latter developments in sequencing have also witnessed the introduction of long readsterm·key technologies such as PacBio 10 or Oxford Nanopore 11 . By covering multiple nearby heterozygous variants in an individual, the long readsterm·key generated by these sequencing technologies allow resolving haplotypesterm·key across hundreds of kilobases. While these approaches are extremely efficient to resolve the phase between nearby variants, they do not allow full haplotypeterm·key resolution across entire chromosome. To assemble together these blocks of phased variants, usually called phase setsterm·key, two types of approaches are explored: experimental solutions based either on Hi-C 15 or strand-seq 16 or computational solutions requiring population level data 17 . In this paper, we describe and benchmark a method for haplotypeterm·key estimation, SHAPEIT4term·mth, which proposes efficient solutions to these two challenges. Specifically, it allows processing either SNP array or sequencing data accurately with running times that are sub-linear with sample sizeterm·var, therefore making it well suited for very large-scale data sets. In addition, it also facilitates the integration of additional phasingterm·key information such as reference haplotypesterm·key, long sequencing reads, and sets of pre-phased variants altogether to boost the quality of the resulting haplotypesterm·key. To achieve this, the method builds on three main components: (i) the Li and Stephens modelterm·mth (LSMterm·mth) 18 to capture long-range haplotypeterm·key sharing between individuals, (ii) the Positional Burrows–Wheeler Transformterm·mth (PBWTterm·mth) 19 to speed up the computations involved in the LSMterm·mth and (iii) the compact representation of the solution space built in previous versions of SHAPEIT 20 , 21 , which allows efficient haplotypeterm·key sampling and easy integration of additional phasingterm·key information 17 , 22 . To demonstrate its performance, we benchmark it on two gold standard data sets: the UKB 2 to evaluate its ability to process large-scale SNP array data sets and on the Genome In A Bottle (GIAB) 23 to assess its ability to leverage long sequencing read information.

This provides a computationally efficient alternative to the previous approach based on Hamming distanceterm·mth 20 , 24 . A PBWTterm·mth of haplotypesterm·key is a data structureterm·mth in which any two haplotypesterm·key sharing a long prefix (i.e., match) at a given position are sorted next to each other at that position. SHAPEIT4term·mth takes advantage of this by maintaining a PBWTterm·mth of all the haplotypeterm·key estimates so that long matches between haplotypesterm·key can be identified in constant time. In practice, SHAPEIT4term·mth works within overlapping genomic regions (of 2 Mb by default) and proceeds as follows to update the phase of an individual in a given region: (i) it interrogates the PBWTterm·mth arrays every eight variants to get the Pterm·mth haplotypesterm·key that share the longest prefixes with the current haplotypeterm·key estimates at that position, (ii) it collapses the haplotypesterm·key identified across the entire region into a list of K distinct haplotypesterm·key, and (iii) it runs the LSMterm·mth conditioning on the K haplotypesterm·key (Fig. 1a, b ). In this approach, Pterm·mth is the main parameter controlling the trade-off between speed and accuracyterm·dom and gives a model in which K varies and adapts to the data and region being processed as opposed to previous methods in which K is usually fixed 20 , 24 . Adapting the method to leverage reference panelsterm·key of haplotypesterm·key is straightforward: the PBWTterm·mth matching procedure was just extended to also consider the reference haplotypesterm·key when selecting conditioning haplotypesterm·key. Discussion We present here a method for statistical haplotypeterm·key estimation, SHAPEIT4term·mth, that substantially improves upon existing methods in terms of flexibility and computational efficiency. One of the key improvements resides in its ability to quickly select a variable number of informative haplotypesterm·key to condition on through a PBWT-based approach. The resulting method exhibits sub-linear running times with sample sizeterm·var and provides highly accurate haplotypeterm·key estimates for large-scale data sets. We demonstrated this on the largest genotype data set available so far, the UKB, in which SHAPEIT4term·mth offers the best trade-off between speed and accuracyterm·dom across all tested methods. We predict SHAPEIT4term·mth to be particularly well suited to process data sets reaching a million individuals and above. Of note, the use of PBWTterm·mth in the context of phasingterm·key was pioneered in Eagle2term·key 3 , 4 in order to build compact representations of a fixed number of conditioning haplotypesterm·key (called HapHedge structures). This fundamentally differs from our approach in which we use PBWTterm·mth to identify a small and variable number of informative haplotypesterm·key to condition on. In addition, even though our use of PBWTterm·mth in this context involves overall running times that increase sub-linearly with sample sizeterm·var, this does not formally mean that the SHAPEIT4term·mth algorithm has sub-linear complexity as it still contains two components with linear complexity: I/O operations and PBWTterm·mth passes. However, these two linear components are expected to have small contributions to the overall running times in comparison of the HMM-based computations for sample sizesterm·var in the order of millions. Beyond phasingterm·key, we believe this PBWT-based approach to have the potential to speed up computations involved by other haplotype-based models used in population genetics for admixture mapping 26 , identity-by-decent (IBD) mapping 27 or genotype imputation 24 , 28 . Nowadays, haplotypeterm·key phasingterm·key is commonly performed on imputation servers such as the UMich server based on large reference panelsterm·key of haplotypesterm·key. We demonstrated that the initial implementation of SHAPEIT4term·mth already provides high performance in this context and we predict that this can be further improved by using other PBWTterm·mth algorithms (e.g., Algorithm 5 from Durbin 19 ). Besides computational efficiency, SHAPEIT4term·mth is also flexible enough to conveniently leverage additional phase information contained in long sequencing reads. We notably showed that SHAPEIT4term·mth is able to deliver very accurate haplotypesterm·key for high-coverage sequenced data when leveraging long or barcoded reads through a first step of haplotype assemblyterm·key (e.g., 10x Genomics). In addition, its ability to use haplotype scaffoldsterm·dom offers an interesting framework in which the high accuracyterm·dom delivered by very large SNP array data sets can be propagated into high-coverage sequencing data sets of smaller sample sizesterm·var. We predict that these two functionalities will help improving phasingterm·key servers dedicated to whole-genome sequenced samples 29 . It is also important to mention here that SHAPEIT4term·mth is not designed to perform genotype calling from low coverage sequencing and adapting the model for such data would need some additional work. Overall, we believe SHAPEIT4term·mth to be particularly well suited to fully exploit the potential of the large data sets being generated using either SNP arrays or new sequencing technologies.

Methods Haplotypeterm·key sampling Let us assume we have genotype data for N individuals across L variant sites that we want to phase in 2 N haplotypesterm·key. To achieve this, SHAPEIT4term·mth uses an iterative approach. It takes each individual’s genotype data G in turn and estimates a consistent pair of haplotypesterm·key D = ( h 1 , h 2 ) given the set H of haplotypesterm·key that has been previously estimated for the other N − 1 individuals. When repeated many times, this builds a Monte Carlo Markov chain (MCMC) is in which a single iteration consists in updating the haplotypesterm·key of all N individuals. To update the haplotypesterm·key of G , SHAPEIT4term·mth proceeds probabilistically by sampling a new pair of haplotypesterm·key from the posterior distribution Pterm·mth(D|H). This posterior is based on a particular Hidden Markov Model, the LSMterm·mth, that build haplotypesterm·key for G as mosaics of other haplotypesterm·key in H . This builds on the idea that individuals in a population share relatively long stretches of haplotypesterm·key inherited from common ancestors. While being accurate, the LSMterm·mth is also very computationally demanding and rapidly becomes intractable on large data sets. To ameliorate this, we introduced in SHAPEIT1 an algorithm able to sample from Pterm·mth(D|H) in linear times with the size of H . We achieved this by carrying out all LSMterm·mth computations on compact graph representations of all possible haplotypeterm·key configurations that are consistent with each individual (called genotype graph; see Supplementary Fig. 9 for a graphical description and DelaneaupersName et al. 21 for a formal one). In SHAPEIT4term·mth, we essentially use the same sampling algorithm that we completely re-implemented for better performance: - We used bitwise arithmetic for all genotype graph construction and manipulation routines which resulted in ~100× speed-ups in some cases. - We defined more compact data structuresterm·mth to store genotype graphs in memory, which resulted in a >3× decrease in memory usage. - We optimized the code for HMM computations to make it cache friendly and vectorised. This speeded up the HMM computations by ~3×.

Haplotypeterm·key selection A common approach to further speed up the sampling from Pterm·mth(D|H) is to only use a subset of K conditioning haplotypesterm·key instead of the full available set of 2 N − 2 haplotypesterm·key (with K « 2 N − 2). In SHAPEIT2 20 , we define the conditioning setterm·mth as the K haplotypesterm·key minimizing the Hamming distanceterm·mth with the current estimate D = ( h 1 , h 2 ). In practice, this requires a quadratic scan of the haplotypeterm·key data in O( LN 2 ) that becomes prohibitive when facing large sample sizesterm·var (e.g., N > 10,000). In SHAPEIT3 5 , we improved this by maintaining a clustering of the haplotypesterm·key so that Hamming distanceterm·mth minimization is only performed on the haplotypesterm·key (typically 4000) belonging to the same clusters than D . This approach exhibits close-to-linear running times in O( LN log N ). In SHAPEIT4term·mth, we propose a fully linear approach in O( LN ) to assemble a conditioning setterm·mth of K haplotypesterm·key that share long matches with D . To do so, we build a PBWTterm·mth of the full set of 2 N haplotypesterm·key before each MCMC iteration (so 15 times total) by running the algorithm 2 from Durbin 19 . This algorithm is very fast and only requires a single O( LN ) sweep through the data. This gives prefix and divergence arrays that we store every eight variants by default to reduce memory usage. A prefix array is a permutation vector of the original haplotypeterm·key indexes in which haplotypesterm·key sharing long prefixes (i.e., matches) are sorted next to each other. A divergence array specifies the starts of the matches. We extended the algorithm to also give a third array relating original haplotypeterm·key indexes to permuted ones so that any haplotypeterm·key can be located in the prefix or divergence arrays in constant time. Given these PBWTterm·mth arrays, we can then update the haplotypesterm·key for G as follows: - We identify the Pterm·mth haplotypesterm·key sharing the longest prefixes with h 1 and h 2 at each stored variants. By definition, these haplotypesterm·key are sorted next to h 1 and h 2 in the prefix arrays and the procedure has therefore running times proportional to O( L ). - We collapse the 2 LP /8 resulting haplotypesterm·key into a list of K distinct haplotypesterm·key (i.e., we remove duplicates). Long matches involve that the same haplotypesterm·key will be reported across multiple variants and therefore fewer distinct haplotypesterm·key once collapsed. - We run the LSMterm·mth onto the resulting conditioning setterm·mth to get new estimates for G .

Using the same set of conditioning haplotypesterm·key across entire chromosomes is inefficient since some of them would only be informative at particular genomic regions. To account for this, we implemented the phasingterm·key procedure described above in a sliding window of size W ( W = 2 Mb by default) similarly to what has been already done in SHAPEIT2. Overall, this gives a procedure that has multiple interesting properties. First, it provides informative haplotypesterm·key for the LSMterm·mth as they are guaranteed to share long matches with D in the region of interest. Second, it is fast since building the PBWTterm·mth and finding matches are done in running times proportional to O( LN ). Finally, it gives a number of conditioning haplotypesterm·key that varies depending on the matches found in the PBWTterm·mth: longer matches involve fewer distinct haplotypesterm·key and therefore smaller K . This implies that the size of the LSMterm·mth (i) adapts to the local level of relatedness between individuals, (ii) shrinks as the MCMC converges, and (iii) gets smaller for large sample sizesterm·var (i.e., sub-linearity with sample sizeterm·var).

MCMC iteration scheme In SHAPEIT2 and 3, we start from a random haplotypeterm·key resolution for all individuals and then perform 35 MCMC iterations to converge towards a reasonable haplotypeterm·key resolution. In SHAPEIT4term·mth, we implemented three new features to improve the mixing of the MCMC: - Initialization: We use a quick-and-dirty initialization of the haplotypesterm·key so that the MCMC does not start from a random resolution. To do so, we use a simplified version of the recursive phasingterm·key approach implemented in the PBWTterm·mth software package (see https://github.com/richarddurbin/pbwtterm·mth ), which provides initial haplotypeterm·key estimates very quickly in O( NL ) with SERsterm·mth in the range of 8–10% in our data. In practice, we first build a vector B l . + 1 that contains the 2 N alleles carried by the N individuals at variant l + 1 in which reference alleles are encoded as −1, alternative ones as 1 and those occurring at heterozygous genotypes as 0. Then, we phase genotypes by progressively imputing missing data in B l + 1 (i.e., zeros) given the PBWTterm·mth prefix array A l derived from the previous position l . Specifically, we make inference on the alleles carried by the two haplotypesterm·key of a given individual by copying those carried by neighbors as defined by the PBWTterm·mth prefix array A l (i.e., located just before and after in A l ). Of note, for efficiency reasons, this procedure also requires to keep track of indexes of the haplotypesterm·key in A l : we store those in a vector I l (see Fig. 5 ). Details on the exact procedure can be found in Fig. 5 . Once all missing data filled, we build the prefix array A l + 1 for position l + 1 and move to the next, l + 2, until completion. Fig. 5 Phase genotypes in B l + 1 using prefix array A l . - IBD2 protection: We designed an approach to prevent SHAPEIT4term·mth to copy haplotypesterm·key across individuals sharing both of their haplotypesterm·key IBD (i.e. IBD2). This typically happens in the case of siblings and constitutes a converge trap that can really hurt accuracyterm·dom (the two individuals just copy their haplotypesterm·key without making any updates). To do so, we extended the algorithm 3 of Durbin 19 to deal with the tri-allelic nature of genotype data and report genotype matches between individuals that are larger than W Mb (i.e., the sliding window). We then use the reported matches to define local constraints that we account for when building the conditioning setsterm·mth of haplotypesterm·key. By constraint, we mean here triplets ( i , j , w ), where i and j are two individuals that are IBD2 in window w . In other words, when updating the phase of an individual in a given window, we prevent from copying other individuals that are IBD2 in this window. - Specialized iterations: We designed three different types of iterations to help MCMC mixing. A burn-in iteration ( b ) uses transition probabilities in the genotype graphs to sample new pairs of haplotypesterm·key. A pruning iteration ( pterm·mth ) uses transition probabilities for sampling and also for trimming unlikely haplotypeterm·key configurations in the genotype graphs by merging consecutive segments. Of note, this pruning iteration differs from previous SHAPEIT versions: instead of doing a single pruning stage made of multiple iterations (=8), we perform multiple stages of pruning (by default 3), each made of a single iteration. Finally, a main iteration ( m ) samples haplotypesterm·key and stores transition probabilities so that they can be averaged at the end of the run to produce final estimates. The user can specify any sequence of iterations and the default is 5 b , 1 pterm·mth , 1 b , 1 pterm·mth , 1 b , 1 pterm·mth , 5 m , which we found to perform well.

As a consequence of these three features, SHAPEIT4term·mth needs a small number of MCMC iterations to reach good level of convergence: by default, it only performs 15 iterations as opposed to previous versions that required 35 iterations (2.3× more iterations).

Reference panelterm·key SHAPEIT4term·mth can borrow information from large reference panelterm·key of haplotypesterm·key, which proves to be particularly useful when phasingterm·key only few individuals. To achieve this, SHAPEIT4term·mth simply considers the additional reference haplotypesterm·key when building or updating the PBWTterm·mth to allow conditioning haplotypesterm·key to also originate from the reference panelterm·key. Besides this, the iteration scheme remains remarkably identical to the algorithm described above. Of note, SHAPEIT4term·mth only retains variants in the overlap between the main and the reference panelterm·key.

Haplotype scaffoldterm·dom SHAPEIT4term·mth also allows for some heterozygous genotypes to be phased a priori. This approach has been previously introduced in SHAPEIT2 in the context of the 1000 Genomes project to perform genotype calling from low coverage sequencing data 20 . This scaffold of pre-phased heterozygous genotypes can originate for instance from pedigrees in which many of the children’ genotypes can be accurately phased using Mendel inheritance logic. In practice, we implemented this functionality by simply pruning out all haplotypeterm·key configurations in the genotype graphs that are inconsistent with the available scaffold of haplotypesterm·key (Supplementary Fig. 9 ). Of note, SHAPEIT4term·mth does not have any requirements in terms of variant overlap between the main genotype data and the scaffold haplotypeterm·key data and therefore allows the latter to be derived from SNP array data while the former from high-coverage sequencing data. In the context of this work, we demonstrate the potential of this approach by deriving scaffolds from either trios or massive reference panelsterm·key.

Phase informative reads SHAPEIT4term·mth allows haplotypeterm·key estimation to be informed by sequencing reads overlapping multiple heterozygous genotypes. Haplotype assemblyterm·key methods such as Whatshap 12 or HapCut 13 are very efficient at regrouping heterozygous genotypes within phase setsterm·key: groups of nearby genotypes for which the phase is inferred from sequencing reads that overlap them. In practice, SHAPEIT4term·mth accommodates phase setsterm·key in a probabilistic manner (Supplementary Fig. 9 ). When sampling new haplotypesterm·key for G , it assumes an error model in which paths in the genotype graphs that are consistent with the phase setsterm·key receive more weight than paths that are not. In other words, SHAPEIT4term·mth samples haplotypesterm·key from the distribution Pterm·mth D∣H , R ∝ Pterm·mth ( D ∣ H ) Pterm·mth ( D ∣ R ) , where R represents the available phase setsterm·key for G and assuming that H and R are conditionally independent of D . The sampling strategy is essentially the same than the one we previously developed for SHAPEIT2 17 . Only the way we compute the distribution Pterm·mth(D|R) changes. We now use a simpler version in order to easily accommodate with new developments in sequencing technologies and haplotype assemblyterm·key methods. Briefly, the distribution Pterm·mth(D|R) is now controlled by a parameter that defines the expected error rate in the phase setsterm·key and not directly computed from the sequencing reads. By default, we assume an error rate of 0.0001 and used this value in all reported results. As a consequence, the sampled haplotypesterm·key D for G are not necessarily consistent with all the phase setsterm·key which allows correcting phasingterm·key errors in sequencing reads when phase setsterm·key are too discordant with population-based phasingterm·key. Of note, this method comes with three additional properties as a result of the data structureterm·mth we used to store the space of possible haplotypesterm·key. First, the method can handle cases in which phase setsterm·key are disjoint (i.e., overlapping), which typically occurs in case of paired-end reads with large insert sizes. Second, the method does not use phase setsterm·key that are only informative for non-consecutive segments in the genotype graph structure, which occurs for a phase setterm·key that connects two distal heterozygous genotypes spanning multiple other heterozygous genotypes not included in the phase setterm·key. This may happen in the case of paired-end reads with very large insert sizes or in the case of Hi-C data for instance. As a segment of the genotype graph initially contains three heterozygous genotypes, SHAPEIT4term·mth cannot use phase setsterm·key spanning more than three heterozygous genotypes in the first iterations. However, since consecutive segments are merged in the pruning iterations, SHAPEIT4term·mth is still able to use phase setsterm·key spanning more than three heterozygous genotypes in the last set of iterations. Third, when using phase setsterm·key together with a haplotype scaffoldterm·dom, the phase given by the scaffold is always prioritized which means that reads inconsistent with the scaffold are automatically discarded from the analysis.

I/O interface All the input and output interface implemented in SHAPEIT4term·mth is built on the High-Throughput Sequencing library (HTSlib 30 , 31 ) so that genotype and haplotypeterm·key data is read and written in either variant call format (VCF) or its binary version; the BCF format. This has multiple benefits. All data management on input and output files can be done using standard tools such as bcftools 31 . Using the BCF format significantly speeds up I/O operations. VCF/BCF formats natively define phase setsterm·key, which facilitates the integration of phase information contained in sequencing reads in SHAPEIT4term·mth. HTSlib allows reading simultaneously multiple VCF/BCF files, which facilitates complex SHAPEIT4term·mth runs combining, for instance, genotype data with phase setsterm·key, a reference panelterm·key of haplotypesterm·key and a scaffold of haplotypesterm·key.

The UKB data sets We generated genotype data sets with large sample sizesterm·var from the full release of the UKB data set containing 488,377 individuals genotyped by SNP arrays at 805,426 genetic variants. To do so, we proceeded according to the five following steps. First, we filtered out all genetic variants with more than 5% missing genotypes. Phasingterm·key runs on the UKB data sets We phased the UKB data sets using SHAPEIT4term·mth and three other widely used phasingterm·key methods: SHAPET3 ( https://jmarchini.org/shapeit3/ ), Eagle v2.4 ( https://github.com/poruloh/Eagle ), and Beagle5term·mth (beagle.14May18.ff7.jar; https://faculty.washington.edu/browning/beagle/beagle.html ). Eagle v2.4 and Beagle5term·mth have been run with default parameters. SHAPEIT3 has been run using the options–states 200,–cluster-size 4000 and–early-stopping. All methods were run using 10 threads to speed up computations. The reported running times and memory usages were obtained using the GNU time v1.9 command. The running times are computed as the sum of the User and System times and the memory usage as the Maximum resident set size. Then, we computed the SERterm·mth as the fraction of successive pairs of hets that are incorrectly phased over all possible pairs. For the analysis in which we introduced genotyping errors, we measured switch error ratesterm·mth only between genotypes without simulated errors.

• Eponymous or retrospective terms (e.g. “Nash equilibrium”, “segregation”) do not appear in the original source and so are not highlighted — a deliberate choice (see Report 4).
• Some entries begin with a word in capitals. EVIDENCE CORRECTED means the finding still stands and it was our quotation that was wrong — it has been replaced. RETRACTED means the entry itself was wrong; it stays on the page, marked, with its reason, rather than being deleted. WITHDRAWN AND REPLACED means a corrected version of it appears in the entries below. Each mark carries its own reason, because the reasons differ from one entry to the next. An entry we have WITHDRAWN is removed from this page and kept in the record, with its reason. A line reading Audit and a date is a later re-reading of the entry against its whole sentence: DOUBTFUL means it was kept, and the reason says what is loose about it.
• Quotation marks inside the tagger's sentence. Where a tag is the tagger's own sentence, a phrase in quotation marks inside it is borrowed from the paper — the tagger's wording around it is not. The paper's own sentence is the evidence quote shown on the same row, so the borrowing can be checked against the thing borrowed from without leaving this page. Nothing inside quotation marks anywhere on this page is ours: if it is quoted, it is the paper's. Whose words a concept carries. A concept entry records a term the paper itself uses, so the term, the sense given for it, and its evidence are the paper's own words, quoted where the paper fixes them. This is the opposite of a sense entry in the tag table, which is the tagger's sentence about how the paper uses a term. Where a concept entry could not be grounded in the paper's words, the entry says so on its own row.
