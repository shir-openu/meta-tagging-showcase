transferfrom toxicology: the hormesis biphasic dose-response model, with noise dose defined by analogy to an ingested toxicant dose (sound level times duration) — “Corresponding to the dose of toxicity that was ingested by organisms, the exposure dose of noise for organisms was the value of exposure sound level times exposure duration.”
mechanismA low-dose stressor breaks homeostasis and triggers a compensatory response that overshoots the inhibition, producing facilitation early on. — “Under the condition of low-dose stimulation, the compensatory response produced by organisms could be greater than the inhibitory response of stimuli, which resulted in the excitatory effect of stimuli.”
mechanismWith continued exposure the compensatory response is exhausted, so the inhibitory effect of the noise comes to dominate. — “It could be caused by a gradual exhaustion of compensatory response.”
mechanismGlutamate acting on hippocampal NMDA receptors admits calcium and thereby changes synaptic plasticity, which is the substrate of learning and memory. — “Subsequently, Mg 2+ , which blocked the channel of Ca 2+ , was removed, and the Ca 2+ flowed into the cell, which induced the change of synaptic plasticity.”

findingSix to ten days of exposure to 70 dB(A) combined traffic noise improved learning and memory in mice. — “The MWM test showed that the ability of learning and memory in mice was improved after short-term exposure (6–10 days, the first batch) to 70 dB(A) CTN, which showed the excitatory effect of stimuli.”
findingLonger exposure reversed the effect, degrading learning and memory. — “Long-term exposure (26–30 days, the third batch; 36–40 days, the fourth batch) led to the decline of learning and memory ability, which indicated the inhibitory effect of stimuli.”
findingLearning rate, measured as the slope of latency against training day, was significantly faster in exposed mice only in the first batch. — “The learning rate of the mice from the experimental group was significantly higher than that for the mice from the control group in the first batch (6–10 days) ( P < 0.05).”
findingMemory, measured as time in the target quadrant, was significantly better in exposed mice only in the first batch. — “The percentage of time spent in a target quadrant for the mice from the experimental group was significantly higher than that of the mice from the control group in the first batch (6–10 days) ( P < 0.05).”
findingHippocampal glutamate rose with short exposure and fell with long exposure, mirroring the behavioural pattern. — “Obviously, the level of Glu in mice rose after short-term exposure (6–10 days) to 70 dB(A) CTN, but the long-term exposure (26–30 days, 36–40 days) led to a decrease in the Glu level in mice.”
findingHippocampal GABA was unchanged by noise exposure at every time point. — “The GABA levels of the mice from the experimental group were approximate to those of the mice from the control group in all batches.”
findingMeasured glutamate stayed inside the normal range, ruling out excitotoxicity as the cause of the later decline. — “On the basis of this, it could be ruled out that too much release of Glu led to an excitatory toxic effect on the neurons in the hippocampus.”
findingThe biphasic time course of the noise effect matches the hormesis dose-response pattern. — “This biphasic dose-effect conformed to the characteristics of hormesis.”
