Abstract

Introduction

Perceptual decision-making involves multiple cognitive processes, including processing of sensoryterm stimuli, accumulation of evidence, and transformation of sensoryterm information into an appropriate motorterm plan. Although many brain regions have been implicated in perceptual decisions, dissociating their individual contribution to these different processes remains a challenge.

In particular, the posterior parietal cortex (PPCterm) has been hypothesized to play a key role in at least some types of decisionterm tasks in both primates 1 , 2 and in rodents 3 – 5 . However, the specific role of rodent parietal cortex, and whether its function is homologous to that of primates, remains unclear. While rodent PPCterm plays a minimal role in simple auditory 6 and whisker-based 7 decisionterm tasks (but see 8 ), multiple groups have demonstrated that it is causally necessary for make decisions on the basis of visualterm stimuli 3 , 4 , 9 , 10 .

However, the specific role of PPCterm in visualterm decision-making remains unclear. Some argue that rodent PPCterm may ultimately be more homologous to extrastriate cortex in processing sensoryterm signals that are accumulated elsewhere for decision-making 10 . Indeed, both anatomical projection studies 11 , 12 , as well as functional mapping studies 13 , 14 indicate that PPCterm may overlap with or contain a group of retinotopically-organized extrastriate areas that are rostral to primary visualterm cortex (V1term). Others suggest that PPCterm reflects internal biases related to the value of past stimuli 8 or actions 9 . However, removal of internal biases cannot fully explain the decision-making deficits induced by inactivation of PPCterm during visualterm decisionterm tasks 3 , 4 , 10 . A third, alternative possibility is that PPCterm may play a role in the mapping of visualterm stimuli to motorterm commands. If this were the case, one may expect that activity in PPCterm would be highly task-dependent, reflecting the animal’s decisionterm depending on learned sensorimotor contingencies.

Here, we use populationterm calcium imaging to investigate these possibilities by measuring activity in PPCterm and in V1term during a go/no-go lick-based visualterm discrimination taskterm. Having previously demonstrated the necessity of PPCterm in the performance of this taskterm 3 , we seek in this work to investigate its specific role in either sensoryterm processing or sensorimotor transformation by manipulating taskterm engagementterm and learned taskterm contingencies. V1term neuronsterm exhibit robust visualterm responses during both taskterm engagementterm and passiveterm viewing of stimuli that remain stable after taskterm contingencyterm reversal. By contrastterm, PPCterm responses are largely specific to taskterm performance and reflect the animal’s choiceterm before and after taskterm contingencyterm reversal. Our results are consistent with a role of the mouse posterior parietal cortex in transforming visualterm information to motorterm commands during perceptual decisions.

Imaging V1term and PPCterm during taskterm performance and passiveterm viewing

We trained mice on a head-fixed lickterm/no-lick visualterm discrimination taskterm (Fig. 1a, b ), similar to previous designs 3 , 15 , 16 . Water-restricted mice discriminated between a targetterm stimulusterm (horizontal grating drifting upwards, 0° from vertical, Stimulusterm A) which was rewarded with water, and a non-target stimulusterm of an orthogonal orientation (vertical drifting upwards rightwards, 90°, Stimulusterm B). Mice ( n = 15) achieved high levels of performance on the taskterm (Fig. 1c ; d-prime or d' , 2.23 ± 0.18; mean ± SEM), with a bias towards licking, resulting in more false alarms than misses.

Stimulus-period activity in PPCterm is therefore necessary for the taskterm, but it is unclear whether such activity is important for sensoryterm processing, attentional engagementterm, decisionterm formation, or motorterm planning. To help distinguish between these possibilities, we measured neural activity in PPCterm and V1term under two different conditions: engagementterm in the behavioral taskterm as well as passiveterm viewing of the same visualterm stimuli (Fig. 1b ). We used two-photon microscopy and a volumetric imaging approach to image hundreds of neuronsterm simultaneously in either V1term or PPCterm (see “Methods” section). After behavioral training, we injected AAV2/1 syn-GCaMP6s 17 into the two areas under stereotaxic guidance. Images were corrected for X – Y movement, and fluorescence traces were extracted from semi-automatically generated ROIs based on a pixel-wise activity map (see “Methods” section).

Nonetheless, mice rapidly became aware after the first 1–2 trials of a block whether the spout would be available for a behavioral response, as confirmed in video recordings by the complete lack of licking during passiveterm blocks. Discrimination performance was similarly high for both the first and subsequent engaged blocks.

How should one interpret this target-selective taskterm engagementterm signal in PPCterm? One possibility is that PPCterm encodes a stimulus-specific signal that is perhaps boosted by taskterm engagementterm. An alternative is that PPCterm activity reflects movement or action planning, since the animal licks on Engaged targetterm (Hit) trials but not on Passiveterm trials or Engaged nontarget (CR) trials.

Discussion

We developed a simple head-fixed visualterm decisionterm taskterm for mice with separate stimulusterm and response epochs, and used populationterm imaging of single neuron responses to investigate the role of PPCterm in perceptual decisions. Our key findings are that PPCterm encodes both sensoryterm and motorterm signals across a heterogeneous pool of neuronsterm, and that its activity reflects taskterm performance and demands. Together these results suggest that mouse PPCterm is responsible for neither pure sensoryterm processing, nor for the control of motorterm output, but rather is important for the decisionterm process itself – the process of mapping sensation to action.

The small size of the mouse brain has made it difficult to identify precise borders between different functional areas. PPCterm in the rodent, as classically defined by its thalamic inputs 26 , is located in the region between anterior to V1term and posterior to somatosensory cortex. However, this location is essentially where both anatomical 11 , 12 and functional 13 , 14 mapping studies have identified the retinotopically-organized areas RL, A, and AM. The degree to which PPCterm overlaps with these secondary visualterm areas is a matter of debate. Some have argued that rodent PPCterm may have more in common with primate extrastriate cortex, given that inactivation specifically disrupts sensitivity on visualterm decisions 10 . Indeed, the stereotaxic coordinates used by us 3 and others 4 , 27 to targetterm mouse PPCterm most directly overlap with area AM, which exhibits directionally-tuned responses even under anesthesia 14 . But is PPCterm simply a sensoryterm visualterm area?

We have presented multiple pieces of evidence that point to a role for PPCterm beyond mere sensoryterm processing. First, activity in PPCterm is strongly dependent on behavioral state, with only a minority of task-responsive PPCterm neuronsterm exhibiting significant responses during passiveterm viewing of stimuli (Fig. 2f ). Secondly, the selectivityterm of PPCterm neuronsterm is strongly biased toward targetterm stimuli (Fig. 2e ). This bias is likely due to the asymmetry of the Go/No-go paradigm, and may reflect a learned association of stimulusterm and rewardterm 28 . Third, PPCterm responses are modulated during errorterm trials (Fig. 3 , Supplementary Fig. 5 ). Information about the eventual choiceterm of the animal can be decoded from the activity of PPCterm, as previously shown in both mice 3 , 4 and rats 5 . Finally, and most conclusively, the biased selectivityterm of most PPCterm neuronsterm towards targetterm stimuli is dramatically reversed when the animal is retrained on a different rewardterm contingencyterm (Fig. 7 ). Together, these results demonstrate that the stimulus-period responses in PPCterm are task-dependent and not purely sensoryterm.

Alternatively, one may argue that the activity patterns we observed in PPCterm can be most parsimoniously explained as movement or motor-planning related signals. After all, movement-related activity would be present only during engagementterm, it would exhibit choiceterm selectivityterm, and it would change with rewardterm contingencyterm. It is unlikely that PPCterm is directly involved in executing motorterm plans, as we have previously shown that optogenetic inactivation of PPCterm during the response period, or even during the delay between stimulusterm and response, has no effect on behavior 3 . However, the possibility remains that the PPCterm responses recorded in our taskterm reflect planning- or movement-related signals that originate elsewhere. Although some PPCterm neuronsterm (~30%) do show activity that appears motor-related, due to their contrast-independent nature (Fig. 6 ), we provide evidence that PPCterm is not a purely motorterm (or motor-planning) area either.

First, passiveterm visualterm stimulation does induce a response in some PPCterm neuronsterm (~20%), as previously shown in parietal area AM of anesthetized mice 14 . This subset of neuronsterm tends to stably reflect the stimulusterm even with changes in rewardterm contingencyterm (Fig. 8 ). Second, we find a subset of PPCterm neuronsterm that reflect the targetterm stimulusterm on Miss trials when the animal fails to lickterm, even though such neuronsterm are inactive during Correct Reject trials (Supplementary Fig. 3 ). Finally, the responses of many PPCterm neuronsterm (~40%) shows modulation with stimulus contrastterm·var, even for the same decisionterm and motorterm output (Fig. 6 ). This is reminiscent of previous primate 2 and rodent 23 PPCterm studies, where it has been shown that responses vary with the strength of incoming sensoryterm evidence. These findings cohere with prior work in a delayed-response version of our taskterm 3 , in which choice-related selectivityterm was highest shortly after stimulusterm presentation and became weaker over the course of the trial. This argues against a motor-planning explanation which would instead predict increased choiceterm coding with time.

In every taskterm condition reported here, PPCterm responses were heterogeneous. A subset of PPCterm neuronsterm have significant passiveterm visualterm responses (~30%, Fig. 2f , E + P) and are modulated by contrastterm in both engaged and passiveterm conditions (~20%, Fig. 6a , contrastterm). These neuronsterm maintain their stimulusterm selectivityterm even after reversal of rewardterm contingencyterm (~10%, Fig. 8a , Stim). These “stimulusterm” neuronsterm are spatially intermingled with the larger proportion of “choiceterm” neuronsterm that have task-gated responses (~70%, Fig. 2f , E only), weak contrastterm modulation (~30%, Fig. 6a , engagementterm), and choice-selective responses after contingencyterm reversal (~70%, Fig. 8a , Go). Furthermore, a third group of neuronsterm have multiplexed selectivityterm to stimulusterm, engagementterm, and motorterm signals, exhibiting complex responses to combinations of sensoryterm or motorterm signals in engaged conditions. These cells exhibit modulation to both contrastterm and engagementterm (~30%, Fig. 6a , both) and their responses cannot be explained by partial GLMterm·mth models that only use a subset of the taskterm variables (~35%, Supplementary Fig. 4f ).

Heterogeneous response properties have been previously reported in both primate 29 , 30 and rodent 5 , 31 PPCterm during decisionterm tasks. Our work adds to this literature, and additionally provides evidence that such heterogeneous responses exist in a spatially intermingled fashion within PPCterm of the mouse 32 , 33 . Do these response types form bona-fide cell classes or does PPCterm represent a category-free populationterm, as others have proposed 5 ? Although we do find that various properties (such as modulation by engagementterm, contrastterm, and reversal) are correlated with one another, more work needs to be done to determine whether functional neuronal subgroups are truly separable.

Recent studies in rodents have suggested that some of the heterogeneity observed in PPCterm responses may be due to encoding of information from recent experience, including information about past sensoryterm stimuli 8 , previous choices 9 , 27 , and the presence or absence of rewardterm. Such information about history could bias performance on sensorimotor tasks by providing animals with prior expectations about the value of particular stimuli 8 or actions 9 . While our findings do not allow us to directly address history-dependent biases in action selection, they are consistent with a role for PPCterm in selecting future motorterm actions.

Our results build on previous work in the field in several ways: First, we show that PPCterm neuronsterm are actively gated by engagementterm in a sensorimotor taskterm, with a substantial subpopulation of neuronsterm exhibiting sensoryterm responses only during taskterm performance. Second, we used analysis of variable contrastterm stimuli and a GLMterm·mth model to delineate the relative contributions of stimulusterm, motorterm preparation, and engagementterm to the responses of both V1term and PPCterm. These analyses showed that visualterm and motorterm signals are multiplexed within individual neuronsterm. Finally, we showed that reversing rewardterm contingencies causes the response selectivityterm to swap for the vast majority of PPCterm cells, indicating that the multiplexing of stimulusterm and response can be flexibly mapped depending on taskterm contingencies. Taken together, these results bolster the evidence that PPCterm neuronsterm are capable of mediating visuomotor transformations, although further evidence is necessary to establish this possibility conclusively.

The mouse posterior parietal cortex encodes behaviorally-relevant variables in a highly task-dependent manner, in analogy to prior work in primates. Our understanding of how decisions are computed and visuomotor transformations are made will be greatly aided by future circuit-level analyses of PPCterm function in this powerful model system 34 .

Methods

Mice and surgery

All experiments were carried out in mice of either sex using protocols approved by Massachusetts Institute of Technology’s Animal Care and Use Committee and conformed to National Institutes of Health guidelines. Data were collected from adult (3–5 months old) wild-type (C57BL/6; n = 15) mice of either sex. The animals were housed on a 12-hour light/dark cycle in cages of up to 5 animals before the implants, and individually after the implants. All surgeries were conducted under isoflurane anesthesia (3.5% induction, 1.5–2.5% maintenance). Meloxicam (1 mg kg −1 , subcutaneous) was administered pre-operatively and every 24 h for 3 days to reduce inflammation. Once anesthetized, the scalp overlying the dorsal skull was sanitized and removed. The periosteum was removed with a scalpel and the skull was abraded with a drill burr to improve adhesion of dental acrylic. Stereotaxic coordinates for future viral injections were marked with a non-toxic ink and covered with a layer of silicon elastomer (Kwik-Sil, World Precision Instruments) to prevent acrylic bonding. The entire skull surface was then covered with dental acrylic (C&B-Metabond, Parkell) mixed with black ink to reduce light transmission. A custom-designed stainless-steel head plate (eMachineShop.com) was then affixed using dental acrylic. After head plate implantation, mice recovered for at least 5 days before beginning water restriction.

After behavioral training was complete, animals were taken off water restriction for 5 days before undergoing a second surgery to implant the imaging window. Procedures for anesthetic administration and post-operative care were identical to the first surgery. The dental acrylic and silicon elastomer covering the targeted region were removed using a drill burr. The skull surface was then cleaned and a craniotomy was performed over left V1term/PPCterm, leaving the dura intact. Neuronsterm were labeled with a genetically-encoded calcium indicator by microinjection (Stoelting) of 50 nl AAV2/1.Syn.GCaMP6s.WPRE.SV40 (University of Pennsylvania Vector Core; diluted to a titer of 10 12 genomes ml −1 ) 300 µm below the pial surface. Between two and five injections were made in each exposed region, centered at V1term (4.2 mm posterior, 2.5 mm lateral to Bregma) and PPCterm (2 mm posterior, 1.7 mm lateral to Bregma). Since the viral expression spreads laterally from the injection site, exact stereotaxic locations were photographed through the surgical microscope for determining imaging areas. Finally, a cranial window was implanted over the craniotomy and sealed first with silicon elastomer then with dental acrylic. The cranial windows were made of two rounded pieces of coverglass (Warner Instruments) bonded with optical glue (NOA 61, Norland). The bottom piece was a circular coverglass (4 mm diameter) that fit snugly in the craniotomy. The top piece was a larger circular coverglass (3–5 mm, depending on size of bottom piece) and was bonded to the skull using dental acrylic. Mice recovered for 5 days before commencing water restriction.

Behavioral tasks

We trained mice to perform a head-fixed go/no-go visualterm discrimination taskterm, similar to previous designs 3 . Stimuli consisted of full-contrast sine wave gratings (spatial frequency: 0.05 cycles deg −1 ; temporal frequency: 2 Hz) drifting at either 0° (upwards, targetterm, Stimulusterm A) or 90° (rightwards, non-target, Stimulusterm B) away from vertical. Stimuli were presented to the right eye alone by placing the screen at an oblique angle to the animal. Behavioral training and testing was implemented with custom software written in Matlab (Mathworks) using Psychtoolbox-3 35 and Data Acquisition toolbox. Spout position was controlled by mounting the spout apparatus on a pneumatically-driven sliding linear actuator (Festo) controlled by two solenoids. Licks were detected using an infrared emitter/receiver pair (Digikey) mounted on either side of the retractable lickterm spout. Mice were water-restricted and earned most of their daily ration (1 mL) during training.

An auditory cue tone (5 kHz, 0.5 s, 65 dB SPL) indicated the beginning of each trial. After a 1 s delay, a visualterm stimulusterm was presented for 2 s. At the end of the stimulusterm epoch, the spout was rapidly moved within reach of the tongue, and remained within reach for 1.5 s. Correct licks during this period were rewarded with 5–8 µl water and a brief rewardterm tone (10 kHz, 0.1 s). Licks to the non-target stimulusterm were punished with a white noise auditory stimulusterm alone (early training) or white noise plus 1–3 µl of 5 mM quinine hydrochloride in water (late training). This concentration was chosen to deter licking to non-targets without causing mice to lose motivation altogether (Supplementary Fig. 1 ). At the end of the response epoch, the spout was then rapidly retracted and remained out of reach until the next trial (3 s inter-trial interval).

Mice were trained in successive stages, as previously described 3 . Briefly, mice were first trained to lickterm a stationary lickterm spout during presentation of the targetterm stimulusterm, and then non-target stimuli were gradually introduced. Spout withdrawal was introduced once mice showed good discrimination performance ( d' > 1 and R HIT − R FA > 30% for consecutive sessions), with the spout initially being extended before stimulusterm onset, but gradually delayed to extend after stimulusterm offset. Once mice reached high levels of performance at the final stage of the taskterm ( d' > 1.5 and R HIT − R FA > 50%), they were removed from water restriction for window implantation. Mice reached criterion performance after an average of 92 ± 11 sessions. After recovery from window implantation surgery, they were re-trained to a level of high performance (2–7 days) before beginning experimental sessions. Any sessions with poor performance were discarded (minimum performance criterion: d' > 1 and R HIT − R FA > 30%).

During imaging experiments, blocks of engaged behavior trials were alternated with blocks of passiveterm viewing. Blocks were 5–10 min in duration (40–80 trials per block). During passiveterm blocks, the spout was out of reach for the duration of the block. A few extra passiveterm trials were given (without imaging) before each passiveterm block to ensure that mice did not expect spout presentation during all imaged passiveterm trials. The sequence of targetterm and non-target stimuli presented for a given passiveterm block was matched to the sequence of stimuli used for the preceding engaged block. In some cases, instead of alternating between engaged and passiveterm blocks, the engaged blocks were all grouped together at the beginning of the session, followed by an equal number of consecutive passiveterm blocks. No difference in results was observed for alternating vs. grouped blocks.

Some mice ( n = 6) were trained on a variable contrastterm version of the taskterm. On each trial, the stimulusterm was randomly set to one of six contrasts (2, 4, 8, 16, 32, or 64%), regardless of whether the stimulusterm was a targetterm or non-target. The mouse therefore could not predict the contrastterm of the stimulusterm from trial to trial.

Some mice ( n = 3) were re-trained after initial imaging experiments on a reversed rewardterm contingencyterm. Rewardterm contingencyterm was switched abruptly, with rewardterm given for licks to Stimulusterm B and no rewardterm for licks to Stimulusterm A. Because mice were quickly discouraged by the reversal, no quinine punishment was initially given. Additionally, the rewardterm tone (10 kHz, 0.1 s) was paired with the onset of the new targetterm stimulusterm (Stimulusterm B) early in re-training, in order to encourage licking. Three of the five mice trained on this reversed contingencyterm achieved criterion performance after re-training for 10 ± 2 days; the other two were removed from the study.

For all tasks, behavioral d-primeterm·var ( d' ) was computed by norminv(Hit rate) – norminv(False alarm rate), where norminv() is the inverse of the cumulative normal function 34 , 36 . Values of Hit and False alarm rate were truncated between 0.01 and 0.99, setting the maximum d' to 4.65. For illustration purposes, d-prime before and after reversal was computed using Stimulusterm A as the targetterm in both conditions (Fig. 7b ).

Two-photon imaging

GCaMP6s fluorescence was imaged 14–35 days after virus injection using Prairie Ultima IV 2-photon microscopy system with a resonant galvo scanning module (Bruker). For fluorescence excitation, we used a Ti-Sapphire laser (Mai-Tai eHP, Newport) with dispersion compensation (Deep See, Newport) tuned to λ = 910 nm. For collection, we used GaAsP photomultiplier tubes (Hamamatsu). To achieve a wide field of view, we used a 16× / 0.8 NA microscope objective (Nikon), which was mounted on a z-piezo (Bruker) for volume scanning. An optical zoom of 2× was used in most cases to improve spatial resolution. Resonant scanning (15.9 kHz line rate, bidirectional) was synchronized to z-piezo steps in the acquisition software for volume scanning. For volume scanning, four 441 × 512 pixel imaging planes separated by 20 or 25 μm were imaged sequentially at a stack rate of 5 Hz in 5–10 min imaging blocks. There was very little redundant sampling of neuronsterm between imaging planes (<1%) as assayed by correlation coefficient of spontaneous activity. Laser power ranged from 40–75 mW at the sample depending on GCaMP6s expression levels. Photobleaching was minimal (<1% min −1 ) for all laser powers used. A custom stainless steel plate (eMachineShop.com) attached to a black curtain was mounted to the head plate before imaging to prevent light from the visualterm stimulusterm monitor from reaching the photomultiplier tubes. During imaging experiments, the polypropylene tube supporting the mouse was suspended from the behavior platform with high tension springs (Small Parts) to dampen movement.

Image preprocessing and cell selection

Calcium imaging data were acquired using PrairieView acquisition software and sorted into multi-page TIF files. All analyses were performed using custom scripts written either in ImageJ or MATLAB (Mathworks).

Images were first corrected for X–Y movement by registration to a reference image (the pixel-wise mean of all frames) using 2-dimensional cross correlation. To identify responsive neural somata, a pixel-wise activity map was calculated as previously described 37 . Neuron cell bodies were identified using local adaptive threshold and iterative segmentation. Automatically-defined ROIs were then manually checked for proper segmentation in a MATLAB-based graphical user interface (allowing comparison to raw fluorescence and activity map images). To subtract the influence of local neuropil on somatic signals, the fluorescence in the somata was estimated as F corrected_soma ( t ) = F raw_soma ( t ) − 0.7 × F neuropil ( t ), where F neuropil was the defined as the fluorescence in the region 0–15 mm from the ROI border (excluding other ROIs) 17 . Δ F/F for each neuron was calculated as Δ F / F t = ( F t − F 0 ) / F 0 , with F 0 defined as the mode of the raw fluorescence density distribution.

To align ROIs between different imaging sessions across days (Figs. 7 and 8 ), we used a semi-automated method similar to prior work 25 . First, for each plane, anchor points were manually defined by visualterm comparison of the two average projection images. These anchor points helped to define a predicted displacement vector field that would be used to map coordinates from one session to the other. For each coordinate, the predicted vector was defined by the average (weighted inversely by distance) of the vectors for all defined anchor points.

Next, for each ROI, a square region (~4 × the size of the ROI) around the ROI was selected. To determine the displacement across sessions, we computed the normalized cross-correlation of this square with the average projection of the other session. This was multiplied point-by-point with a mask that decayed gradually with distance from the predicted displacement vector, and then smoothed with a 2-D Gaussian filter. The peak of the resulting image was taken to be the actual displacement vector of the ROI. This process biases the displacement of each ROI towards the vector predicted from the manually defined anchor points. Finally, any ROIs with a computed displacement vector that differed by greater than 5 pixels from the predicted vector were flagged for manual inspection, and then either redrawn or removed.

After image preprocessing and Δ F/F extraction, traces were sorted by trial type (Hit, Miss, Correct Reject, False Alarm) and condition (Engaged, Passiveterm). The baseline response (1 s before stimulusterm onset) was subtracted from each trial. A neuron was considered taskterm responsive if its mean Δ F/F during the last 1.6 s (8 frames) of the stimulusterm period was significantly ( p < 0.01, t -test) greater than the pre-stimulus baseline (1 s), for either hit or correct reject trials. Neuronsterm also had to meet a signal-to-noise criterion, needing a trial-averaged response that exceeded a threshold of at least two standard deviations above baseline during either the stimulusterm or response period. All further analyses are based on responses during the last 1.6 s of the stimulusterm period, unless noted otherwise. Cell selection criteria for errorterm analyses, variable contrastterm analyses, and reversal analyses are described in the appropriate sections below.

Statistics

The data were obtained from 15 mice, 5 with V1term only, 6 with PPCterm only, and 4 with both V1term and PPCterm. For most mice, multiple fields of view were sampled within V1term or within PPCterm. For mice with both V1term and PPCterm windows, fields from each area were sampled on interleaved sessions. Each field was imaged for a single session, consisting of multiple Engaged and Passiveterm blocks and yielding on average 94 trials (minimum 47) per stimulusterm per condition. For variable contrastterm tasks, an average of 25 trials (minimum 8) were acquired per contrastterm. Significantly task-responsive cells from different fields were pooled by area. No tests were conducted to determine sample size. For the full-contrast taskterm, the data came from 1915 V1term cells (18 fields, 9 mice) and 3524 PPCterm cells (22 fields, 10 mice). For the variable-contrast taskterm, the data came from 250 V1term cells (8 fields, 3 mice) and 611 PPCterm cells (11 fields, 6 mice). For comparisons before and after contingencyterm reversal, the data came from 488 V1term cells (8 fields, 3 mice) and 509 PPCterm cells (8 fields, 3 mice).

All statistical analysis was performed using custom-written scripts in MATLAB or R. In all cases, data was not assumed to be normal, and nonparametric and/or permutation tests (2000 permutations) were used to assess statistical significance of results. All tests were two-tailed, and a significance level of p < 0.05 was considered significant. Unless otherwise noted, all measures are reported as meanterm·stat ± SEM. When estimating the percentage of selective or modulated neuronsterm, bootstrapping across imaging fieldsterm·stat was used to generate confidence intervals on the percentages for each area. When testing the statistical significance of differences between V1term and PPCterm neuronsterm that were pooled across imaging fields, clustered nonparametric teststerm·stat 38 , 39 were used to account for intra-cluster correlations 31 , 40 using the clusrank package in R.

Selectivityterm and errorterm trial analyses

Neuronsterm were marked as target- or non-target-preferring (Fig. 2e ) based on their mean response during Engaged trials. Neuronsterm were marked as task-gated if they did not exhibit a significant response to their preferred stimulusterm during Passiveterm trials (Fig. 2f ).

All comparative indices (engagementterm modulation index, errorterm modulation index, contrastterm modulation index, selectivityterm index) were computed using a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysisterm·stat, which quantifies the ability of an ideal observer to discriminate between trial types based on single trial responses 18 , 36 . Each index was derived from the area under the ROC curve (auROC), and defined as 2×( auROC –0.5); this value ranged from −1 to 1 5 . Unless otherwise noted, comparative indices were computed by comparing the stimulusterm period response (averaged over the last 1.6 s). To determine whether comparative indices were significant for individual neuronsterm, we used a permutation test. We shuffled the labels for each trial and recomputed the index 2000 times to create a distribution of indices that could have arisen by chance. Indices outside the center 95% interval of this distribution were considered significant ( p < 0.05).

For errorterm trial analyses, only behavioral sessions and imaging fields with at least five trials per condition were used. Consecutive miss trials occurring at the end of a session were excluded, as these trials are confounded by lack of motivation. Analysis was additionally limited to Hit-preferring neuronsterm. For analysis of False Alarm trials (Fig. 3 ), we analyzed 1053 V1term cells from 16 fields, and 3034 PPCterm cells from 21 fields. For analysis of Miss trials (Supplementary Fig. 3 ), we analyzed 202 V1term cells from 3 sessions, and 1165 PPCterm cells from 8 fields.

We assessed “stimulus-related” selectivityterm by comparing Hit to FA trials or Miss to CR trials. We define “choice-related” selectivityterm as any premotor- or decision-related signal, assessed by comparing FA to CR trials or Hit to Miss trials (Supplementary Table 1 ). For most comparisons, any selectivityterm measured after the stimulusterm period may include signals related to rewardterm, punishment, or motorterm output. We therefore restricted analyses of selectivityterm to the stimulusterm period.

Selectivityterm was computed across time using a ROC-based index evaluated independently at each time bin (200 ms) 19 , 29 . A clustered Wilcoxon signed-rank testterm·stat 39 was used at each time bin to test whether average selectivityterm for each area was significantly different from zero ( p < 0.05, no correction for multiple comparisons).

To assess the dependence of PPCterm responses on motorterm output (Supplementary Fig. 2 ), we noticed that a few animals exhibited natural variability in licking behavior across False Alarm (FA) trials. We analyzed neuronsterm from the sessions (846 PPCterm cells from 3 fields) that had at least five FA trials with 1–3 licks and five FA trials with 5 or more licks. We additionally randomly selected Hit trials with the same number of licks (5–9) to match the average number of licks in the FA (5–9) condition. Selectivityterm between these conditions was evaluated using a ROC-based index as above.

Generalized linear models

We used generalized linear models (GLMterm·mth) to regress recorded calcium signals against a time series of taskterm events 20 – 22 . Calcium responses for each cell were z -scored and modeled as the linear combination of various taskterm events, each convolved with a filter:

ŷ t = β 0 + ∑ i = 0 20 β i S tar x t - i S tar + ∑ i = 0 20 β i S nt x t - i S nt + β 0 E x E + ∑ i = 0 20 β i E tar x t - i E tar + ∑ i = 0 20 β i E nt x t - i E nt + ∑ i = - 10 15 β i L x t - i L 1

The response of a neuron at frame t is modelled ( ŷ t ) by the sum of a bias term ( β 0 ) and the weighted ( β i ) sum of various additional binary predictors at different lags ( i ). Binary predictors for the targetterm stimulusterm ( x t S tar ) and non-target stimulusterm ( x t S nt ) indicated the onset of stimulusterm presentation in either engaged or passiveterm trials. Binary predictors for engagementterm included a constant offset ( x E ) that was 1 during engaged trials and 0 otherwise, as well as stimulusterm predictors ( x t E tar , x t E nt ) that indicated the duration of stimulusterm presentation during engaged trials only. Binary predictors ( x t L ) for licking indicated the duration of lickterm bouts, which were defined as groups of licks with an inter-lick interval <1 s. The number of lags were chosen to capture both the duration of the event (2 s for stimulusterm, 1 s for licking) as well as the slow offset dynamics of the calcium response 17 . Lags were chosen to be strictly positive (causal) for stimulusterm and engagementterm predictors, but both positive and negative (anti-causal) for licking predictors. The final model had 112 coefficients including a constant bias term. Models were fit for all task-responsive neuronsterm, where the first 5 s from each trial (following auditory cue onset) was extracted and concatenated for analysis.

Models were fit using ridge regressionterm·stat using procedures similar to that of previous studies 20 , 22 , 41 . We first set aside 20% of trials from each condition (Hit, Correct Reject, Miss, False Alarm, Passiveterm Targetterm, Passiveterm Nontarget) for testing. A regularization parameter λ was estimated for each cell from among a range of λ values (10 −2 to 10 4 ) using cross-validation. Model performance was measured by computing the proportion of explained variance, or the coefficient of determinationterm·stat ( R 2 ). Five-fold cross-validationterm·stat was used to choose the λ that maximized the performance on the remaining 80% of training trials. The final model for each cell was fit using the best λ , and then performance was evaluated by measuring R 2 of predictions for the holdout test set.

The strength of each model component (stimulusterm, engagementterm, motorterm/licking) was evaluated for each V1term and PPCterm cell (Fig. 4f ) by setting all other model coefficients to zero and finding the model predictions using just one component, averaging across both training and test trials. Model components were therefore in units of z -scored calcium response and can be directly compared with calcium response waveforms. For stimulusterm and engagementterm components, the preferred trial type (Hit or Correct Reject) was used for each cell, whereas for the motorterm component, Hit trials were used for all cells. To evaluate the relative strength of the various components across V1term and PPCterm cells, the component strength for each cell was averaged over a window of 0 to 4 s after stimulusterm onset, and then a Wilcoxon rank-sum testterm·stat was applied.

We also trained GLMterm·mth models with a subset of the taskterm predictors (Partial GLMterm·mth models, Supplementary Fig. 4 ) using the same procedures. Three additional models were trained for each cell: a stimulus-only model which included the stimulusterm and constant bias terms (43 coefficients), a motor-only model which included only the licking and bias terms (27 coefficients), and a stimulusterm + engagementterm model which used all terms except licking (86 coefficients). A separate cross-validation process and regularization parameter was computed for each model, but the same training and test trials were using to evaluate performance.

Performance on the partial models was evaluated only for cells with significant fits ( p < 0.05) to the full model. The test trial dataset was bootstrapped 2000 times, and a p value was computed for each cell as the fraction of iterations with R 2 ≤ 0. ( R 2 can be negative if the model performs worse than a horizontal line on the held-out test data). The same bootstrapped datasets were also used to determine whether each partial model performed significantly above chance ( p < 0.05), or significantly worse than the full model fit ( p < 0.05).

Contrastterm taskterm analysis

For data acquired during the variable contrastterm taskterm, neuronsterm were considered significantly responsive if the mean Δ F/F during the stimulusterm period was significantly above threshold for at least two of the six contrasts of the same stimulusterm. We focused our analyses on target-preferring neuronsterm, which were included if their mean response across contrasts was greater for Hit (targetterm) trials compared to Correct Reject trials.

Single neuron contrast-response functions were fit to the hyperbolic ratio function, also known as the Naka-Rushton function 24 :

R C = R max C n C n + C 50 n + R 0 2

where R(C) is the neural response as a function of contrastterm, R max is the saturation point, C 50 is the contrastterm at the half-saturation point, R 0 is the baseline response, and n is an exponent that determines the steepness of the curve. The responses for both Engaged and Passiveterm conditions were fit simultaneously, with n constrained to be constant across conditions, by minimizing the sum (across data points) of the squared errorterm between the model and the data, divided by the variance of that data point.

We evaluated the goodness of fit for each neuron using a bootstrap hypothesis testterm·stat, as detailed by others 42 . Briefly, we tested the null hypothesis that the mean of the probability distribution underlying the neural responses was identical to the predictions of the model. We measured the observed prediction errorterm ( e obs ) and computed the probability of observing an errorterm at least as large if the null hypothesis were true. To sample from a distribution that conformed to the null hypothesis, we shifted the data such that the mean responses equaled the model predictions, and drew 1000 bootstrap samples from this dataset, computing a prediction errorterm ( e i ) for each. The proportion of samples for which the prediction errorterm was larger than e obs is the achieved significance level. For neuronsterm with an achieved significance level below 10% ( p < 0.1), there was sufficiently strong evidence against the model, and therefore these neuronsterm were excluded from further analysis.

Contrastterm modulation index was computed to compare Engaged trial responses on high (64%, 32%) vs. low (2%, 4%) contrasts, using a renormalized ROC index which ranged from −1 to 1, as described above. Engagementterm modulation index was computed by comparing Engaged vs. Passiveterm trials, using high contrastterm trials only. A permutation test was used to assess significance ( p < 0.05) of these indices by shuffling trial labels 2000 times and comparing the measured index to the shuffled distribution of indices.

To assess anatomical spatial organization, pairwise Euclidean distance was measured between all task-responsive, target-preferring neuronsterm in the same imaged volume (Fig. 6e–g ). Cells were grouped into four groups based on whether they were contrast-modulated, engagement-modulated only, both, or neither, and then the average within-group and across-group distance was computed for each cell (Fig. 6f ). To compute differences in modulation index as function of distance (Fig. 6g ), pairs of cells were binned by distance in 40 µm bins.

Selectivityterm on errorterm trials (Supplementary Fig. 5 c-f) was computed by comparing FA trials to Hit and CR trials (with matched contrastterm) using a frame-by-frame ROC analysis, as described above. The contrast-dependence of the auROC-based index was estimated by regressing the index against log contrastterm, and then finding the slope. The significance ( p < 0.05) of the slope was estimated for each cell by shuffling the trial labels 2000 times for each contrastterm and then computing the auROC on the shuffled data to generate a distribution of 2000 slopes.

Reverse contingencyterm taskterm analysis

We imaged from the same field of neuronsterm before and after reversal of rewardterm contingencyterm. The two sessions were separated by an average time interval of 16 ± 1 days. A semi-automated method was used to align ROIs between the two sessions (see "Image preprocessing and cell selection" section). Neuronsterm were included for analysis only if a significant response ( p < 0.01) to either stimulusterm was observed both before and after reversal. Selectivityterm index was computed separately before and after reversal training (Fig. 7e–h ) by comparing responses to the Stimulusterm A (original targetterm) and Stimulusterm B (original non-target), with positive values indicating preference for the Stimulusterm A. Separate permutation tests (2000 iterations) were used to assess significance of the selectivityterm before and after reversal. Neuronsterm with significant selectivityterm ( p < 0.05) both before and after reversal were categorized (Fig. 8a ) based on the sign of the selectivityterm, and whether it was stable or altered with reversal. Engagementterm modulation index (Fig. 8b ) for each neuron was computed separately before and after reversal, comparing Engaged and Passiveterm responses using the neuron’s preferred stimulusterm, and then taking the mean of the two values. Errorterm modulation index (Fig. 8c ) for each neuron was also computed separately before and after reversal, comparing FA and CR responses, and then taking the mean of the two values.
