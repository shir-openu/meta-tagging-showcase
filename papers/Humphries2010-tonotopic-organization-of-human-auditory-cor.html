limitationThe absence of a gradient corresponding to macaque area RT may be a sensitivity failure of the stimuli rather than evidence that the area is absent in humans. — “The lack of frequency-selective activation in this region could be due to low sensitivity to the stimuli used in the current study.”
limitationThe sample was small enough to force non-parametric permutation testing for the group analysis. — “Because of the small number of subjects, significance was calculated using a non-parametric permutation test ( Nichols and Holmes 2002 ).”
limitationthe tested range truncates the tuning estimate, so points preferring 200 or 6400 Hz were excluded from that analysis — “Points were also excluded if their largest response was to the lowest (200 Hz) or highest (6400 Hz) frequency. The reason for this is that activation levels at the lowest and highest frequencies may not reflect the largest possible response for that brain area, because the area could possibly have larger responses to even lower or higher frequencies that were not included in the experiment. Therefore, normalized values for these regions could be inaccurate.”
limitationan absence the authors decline to resolve: no anterior RT-like gradient, which may be the stimuli or may be the species — “The lack of frequency-selective activation in this region could be due to low sensitivity to the stimuli used in the current study. limitationthe core/belt boundary cannot be fixed from frequency response alone, so the line drawn is an estimate imported from other evidence — “The exact boundary between core and belt cannot be determined based solely on frequency response, thus this line is an estimate guided by the known topographical organization in the macaque and by previous human cytoarchitectonic studies”

findingTwo mirror-symmetric frequency gradients run anterior-posterior from a low-frequency zone on lateral Heschl's gyrus out to high-frequency zones in front of and behind it. — “Two mirror-symmetric frequency gradients were found extending along an anterior-posterior axis from a zone on the lateral aspect of Heschl's gyrus (HG), which responds preferentially to lower frequencies, toward zones posterior and anterior to HG that are sensitive to higher frequencies.”
findingThese principal gradients lie perpendicular to HG, overturning the prevailing assumption that they run parallel to it. — “The orientation of these two principal gradients is thus roughly perpendicular to HG, rather than parallel as previously assumed.”
findingA third, smaller gradient was found on the lateral posterior superior temporal gyrus. — “A third, smaller gradient was observed in the lateral posterior aspect of the superior temporal gyrus.”
findingThe human maps are argued to be closely homologous to nonhuman primate auditory cortex, with the two main gradients corresponding to macaque A1 and R. — “Based on these similarities, it is likely that the two gradients observed in the current study encompass two separate regions of cortex homologous to macaque areas A1 and R.”

A second study, however, described two mirror-symmetric frequency gradients (high-low-high) extending approximately along the axis of HG ( Formisano et al., 2003 ). In a third study, three consistent gradients were reported, none of which clearly follow the long axis of HG ( Talavage et al., 2004 ). Finally, the authors of a fourth study found differences in activation between anterior and posterior HG as well as medial and lateral differences, but concluded that the observed activation profile did not represent frequency gradients but instead different functional regions within the auditory cortex”
