content-observations: finding · quantity · variable · definition · key-term — grounded in the full text
findingMotor learning reorganized dendritic spines only on the distal apical dendrites of L2/3 excitatory neurons in layer 1, leaving perisomatic dendrites stable, i.e. the plasticity was confined to a specific dendritic compartment. — “Here using in vivo two-photon imaging in awake mice, we found that learning-induced spine reorganization of L2/3 excitatory neurons occurs in the distal branches of their apical dendrites in L1 but not in the perisomatic dendrites.”
findingThe two inhibitory neuron subtypes changed in opposite directions during training: SOM-IN boutons dropped early while PV-IN boutons rose gradually. — “Somatostatin-expressing inhibitory neurons (SOM-INs) that mainly inhibit distal dendrites of excitatory neurons showed a decrease in axonal boutons immediately after the training begins, whereas parvalbumin-expressing inhibitory neurons (PV-INs) that mainly inhibit perisomatic regions of excitatory neurons exhibited a gradual increase in the axonal boutons during training.”
findingBoth increasing and decreasing SOM-IN activity optogenetically disrupted spine dynamics and impaired the acquisition of stereotyped movements, so learning depends on an intermediate level of SOM inhibition. — “Optogenetic enhancement and suppression of SOM-IN activity during training destabilized and hyper-stabilized spines, respectively, and both manipulations impaired the learning of stereotyped movements.”
findingInhibitory postsynaptic sites on the distal dendrites of excitatory neurons, imaged as Gephyrin-GFP puncta, also declined during learning, confirming that the SOM bouton loss translates into less dendritic inhibition of excitatory cells. — “Thus, inhibitory synapses on distal dendrites of excitatory neurons are reduced during motor learning.”
findingActivating SOM-INs did not block the formation of new spines but blocked their stabilization and additionally destabilized older spines. — “However, SOM-IN activation prevented the stabilization of these learning-related nascent spines and also destabilized some of the pre-existing spines”
findingSilencing SOM-INs left spine formation intact while nearly eliminating spine loss, showing spine turnover is bidirectionally sensitive to SOM inhibition. — “Thus, spine dynamics is highly sensitive to the level of SOM inhibition.”
findingThe bouton changes were specific to trained forelimb cortex: untrained animals and the hindlimb area showed no change. — “However, bouton density was stable in the forelimb area of untrained mice and in the hindlimb area during training”
findingBehaviourally, SOM-IN activation increased missed trials and prevented movement kinematics from becoming stereotyped. — “Furthermore, activation of SOM-INs blocked the formation of stereotyped movements, shown by the low trial-to-trial correlation of their movement kinematics throughout training”
findingboth raising and lowering SOM interneuron activity during training impaired learning of stereotyped movements — “Optogenetic enhancement and suppression of SOM-IN activity during training destabilized and hyper-stabilized spines, respectively, and both manipulations impaired the learning of stereotyped movements.”
findingtoo much or too little SOM inhibition is detrimental to spine reorganization and motor learning — “Our observations on the rapid loss of SOM-IN boutons and the bidirectional effects of SOM-IN inhibition on the spine stability observed in the optogenetic manipulations further demonstrate that spine stability on distal dendrites is exquisitely sensitive to the level of SOM-IN inhibition; too much or too little SOM inhibition is detrimental to spine reorganization and motor learning.”
findingspine formation rose in the distal apical branches in L1 during the first three sessions, followed by increased elimination of pre-existing spines — “In the distal branches of apical dendrites located in L1, spine formation was significantly increased during the first 3 sessions of training compared to untrained controls ( Fig. 2c-e ), followed by an increased elimination of spines that existed at the beginning of training”
finding75% of spines formed in the first three sessions survived to the end of the experiment — “75% (18 out of 24) of the spines that formed in the first 3 sessions of training were stable and remained until the end of the experiment.”
findingperisomatic spines stayed stable throughout learning — “In contrast to the reorganization of distal spines, spines on perisomatic dendrites (<75 μm from soma) in L2/3 were relatively stable during learning”
findingSOM interneuron bouton density fell during learning, and was stable in untrained mice and in the hindlimb area — “We found that motor learning led to a significant reduction in the density of SOM boutons ( Fig. 3d ). However, bouton density was stable in the forelimb area of untrained mice and in the hindlimb area during training”
findingPV interneuron boutons rose transiently instead — “In contrast to SOM-IN boutons, motor learning induced a transient increase in the PV-IN bouton density compared to untrained controls”
findinginhibitory synapses on distal dendrites, measured directly by Gephyrin puncta, decreased during learning — “By repeatedly imaging the same distal branches of apical dendrites in L1 ( Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 1f ), we found that GFP-positive puncta density decreased during learning, compared to untrained control ( Fig. 6c ). The time course and extent of Gephyrin-GFP dynamics mirrored those of SOM boutons. Thus, inhibitory synapses on distal dendrites of excitatory neurons are reduced during motor learning.”
findingSOM activation left spine formation intact but prevented the new spines from stabilising, abolishing the density increase — “When ChR2 was activated in SOM-INs during training, a similar increase in spine formation rate was observed compared to control mice expressing tdTomato instead of ChR2 (sessions 1–3, Fig. 7d-e ). However, SOM-IN activation prevented the stabilization of these learning-related nascent spines and also destabilized some of the pre-existing spines ( Fig. 7f-g ). As a result, the increase in spine density was abolished with SOM-IN activation”
findingSOM inactivation left formation normal but almost completely abolished elimination, raising spine density — “In this condition, learning-related spine formation happened normally ( Fig. 7d-e , Supplementary Fig. 6d ) but spine elimination was almost completely abolished ( Fig. 7d, f-g ). This resulted in an increased spine density that was maintained until the end of training”
findingSOM activation made mice miss more trials, take longer to reach reward, and fail to form stereotyped movements — “Indeed, mice that had SOM-INs activated during training missed more trials than control mice ( Fig. 8b ) and took a significantly longer time to achieve the threshold for a reward from the cue onset ( Fig. 8c ). Furthermore, activation of SOM-INs blocked the formation of stereotyped movements, shown by the low trial-to-trial correlation of their movement kinematics throughout training”
findingSOM inactivation impaired the same three measures — “Similarly, SOM-IN inactivation also impaired motor learning, shown by a lower fraction of successful trials, longer time to achieve a reward, and lower correlation of movement kinematics”
findingthe identical protocol applied to PV interneurons affected neither spines nor behaviour — “The observed effects of SOM-IN manipulation on spine dynamics and learning were in stark contrast to when PV-INs were activated using the same protocol, which neither affected spine dynamics nor behavior ( Supplementary Fig. 6b, d and e ), indicating that the impairment of learning-related spine dynamics and motor learning was specific to SOM-IN activation.”
findingonce the skill was acquired, SOM activation no longer affected performance — “Importantly, once the mice had acquired the skill, SOM-IN activation did not affect their performance ( Fig. 8e-f ). Thus, the effect of SOM-IN activation is specific to the acquisition of a new motor skill.”
findingmost spine changes occurred between two hours after training and the next day, while about half of SOM bouton changes occurred during or within two hours of the session — “We found that the vast majority of spine changes occurred between 2 hours after training and the next day ( Fig. 2g ), consistent with a recent study 26 . PV bouton changes also followed a similar trend ( Fig. 4e ). However, about 50% of the changes in SOM boutons occurred during or within 2 hours after behavioral sessions ( Fig. 3e ), indicating that training induces a rapid elimination of SOM boutons.”

findingSOM and PV interneurons together make up about two thirds of cortical inhibitory neurons — “SOM-INs mainly inhibit distal dendrites of excitatory neurons, while PV-INs mainly inhibit perisomatic regions ( Fig. 3a ) 12 , and SOM- and PV-INs together compose about two thirds of cortical inhibitory neurons”
