Introduction Mercuryterm·dom (Hgterm·dom) is a potent neurotoxic substance, since the organic form thereof, methylmercuryterm·dom (MeHgterm·dom), can result in neurocognitive abnormalities in kids and cardiovascular issues in adults ( 1 , 2 ). In the annals of history, MeHgterm·dom exposure has resulted in substantial health concerns to human beings. For example, 1,784 people died of the Minamata disease in Japan ( 3 ). More than 10,000 deaths from fatal heart attacksterm·dom (FHAterm·dom) are thought to occur each year in China as a result of exposure to MeHgterm·dom ( 4 ). It is calculated that MeHgterm·dom exposure costs the United States and Europe $16 billion in lost productivity due to the intelligence quotient (IQ) decline of developing brains ( 5 , 6 ). About 60% of the Hgterm·dom emitted to the atmosphere each year derives from anthropogenic Hgterm·dom that was previously deposited in soil and water ( 7 ). Just 10% of the current emissions come from natural sources, and the remaining 30% come from manmade sources ( 8 ). Human-related Hgterm·dom emissions play a sizable part in the global Hgterm·dom budget ( 9 ). Among the background of such context, the Minamata Conventionterm·key on Mercuryterm·dom was signed by 128 countries to establish a mandate for measures to combat global Hgterm·dom contamination ( 10 ), including reducing artisanal and small-scale gold miningterm·dom (ASGMterm·dom) as well as the purposeful use of Hgterm·dom in goods and industrial processes.

Through international tradeterm·dom, locations where commodities are consumed are separated from those where emissions and the resulting pollution and health effects occur ( 11 , 12 ). Although items from downstream industries don't contain Hgterm·dom, using these products can cause upstream Hgterm·dom emissions from the manufacture of raw and intermediate materials ( 13 , 14 ). Moreover, developed economies frequently transfer Hgterm·dom emissions to emerging countries in order to boost financial returns by utilizing cheap labor and energy as well as lax environmental regulations, which can be attributed to differences in division of labor in the global value chains ( 15 , 16 ). As such, there is debate among academic circles concerning whether existing production-side controls (for example, putting up Hgterm·dom removal facilities and adopting cleaner production techniques) can effectively reduce emissions, since the drivers (that is, consumption) behind such emissions are neglected ( 17 , 18 ). Thus, the role of international tradeterm·dom should be identified in the relocation of global Hgterm·dom emissions, pollution, exposure, and related health burden.

A series of processes, such as Hgterm·dom emission, atmospheric transport and deposition, environmental exchange, Hgterm·dom methylation, food web transfers, and dietary intake, affect MeHgterm·dom exposure to humans ( 19 ). The various phases of the Hgterm·dom cycle are interrelated and may have an impact on one another. Several specific segments of the chain have been evaluated in previous studies. For example, Hgterm·dom emissions are generated from production activities and induced by consumers through international tradeterm·dom ( 20 , 21 ). The physical transport of Hgterm·dom emissions from other countries can significantly affect domestic Hgterm·dom pollution ( 22 , 23 ). Hgterm·dom levels in environmental medias can influence the MeHgterm·dom levels in various foods including seafood, freshwater fish, and rice ( 24 , 25 ). MeHgterm·dom intake in one place may result from a variety of meals from distinct worldwide regions ( 26–28 ). Despite the aforementioned findings, the global biogeochemical Hgterm·dom cycle has not been identified along a longer path from economic production to human health.

Notably, the effects of subnational trade on Hgterm·dom emissions, transportation, deposition, food exposure, and related health impactsterm·key have been investigated in China ( 4 ). However, in such research, the atmospheric Hg depositionterm·dom was taken as the sole proxy to scale the change of food MeHgterm·dom levels without inclusion of marine plankton and soil Hgterm·dom levels which are more suitable proxy indicators for seafood and rice ( 29 ). At the same time, studies focusing on a sole country result in a failure to provide effective policy instruction for the implementation of the Minamata Conventionterm·key from a multinational perspective. The limitations in previous studies make it difficult to identify how international tradeterm·dom affects the Hg-related health risks and to propose practical policy solutions to mitigate those risks through cooperation on a global scale. In this context, a comprehensive investigation of the Hgterm·dom health impactsterm·key associated with global trade is urgently needed for promoting international cooperation on the Minamata Conventionterm·key ( 30 ).

Results and discussion Relocation of Hgterm·dom emissions of countries Globally, human activities release a total of 1,833.3 Mg of Hgterm·dom in 2011 ( 31 ), 47.1% (864.2 Mg) of which is embodied in international tradeterm·dom. In other words, nearly half of the global Hgterm·dom emissions can be linked to exports to other countries (see Fig. S1 for emissions embodied in bilateral trade). Figure 1 displays the net impact of international tradeterm·dom on Hgterm·dom emissions in 13 world regions (see Fig. S2 for regional definitions). The top net Hgterm·dom receptors are all less developed economies (red shading, Fig. 1 ), such as sub-Saharan Africa (+199.4 Mg), Latin America (+166.3 Mg), and China (+80.9 Mg). Such economies are found to be major sources of global Hgterm·dom emissions (Fig. S3 ), of which China is the world's largest emitter (546.1 Mg), followed by sub-Saharan Africa (291.8 Mg) and Latin America (291.3 Mg). From the view of sector, the smelting and pressing of nonferrous metals are the primary direct contributors to the Hgterm·dom emissions of the three regions, even accounting for over 90% in sub-Saharan Africa (Fig. S3 ). The aforementioned findings can primarily be attributed to the heavy use of Hgterm·dom by the ASGMterm·dom in such regions ( 35 ). The top net Hgterm·dom outsourcers are mainly developed economies (blue shading, Fig. 1 ), such as the United States (−145.2 Mg), Western Europe (−140.9 Mg), and Japan (−36.9 Mg). However, several developing economies such as the Middle East and North Africa (−112.7 Mg), India (−20.6 Mg), and Eastern Europe (−5.9 Mg) are also found with net negative emissions, which could be ascribed to their lower emissions thereof from ASGMterm·dom, thereby contributing to reducing production-based emissions (Fig. S3 ).

Developing economies, the main suppliers of primary and semimanufactured products (for example, metals and nonmetallic mineral products), are located upstream of the global supply chains. In contrast, industrialized economies have higher consumption-based emissions since they are further away from global supply chains (Fig. S3 ). These results demonstrate that developed economies can avoid significant amounts of Hgterm·dom emissions by transferring Hg-intensive production activities to emerging economies through international tradeterm·dom ( 18 ), thereby highlighting a necessity for international responsibility sharing on Hgterm·dom control from an environmental equity perspective.

Policy implications A map of the global health risks brought on by trade-induced Hgterm·dom emissions is identified from the more comprehensive biogeochemical Hgterm·dom cycle at the global scale. International tradeterm·dom considerably relocates the regional distribution of Hgterm·dom challenges across the world, which enables the whole world to avoid considerable Hg-related health risks, while also resulting in contrary changespersName in health loss between developed and developing economies. To expand further, some of the gains from Hgterm·dom emission reduction in developed economies are likely to be at the expense of the ecosystem and human health in developing economies, which is a critical inequity issue between developed and developing countries. However, the transfer of heavy industries (for example, smelting and pressing of metals) from sensitive areas (for example, the coast of developed countries) to less sensitive areas (for example, inland regions of less developed countries) could alleviate the global total MeHgterm·dom exposure. As such, a fair and effective responsibility sharing and compensation mechanism need to be established for trade-induced Hg-related health risks, so as to advance international cooperation on the implementation of the Minamata Conventionterm·key on Mercuryterm·dom. For instance, organizations such as the United Nations and the World Health Organization could establish an international compensation fund in which countries benefiting from reduction in Hgterm·dom emissions should compensate the countries suffering losses based on changespersName in economic lossterm·key resulting from international tradeterm·dom.

The understanding of Hg-related dangers embedded in global trade expands upon the present mitigation strategies for Hgterm·dom, which solely aim to reduce direct emissions, and thus offers opportunities for reducing indirect emissions. To mitigate Hg-related health risks in an effective way, targeted policy methods should be implemented at various stages of global supply chains. Production-side controls work well in key regions and sectors with huge direct Hgterm·dom emissions and significant health concerns. These controls include increasing energy efficiency, phasing out outdated factories, and building Hgterm·dom removal facilities. As an example, these steps are successful for sources like nonmetal mineral products, smelting and pressing of metals, and production and supply of electricity and heat power in sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America, and China. Demand-side control strategies work well in key regions and sectors whose final consumption is responsible for significant upstream health hazards. These strategies include adjusting consumption taxes to influence consumer behavior. For example, demand-side actions are more frequently thought about when products from the construction, general and special equipment, and electric equipment and machinery sectors are consumed. Regionally, developed economies, such as the United States, Western Europe, Middle East, and North Africa, should prioritize implementation of these demand-side control measures.
