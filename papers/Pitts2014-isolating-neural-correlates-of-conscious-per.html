mechanismRequiring a trial-by-trial report makes aware trials recruit working-memory maintenance and higher-level access for decision and response, so aware-minus-unaware contrasts pick up those post-perceptual operations rather than the conscious percept itself. — “On aware trials, subjective reports rely on the maintenance of perceptual information in working memory and access of this information by higher-level cognitive systems that enable decision-making and response planning/execution.”
mechanismLate components track the extra operations demanded by the task rather than the percept, which is why their amplitudes peak when the stimulus is both seen and task-relevant. — “Instead, these later components are likely to reflect post-perceptual or attention-based processes necessary for completing the task, as their amplitudes were by far the largest in the aware/task-relevant condition.”
mechanismSevere masking lowers bottom-up stimulus strength so that an unseen stimulus reaches pre-conscious processing only if top-down attention is added, explaining why the CIN survived inattentional blindness in the earlier study but not masking here. — “In other words, with such severe masking and the resulting reduction of bottom-up stimulus strength, an unseen stimulus might only be processed pre-consciously if attended.”
mechanismTrial-to-trial jitter in the moment of noticing an irrelevant stimulus smears the averaged waveform, shrinking and stretching the VAN in task-irrelevant blocks without any change in whether the stimulus was seen. — “Another possibility is that the timing of perceptual awareness is more consistent across trials when the stimulus is relevant to the task; whereas, on task-irrelevant trials subjects may notice the irrelevant stimuli on every trial but at slightly different times on different trials because their primary task is to determine if a potential target is present.”
mechanismConscious awareness may arise from an interaction between object-based attention and high-level perceptual representations, with the VAN indexing that interaction rather than awareness per se. — “A final possibility worth consideration is whether a particular type of interaction between attention and perceptual representation is the underlying neural mechanism of conscious awareness, and the VAN is a marker of this type of interaction.”
mechanismon aware trials the subject maintains the percept in working memory and accesses it for a decision; on unaware trials there is nothing to maintain, so the two trial types differ in post-perceptual processing as well as in awareness — “On aware trials, subjective reports rely on the maintenance of perceptual information in working memory and access of this information by higher-level cognitive systems that enable decision-making and response planning/execution. Introduction

Determining the neural basis of consciousness is one of the most challenging problems in modern cognitive neuroscience. Much progress has been made over the past 25 years by simplifying the problem and focusing first on identifying neural correlates of conscious perceptionterm or “NCCs” (Baars, 1989 ; Logothetis and Schall, 1989 ; Crick and Koch, 1990 , 2003 ). The primary strategy has been to compare brain activity elicited by physically identical stimuli of which subjects are aware vs. unaware. While appealing in its simplicity, this approach has recently been criticized for being too inclusive in what counts as an NCCterm (Aru et al., 2012 ; de Graaf et al., 2012 ). Depending on how awarenessterm is manipulated, neural mechanisms that are necessary-but-not-sufficient or sufficient-but-not-necessary for conscious perceptionterm have often been misinterpreted as true-NCCs. Aru et al. ( 2012 ) refer to such neural processes as “pre-requisites” and “consequences” of conscious perceptionterm, respectively, and have encouraged researchers to develop new paradigms to help distinguish the “NCC-proper” from these related, yet functionally distinct, processes.

Terminology and theoretical considerations

While the central tenets of Aru et al.'s ( 2012 ) proposal are well-justified, we prefer to use the terms “pre-consciousterm” and “post-perceptualterm” instead of “pre-requisite” and “consequence.” In our view, the term pre-requisite is too general and the term consequence too restrictive. For example, retinal processing could be considered a pre-requisite of visual awarenessterm (with the exception of TMS-induced phosphenes); however, no one has proposed retinal activity as a potential NCCterm. The term pre-consciousterm , instead, is used here to refer only to cortical and cortico-thalamic activity following the initial feedforward activation of primary sensory cortex. Pre-consciousterm processing immediately precedes (both temporally and functionally) conscious processing and is capable of establishing elaborate perceptual representations that may become conscious (Dehaene and Naccache, 2001 ; Dehaene et al., 2006 ). The term consequence, on the other hand, appears to be used by Aru and colleagues to refer to certain neural events that necessarily follow the conscious awarenessterm of a stimulusterm. In contrast, the term post-perceptualterm leaves open the possibility that conscious perceptionterm may occur with or without these subsequent processing events. As outlined below, simple manipulations of the task can eliminate post-perceptualterm processing while leaving conscious perceptionterm intact.

It should also be noted that the term “perceptual awarenessterm·def” (often abbreviated as “awarenessterm”) will be used here to describe situations in which perceptual content is reportable, although actual reporting may not take place, e.g., because the subjectterm has been instructed to only reportterm·mth awarenessterm of certain types of stimuli. This reportability requirement is commonly used to operationally define conscious perceptionterm but differs from certain theoretical frameworks that consider some types of perceptual processing as “phenomenally conscious” even if the subjectterm is unable to reportterm·mth anything about these percepts when specifically instructed to do so (Lamme, 2006 ; Vandenbroucke et al., 2014 ). It also differs from notions of phenomenal consciousness in which perceptual content is accessible, yet not necessarily accessed (Block, 2007 , 2011 ). In both cases, we consider such processing as “pre-consciousterm” rather than a special type of unreportable consciousness, and our main goal is to isolate neural correlates of “accessterm consciousness” from correlates of pre-consciousterm and post-perceptualterm processing (Dehaene and Changeux, 2004 , 2011 ; Block, 2005 ; Dehaene et al., 2006 ). Because the term “accessterm” can have different meanings in different contexts, we will qualify its usage for clarity, e.g., “conscious accessterm” typically refers to global availability of perceptual information for flexible use by a variety of cognitive systems, while “access of perceptual information for reportterm·def” refers to a much narrower set of post-perceptualterm operations involved in task-related memory and decision-making processes.

While it is important to separate both pre-consciousterm and post-perceptualterm activity from correlates of conscious perceptionterm, the current study focuses mainly on post-perceptualterm processing. To illustrate the problem of conflating neural correlates of post-perceptualterm processing with correlates of conscious perceptionterm, it is useful to consider “the refrigerator door problemterm·def” (a neural counterpart to the “refrigerator light illusion” described by Block, 2001 ). Imagine you have no accessterm to the internal machinery of a refrigerator and your goal is to determine under what conditions the light inside the refrigerator turns on. You might start by opening the door to check and proceed to try just opening it a crack or just for a brief moment. However, every time you open the door the light is always on and you can't be sure the light would have been on if you had kept the door closed. In consciousness research, one of the goals is to determine whether unique brain signal X correlates with conscious perceptionterm (the light turning on), but to do so requires a perceptual reportterm·mth from the subjectterm (opening the door). The problem is that it is often difficult to determine whether the same brain signal X would have occurred if the subjectterm had not accessed this information for reportterm·mth (kept the door closed).

To manipulate visual awarenessterm in the lab, the most commonly used paradigms include backward maskingterm·mth, the attentional blink, change blindness, binocular rivalry, and signal detectionterm at thresholdterm (e.g., Pins and ffytche, 2003 ; Fernandez-Duque et al., 2003 ; Sergent et al., 2005 ; Koivisto et al., 2006 ; PittspersName et al., 2010 ). This modified inattentional blindnessterm·mth paradigmterm·mth shows promise in helping to distinguish pre-consciousterm and post-perceptualterm activity from the NCC-proper. Future fMRI and intra-cranial EEG studies should consider adopting similar methods. However, one drawback with the inattention paradigmterm·mth is that it only includes 3 of the possible 4 combinations of visual awarenessterm and task-relevanceterm; i.e., the unaware, task-relevant condition, which is common in other awarenessterm paradigms, is absent in the inattentional blindnessterm·mth paradigmterm·mth. This missing condition, in which a stimulusterm is attended and relevant to the task but not consciously perceived, may be important for separating neural correlates of awarenessterm from neural correlates of attentionterm. It is also advantageous to cross-validate results by employing more than one type of experimental paradigmterm·mth, and the above mentioned results have only been obtained so far using the inattentional blindnessterm·mth approach. The current study was designed to address these outstanding issues.

A 2 × 2 manipulation of visual awarenessterm and task-relevanceterm in a maskingterm·mth paradigmterm·mth

One option for measuring NCCs using the maskingterm·mth paradigmterm·mth is to present stimuli for a brief duration followed by a mask such that the stimulusterm is consciously perceived on approximately 50% of trials (Koivisto et al., 2008 ). This approach allows comparisons of brain activity on aware vs. unaware trials while the physical stimuli (stimulusterm + mask) remain identical. Of course, with this approach all stimuli must be task-relevant because the experimenter has no other means of sorting individual trials into aware and unaware conditions. A different approach is to employ two different maskingterm·mth latencies (i.e., stimulusterm durations), one that is very short leading to 0% awarenessterm and another that is considerably longer resulting in 100% awarenessterm (Koivisto and Revonsuo, 2008 ). In this variation of the maskingterm·mth paradigmterm·mth, task-relevanceterm can be manipulated, and trial-by-trial reporting is unnecessary. However, the use of two different mask-onset latencies introduces another problem, namely that brain activity is likely to differ due to physical stimulusterm differences as opposed to a difference in awarenessterm (Bachmann, 2009 ). For example, ERPs elicited by a mask presented 16 ms after a stimulusterm will be superimposed with ERPs elicited by the stimulusterm, whereas a mask presented 300 ms after a stimulusterm will only affect the stimulus-elicited ERPs at latencies beyond ~350 ms. To control for this confoundterm, a control stimulusterm can be presented and masked at each of the two latencies, and the ERPs elicited by the control stimuli can be subtracted from the ERPs elicited by the stimuli of interest prior to making any aware vs. unaware contrasts. The subtraction essentially removes the mask-elicited ERPterm that is superimposed with the stimulus-elicited ERPterm. This was the approach used in the current study, which compared stimuli of 16 ms (unaware) vs. 300 ms (aware) duration, each of which was either task-relevant or task-irrelevant on separate blocks of trials. The overall design was similar to a previous study that compared ERPs elicited by masked vs. unmasked letters while spatial and non-spatial attentionterm were manipulated (Koivisto and Revonsuo, 2007 ).

Here, the stimuli were contour shapes, colored lines, and control stimuli consisting of a random array of lines (Figure 3A ). We first performed a behavioral detectionterm study in which these stimuli were masked at 5 different latencies (i.e., stimulusterm durations of 16, 33, 50, 67, and 300 ms). Based on the results from this experiment, two maskingterm·mth latencies (16 and 300 ms) were selected for the EEG experiment because these mask-onsets showed close to 0 and 100% detectionterm rates, respectively, for both shapeterm and colorterm stimuli. In the EEG experiment, on separate blocks of trials, shapes or colored-lines were deemed task-relevant, and the shapeterm, colorterm, and control stimuli were masked at each of the two latencies. This design allowed comparisons of ERPterm difference waves (shapeterm minus control, colorterm minus control) across 4 types of trials: aware, task-relevant; aware, task-irrelevant; unaware, task-relevant; unaware, task-irrelevant.

Methods

Participants

Twenty-six healthy adults participated in the EEG experiment. All were recruited as volunteers and gave informed consent prior to the beginning of the experiment. Data from eight participants were later excluded; three due to excessive EEG artifact, and five due to awarenessterm of some of the 16 ms colorterm stimuli, as assessed by a behavioral post-test (see below). The final group consisted of 18 participants (mean age: 21 years old; 13 female). An initial behavioral experiment was conducted to determine the maskingterm·mth SOAs used in the EEG experiment. Twelve (different) subjects participated in this behavioral study; data from one subjectterm was excluded from analyses due to failure to follow task instructions. All experimental procedures were approved by the Reed College institutional review board in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Stimuli and procedure

Stimuli consisted of 20 × 20 arrays (visual angle = 6.2 × 6.2°) of oriented white line segments (0.34°), identical to those used in PittspersName et al. ( 2012 , 2014 ). In the inter-stimulus intervals (ISIs) the lines were visible and in randomized orientations. Upon each stimulusterm presentation all of the lines rotated to new positions for the specified duration. For control stimuli, the line elements shifted to new random orientations. Two types of shapeterm stimuli were created by arranging the new orientations of subsets of line segments to form a 10 × 10 square or a 7 × 7 diamond. Two types of colorterm stimuli were created by changing either 3 or 4 of the randomly oriented line segments from white to red (RGB: 255,0,0). The spatial locations of the red lines were randomized but constrained to overlap with the locations of the shapeterm contours to control for spatial attentionterm across the shapeterm and colorterm tasks (see procedure below). Across trials, the positions of the red lines varied but were restricted to occur in upper, lower, left, and right regions with no two red lines being located in the same region. Example stimuli are shown in Figure 3A . For both the behavioral experiment and the EEG experiment, the line element arrays were always visible; no blank inter-stimulus intervals (ISIs) were used. Each stimulusterm presentation was followed by a return to a new pattern of randomly oriented line segments, which served as the maskingterm·mth/ISI stimuli; each ISI lasted a random interval between 1000 and 1200 ms. A central fixation cross (0.5°) was present at all times. All stimuli were presented on a dark background (0.07 cd/m2) on an LCD monitor (refresh rate = 60 Hz). Stimuli were created and displayed using Presentation software (Neurobehavioral Systems, Albany, CA).

In the behavioral experiment (Figure 3B ), three different stimuli were presented: square shapes, 4-red-lines, and random (control) arrays. The diamond and 3-red-line stimuli were not presented because these stimuli would eventually serve as targets in the EEG experiment, while the ERPs of interest would be for the non-target stimuli (squares, 4-red-lines). Each stimulusterm was followed by a maskingterm·mth (ISI) stimulusterm at 5 equiprobable stimulusterm onset asynchronies (SOA): 16.67, 33.34, 50, 66.67, and 300 ms. Thus, there were 15 combinations of stimulus-type and mask SOA, and each combination was presented 20 times. The subjectterm's task was to press a response button with their right index finger whenever they perceived either colorterm or shapeterm. Subjects were encouraged to adopt a liberal response criterion, responding even if they just caught a glimpse of colorterm or shapeterm, while avoiding guessing. The goal of this experiment was to determine a maskingterm·mth SOA that would render both colorterm and shapeterm invisible for all subjects on all trials. It was also important to verify that the 300 ms stimuli could be detected on 100% of trials.

Based on the results from the behavioral experiment, two different maskingterm·mth SOAs were employed in the EEG experiment (16.67 and 300 ms), resulting in 10 different stimulus-mask combinations: (2 types of shapeterm stimuli + 2 types of colorterm stimuli + 1 random control stimulusterm) × (2 mask SOAs) = 10 combinations. During each block of trials, these 10 trial types were intermixed and presented in random order, each at 10% probability. On separate blocks of trials, subjects performed either a colorterm or a shapeterm task. In the colorterm task, the target stimuli were 3-red-lines and in the shapeterm task, the diamond stimuli served as targets. Subjects pressed a response button with their right index finger upon target detectionterm. In order to control for between-condition ERPterm differences associated with motor preparation and execution, all target trials (and any trials in which subjects responded) were excluded from ERPterm analyses. Each task was performed for 900 trials (~15 min) before switching to the other task for 900 trials, and this sequence was then repeated (task-order was counterbalanced across subjects). For example, half of the subjects performed the colorterm task for 15 min, switched to the shapeterm task for 15 min, switched back to the colorterm task for 15 min, and finished with 15 min of the shapeterm task. Thus, 1800 total trials were completed for each task, 180 of each stimulusterm type. Figure 3C shows a summary of the EEG experiment design, and a video example of the stimulusterm sequence is provided in Movie 1 .

Because the behavioral experiment revealed detectionterm rates greater than zero for the 16.67 ms colorterm stimuli in some subjects, an additional behavioral post-test was conducted after each EEG session. In this post-test, colorterm stimuli (4-red-lines), shapeterm stimuli (squares), and control stimuli (random arrays) were presented and masked at 16.67 or 300 ms. A total of 180 stimuli were presented (across three 1 min blocks), 30 trials of each stimulus-mask combination. As in the behavioral study, subjects were instructed to press a button whenever they detected either colorterm or shapeterm (employing a liberal response bias). Five of the 26 participants detected 16.67 ms colorterm stimuli on at least one trial during this post-test and were excluded from ERPterm analyses. No subjects detected any of the 16.67 ms shapeterm stimuli, nor were there any “false alarm” responses to the randomized control stimuli. Active experiment time for the EEG study was ~60 min, short rest breaks were provided after every 60 trials (~1 min), and longer breaks were given after every 300 trials (~5 min). Each experimental session lasted 3.5–4.5 h including EEG cap preparation, practice trials, rest breaks, and the behavioral post-test.

EEG recording and ERPterm pre-processing

EEG was noninvasively recorded from the scalp via Ag/AgCl electrodes sewn into customized caps with 96 electrode placements (EASYCAP, Herrsching, Germany). Electrode locations were modified from the standard 10–20 system to allow equidistant spacing (electrode positions reported here refer to the nearest channels of the international 10–20 system). Electrode impedances were kept below 5 kΩ. Signals were digitized (at 500 Hz) and amplified by three 32 channel amplifiers (Brain Amp Standard, Brain Products, Gilching, Germany). Eye movements and blinks were monitored by left and right horizontal EOG channels and a vertical EOG channel under the left eye, respectively. An electrode positioned at CPz served as the reference during recording.

ERPs were time-locked to the line segment orientation changes, low-pass filtered at 25 Hz (24dB/Oct), re-referenced to the average of the left and right mastoids, and baseline corrected from −100 to 0 ms. The left and right horizontal EOG channels were re-referenced as a bipolar pair. Trials were discarded if they contained eye movements, blinks, or other muscle artifacts in a −600 to +600 ms interval surrounding stimulus-onset. Artifact detectionterm was accomplished semi-automatically via per-subject adjustment of the following peak-to-peak thresholds: eye movements (50 μV, 50 ms steps, in bipolar HEOG), blinks (100 μV, 200 ms steps, in VEOG and FP1), and muscle noise (150 μV, 200 ms steps, all remaining channels). On average, 16% of trials were rejected due to a combination of these artifacts. Individual electrodes showing extended periods of noise in the raw EEG were removed and replaced by interpolated signals from surrounding channels using topographic spherical splines (channels included in ANOVAs were not interpolated).

ERPterm analyses

Our strategy for ERPterm analyses was to first identify time windows and electrodes of interest in the grand-averaged difference waves of all conditions averaged together, using our previous results as a guide (PittspersName et al., 2012 ). Peak latencies were identified and mean amplitudes were assessed in ± 20 ms time windows around these peaks (± 50 ms for the broad P3bterm). The electrode showing the maximal signal, along with 5–8 adjacent sites (according to each component's scalp topography), were selected for analysis. For the shapeterm stimulusterm difference waves (shapeterm minus random), four distinct components were evident, each of which closely matched our previous results in terms of timing and scalp distribution: contour integration negativity, “CINterm” (160–200 ms), visual awarenessterm negativity, “VANterm” (200–240 ms), late occipital positivity, “LOP” (310–350 ms), and the “P3bterm” (380–480 ms). For the colorterm stimulusterm difference waves (colorterm minus random), four components were also evident, some of which showed reduced latencies compared to the corresponding shape-elicited components. The first color-elicited component showed a very similar time course and scalp topography to a previously reported colorterm vs. non-color ERPterm difference (Schoenfeld et al., 2003 ). This component, which consisted of a midline ventral-posterior negativity accompanied by a vertex positivity from 130 to 170 ms, has previously been referred to as the “sensory effect of colorterm” which we abbreviate here as “SEC” (Zinni et al., 2014 ). The SEC (130–170 ms) was followed by the VANterm (200–240 ms), LOP (290–330 ms), and P3bterm (380–480 ms) components, each of which showed similar scalp topographies to their shape-elicited counterparts.

Statistical analyses began with 2 × 2 ANOVAs with the factors awarenessterm (16 or 300 ms durations) and task-relevanceterm (attended or unattended) for each difference wave component using the time windows specified above. Difference wave amplitudes were averaged across electrode clusters according to the scalp topographies of each component as follows: CINterm [ PO4, P6, O2, PO8, O10, PO10 ]; SEC [ FC1, FCZ, FC2, C1, CZ, C2, CPZ ]; VANterm [ PO4, P6, O2, PO8, O10, PO10 ]; LOP [ P1, PZ, P2, PO3, POZ, PO4, O1, OZ, O2 ]; P3bterm [ CP1, CPZ, CP2, P1, PZ, P2, POZ ].

All main effects and interactions were then further explored by conducting cluster mass permutation tests on the difference amplitudes (shapeterm minus random; colorterm minus random) for each of the 4 conditions separately ( aware, task-relevant ; aware, task-irrelevant ; unaware, task-relevant ; unaware, task-irrelevant ). To increase statistical power, separate tests were carried out for the early (100–300 ms) and late (300–600 ms) time windows and permutation analyses were restricted to 63 of the 96 electrodes (covering central, parietal, temporal, and occipital regions) based on a priori hypotheses regarding scalp topographies of each component. Thus, 6363 comparisons were made for the early time window and 9450 for the late time window. In all cases, two-tailed cluster mass permutation teststerm·stat (Bullmore et al., 1999 ), with a family-wise alpha level of 0.05, were conducted using the original data and 2500 random within-subject permutations of the data. Electrodes within approximately 3.02 cm of one another were considered spatial neighbors. All cluster mass permutation analyses were carried out using the mass univariate ERPterm Toolbox (Groppe et al., 2011 ).

Discussion

In agreement with previous results using the inattentional blindnessterm·mth paradigmterm·mth (PittspersName et al., 2012 , 2014 ), the present study used a 2 × 2 manipulation of awarenessterm and task-relevanceterm in a backward maskingterm·mth task and found a consistent ERPterm correlate of conscious visual perception, the visual awarenessterm negativity (VANterm) component. The VANterm is a mid-latency occipital-parietal negativity (measured here from 200 to 240 ms) that has been targeted by a number of previous studies using various awarenessterm manipulations (Koivisto and Revonsuo, 2003 , 2008 , 2010 ; Ojanen et al., 2003 ; Wilenius-Emet et al., 2004 ; Koivisto et al., 2005 ; Wilenius and Revonsuo, 2007 ; Railo et al., 2011 ; Sandberg et al., 2013 ). ERPterm components preceding the VANterm are likely to reflect pre-consciousterm processing, considering that a previous study using identical stimuli (PittspersName et al., 2012 ) found evidence for the elicitation of such components in unaware (and unmasked) conditions. In the current study, components prior to the VANterm were absent in the unaware conditions, suggesting interference of pre-consciousterm processing by the maskingterm·mth stimulusterm (presented at 16 ms SOA). Components subsequent to the VANterm have often been proposed as potential NCCs, including most notably the P3bterm component (Sergent et al., 2005 ; Babiloni et al., 2006 ; Del Cul et al., 2007 ; Lamy et al., 2009 ; Dehaene and Changeux, 2011 ; Batterink et al., 2012 ; Kouider et al., 2013 ). The current study, however, provides evidence against this view, as the P3bterm along with another component subsequent to the VANterm (the LOP) did not consistently correlate with awarenessterm. Instead, these later components are likely to reflect post-perceptualterm or attention-based processes necessary for completing the task, as their amplitudes were by far the largest in the aware/task-relevant condition. Moreover the P3bterm was present in some of the unaware, task-relevant conditions, and absent in some of the aware, task-irrelevant conditions.

Correlates of visual awarenessterm vs. post-perceptualterm processing

It is difficult to create controlled conditions in which subjects are aware vs. unaware of physically identical stimuli without requiring an immediate trial-by-trial perceptual reportterm·mth. Nevertheless, it is important to devise new methods for allowing such contrasts because the neural correlates of conscious perceptionterm are easily confusable with the neural correlates of post-perceptualterm maintenance and accessterm for immediate subjective reportterm·mth. In both task-relevant and task-irrelevant situations (in which the stimuli are readily visible), subjects are considered to be conscious of the perceptual information because whenever they are asked to reportterm·mth what they see, they are able to easily do so. It follows that brain activity associated with conscious perceptionterm should be present in both cases. However, when perceptual information is accessed for immediate reportterm·mth, a variety of additional neural processes are likely to ensue, each of which is related to “doing something extra” with the perceptual information the subjectterm is already conscious of. Importantly, our manipulation of task-relevanceterm was designed to test a particular post-perceptualterm process: access of perceptual information for reportterm·def . This should not be confused with the more general concept of conscious accessterm (Dehaene and Changeux, 2004 ; Block, 2005 ), which we assume was present in our aware (300 ms stimulusterm) task- irrelevant conditions. In other words, we expect that on any given trial, if the subjects had been asked, they would have easily been able to reportterm·mth seeing the 300 ms task-irrelevant stimuli (this was indirectly verified by our behavioral experiment and behavioral post-test), thus perceptual information was globally available but the subjects did not have to use this information to complete the primary task-at-hand.

In our previous inattentional blindnessterm·mth experiments (PittspersName et al., 2012 , 2014 ; Shafto and PittspersName, 2013 ), we attempted to deal with this issue by rendering a stimulusterm easily visible (if expected) but irrelevant to the task-at-hand and delaying the perceptual reportterm·mth until after a full block of trials. In this situation, in which subjects were fully aware of a stimulusterm but did not have to do anything with the perceptual information because it was irrelevant to the task, we found that only early sensory (CINterm) and mid-latency (VANterm) ERPterm components were elicited. Subsequent components such as the P3bterm were only evident when the stimuli became relevant to the task. In the current experiment, instead of diverting attentionterm to create an unaware condition, we severely masked some stimuli (16 ms maskingterm·mth SOA) and compared ERPs to conditions in which the same stimuli were clearly visible (300 ms maskingterm·mth SOA). Importantly, in order to assess post-perceptualterm processing we manipulated the task such that stimuli were relevant to the task during some blocks of trials and irrelevant during other blocks. We found a similar pattern of results as in the inattentional blindnessterm·mth studies, in that the VANterm was consistently associated with perceptual awarenessterm·def, while subsequent components were either absent when the stimuli were irrelevant to the task or present when subjects were unaware of task-relevant stimuli.

Despite the main pattern of results, it remains possible that a small P3bterm was present (but below the statistical thresholdterm) during aware, task-irrelevant shapeterm trials (both in the current study as well as in PittspersName et al., 2012 and PittspersName et al., 2014 ). In this case, one might argue that the P3bterm is a correlate of conscious perceptionterm but was very weak during the task-irrelevant conditions because subjects were so focused on the relevant stimuli that they only caught a glimpse of the irrelevant stimuli on a subset of trials. Future studies might address this by incorporating infrequent, unexpected, probe trials in which the experiment is interrupted after a task-irrelevant stimulusterm and subjects are asked to “reportterm·mth what you just saw.” If subjects can always reportterm·mth awarenessterm of task-irrelevant stimuli, but the P3bterm is still absent, a stronger case could be made for a lack of a relationship between the P3bterm and awarenessterm; however, one must be careful to avoid inadvertently rendering the task-irrelevant stimuli relevant by including too many “surprise” reportterm·mth trials.

Attentionterm in the absence of awarenessterm?

An unexpected finding in the current study was evidence for a P3bterm elicited in the absence of reported awarenessterm. This effect appeared in the unaware, task-relevant condition for colorterm stimuli. It was absent for the equivalent condition for shapeterm stimuli and there was no evidence for a P3bterm in any of the unaware, task-irrelevant conditions. While very few studies have reported a P3bterm in the absence of conscious perceptionterm, it is generally accepted that attentionterm and awarenessterm are independent (yet often interact), and there is growing evidence for attentional modulations in the absence of awarenessterm (Bernat et al., 2001 ; Koch and Tsuchiya, 2007 ; Wyart and Tallon-Baudry, 2008 ; Tsuchiya and Koch, 2009 ; Bachmann, 2011 ; Tallon-Baudry, 2011 ; Marchetti, 2012 ; Aru and Bachmann, 2013 ). We previously posited that the P3bterm is post-perceptualterm and argued that while the P3bterm does not appear to be necessary for conscious perceptionterm, it may be a sufficient marker; i.e., whenever a P3bterm is observed one can be confident that the subjectterm was aware of the stimulusterm (PittspersName et al., 2012 , 2014 ). The current result, however, suggests that the P3bterm might not be necessary or sufficient, as it was elicited by colorterm stimuli of which the subjectterm was presumably unaware. Interestingly, the P3bterm was completely absent for task-irrelevant colorterm stimuli of which the subjectterm was also unaware, thus suggesting a task-based attentional modulation of perceptually undetected stimuli. This result is consistent with a recent study that showed an N2pc component for colorterm singletons that captured attentionterm even when the subjectterm was unaware of the stimuli, but only when colorterm was relevant to the search task (Ansorge et al., 2010 ), as well as an earlier study that found evidence of attentionterm capture (N2pc) in the absence of awarenessterm due to object substitution maskingterm·mth (Woodman and Luck, 2003 ). Similarly, recent studies have provided evidence for working memoryterm related processing in the absence of awarenessterm (Hassin et al., 2009 ; Gilchrist and Cowan, 2010 ; Soto et al., 2011 ).

An alternative explanation for this finding is that some subjects on some trials may have been partially aware of the 16 ms colorterm stimuli even though their reports indicated lack of awarenessterm. In these partial awarenessterm situations, subjects may have attempted to perform the discrimination task (to determine if the stimulusterm contained 3 or 4 red lines) but could only detect the presence of colorterm without being able to discriminate between target and non-target colorterm stimuli (see Windey et al., 2014 for a recent review of graded vs. dichotomous awarenessterm). While we conducted an initial behavioral study to determine an appropriate maskingterm·mth SOA and administered a behavioral post-test to each of the EEG subjects in which a simple detectionterm task replaced the discrimination task, it is still possible that some residual awarenessterm for a number of colorterm stimuli occurred during the EEG portion of the study. Indeed, we excluded five out of the original 26 participants because they were able to detect at least one of the 16 ms colorterm stimuli during the behavioral post-test. Because one of the goals of the current design was to avoid trial-by-trial reports, we intentionally did not acquire the data necessary to fully evaluate this alternative interpretation. Future studies should consider following up on this preliminary result in order to determine whether a P3bterm can be elicited during attentive, but unaware conditions, perhaps by employing a detectionterm task with low response criterion during EEG recording (Squires et al., 1973 , 1975 ).

Pre-consciousterm processing

In contrast to our previous results using the inattentional blindnessterm·mth paradigmterm·mth (PittspersName et al., 2012 ), the current study provided no evidence for an early pre-consciousterm ERPterm difference between shapeterm and random stimuli in the unaware conditions. One of the major findings in our previous study was that a component we labeled as Nd1 (for negative difference 1, here referred to as the CINterm), distributed over the occipital midline from ~160–200 ms, was elicited in all conditions, even when subjects were unaware of (inattentionally blind to) the shapeterm patterns. Why was this component absent from the unaware conditions in the current study? The most likely possibility is due to the differences in bottom-up stimulusterm strength between the two studies, a factor that is known to influence whether a stimulusterm will be processed non-consciously, pre-consciously, or consciously (Dehaene et al., 2006 ). In our previous inattentional blindnessterm·mth experiment, the shapeterm stimuli were always presented for 300 ms in duration (including the unaware condition), whereas the current study employed heavily masked stimuli (16 ms durations) to create the unaware conditions. In line with the interactions between top-down attentionterm and bottom-up stimulusterm strength described by Dehaene et al. ( 2006 ), the current results showed a small (just below statistical thresholdterm) CINterm in the unaware, task-relevant condition. In other words, with such severe maskingterm·mth and the resulting reduction of bottom-up stimulusterm strength, an unseen stimulusterm might only be processed pre-consciously if attended. In contrast, in our previous study top-down attentionterm was not required because sufficient bottom-up stimulusterm strength allowed this same stimulusterm to be processed pre-consciously.

One of the advantages of the current experimental design was a degree of internal replication made possible by the 2 × 2 crossing of the colorterm and shapeterm stimuli/tasks. This allows one to ask whether the same pattern of results seen for the shapeterm stimuli was also observed for the colorterm stimuli. An early sensory effect of colorterm (SEC) was not observed for either of the unaware conditions, although a trend in this direction was evident (see Figure 9 , left column, third row). To further explore how pre-consciousterm processing is influenced by top-down attentionterm and bottom-up stimulusterm strength, future studies could present extended duration colorterm stimuli during inattentional blindnessterm·mth or present backward-masked colorterm stimuli at various maskingterm·mth SOAs while manipulating task-relevanceterm.

VANterm: attentionterm or awarenessterm?

A main focus of recent consciousness research has been the relationship between attentionterm and awarenessterm (Tsuchiya and vanterm Boxtel, 2013 ). Historically, attentionterm and consciousness were often treated as similar if not identical concepts; nowadays, however, many researchers are proposing that each refers to a separate category of neural and psychological processes. Although attentionterm and awarenessterm may be functionally distinct, the question of whether each can exist independently of the other remains a topic of debate (Cohen et al., 2011 , 2012 ; Tsuchiya et al., 2012 ; Aru and Bachmann, 2013 ). One view posits that attentionterm can operate in the absence of awarenessterm, and awarenessterm can occur in the absence of attentionterm, whereas an opposing view argues that while attentionterm can influence processing of stimuli of which the subjectterm is unaware, there is no such thing as awarenessterm in the absence of attentionterm; i.e., attentionterm is necessary for conscious perceptionterm. Results from experiments employing the inattentional blindnessterm·mth paradigmterm·mth offer strong support for the latter view (Cohen et al., 2011 ; Mack and Clarke, 2012 ; PittspersName et al., 2012 ).

The present results, along with our previous inattentional blindnessterm·mth results, suggest that the most viable candidate for an ERPterm correlate of awarenessterm is the VANterm (Nd2) component. In our previous study (PittspersName et al., 2012 ), a well-known attention-related component, the SNterm (Harter and Aine, 1984 ; HillyardpersName and Anllo-Vento, 1998 ), was evident immediately after the VANterm, but was only observed in conditions where the stimulusterm was task-relevant. Distinguishing between the VANterm and the SNterm in a data-driven manner is not easy, because both components consist of a mid-latency (~200–300 ms), posterior, bi-lateral negativity. To further complicate matters, both the VANterm and the SNterm show variable (and overlapping) latencies depending on the stimuli and task. The only way to isolate the VANterm from the SNterm component may be to manipulate task-relevanceterm; in our previous study both components were present in task-relevant situations, whereas only the VANterm was present in aware but task-irrelevant conditions (PittspersName et al., 2012 ). In the present study, however, no clear SNterm was evident in the aware, task-relevant condition, perhaps because the stimuli used here were so easily discriminable that the processing resources indexed by the SNterm did not have to be engaged. Alternatively, it is possible that the VANterm measured in the current study overlapped the SNterm in the task-relevant condition such that the two components were indistinguishable. It will be important for future studies to distinguish between the VANterm and the SNterm, especially in situations in which the stimuli are task-relevant.

In the current study as well as in PittspersName et al. ( 2012 ), the amplitudeterm of the VANterm was larger in the task-relevant compared to the task-irrelevant condition. If the VANterm indeed reflects perceptual awarenessterm·def, which is often assumed to be an all-or-none phenomenon (you see the stimulusterm or you don't), why might its amplitudeterm vary according to the task? One possibility might be that subjects were not aware of the task-irrelevant 300 ms stimuli on every single trial because their attentionterm was focused on a separate task (i.e., partial inattentional blindnessterm·mth). This seems unlikely, especially for the colorterm stimuli (see Movie 1 ); in a separate experiment (PittspersName et al., 2014 ) we found a 0% inattentional blindnessterm·mth rate for similar 300 ms colorterm stimuli. Another possibility is that the timing of perceptual awarenessterm·def is more consistent across trials when the stimulusterm is relevant to the task; whereas, on task-irrelevant trials subjects may notice the irrelevant stimuli on every trial but at slightly different times on different trials because their primary task is to determine if a potential target is present. If this is the case, one would expect larger amplitudes with briefer time-courses for awareness-related ERPs in task-relevant conditions and smaller amplitudes with extended durations for the same ERPterm components in task-irrelevant conditions, given that the ERPs are derived by averaging across many trials. Data from PittspersName et al. ( 2012 ) follow this pattern very closely, and although the current data show amplitudeterm differences for the VANterm without obvious corresponding differences in component duration, the shapeterm of the VANterm is slightly skewed with a longer right-tail in the task-irrelevant condition (see Figure 7 ), consistent with the trial-by-trial latencyterm jitter account. A third alternative explanation is that the VANterm and SNterm components may have been temporally superimposed such that only a portion of this negativity reflects perceptual awarenessterm·def. The amplitudeterm increase during task-relevant situations might be due to an increase in top-down attentionterm reflected by the SNterm component, rather than a larger number of aware trials or greater trial-to-trial consistency in the timing of awarenessterm.

While there is growing evidence that the VANterm tracks closely with awarenessterm while the SNterm varies as a function of task demands and attentionterm, it is important to acknowledge the existence of a variety of attentional processes. In addition to exogenous (bottom-up) and endogenous (top-down) attentionterm, visual attentionterm can be selectively allocated to spatial locations, features, or entire objects. An alternative interpretation of the VANterm is that it reflects some form of object-basedterm attentionterm that is necessary for conscious perceptionterm (perhaps a specific interaction between attentionterm and high-level perceptual representations). Because many manipulations of awarenessterm also involve manipulations of attentionterm, this possibility cannot be easily discounted. To give some examples, inattentional blindnessterm·mth involves altering attentionterm to alter awarenessterm; backward maskingterm·mth at short vs. long latencies alters bottom-up attentionterm to influence awarenessterm; backward maskingterm·mth and signal detectionterm at thresholdterm capitalizes on stochastic trial-by-trial fluctuations of attentionterm; the attentional blink involves differences in attentionterm between seen and unseen stimuli; and change blindness differs from change detectionterm based on the allocation of spatial attentionterm. Because of the common co-manipulation of attentionterm and awarenessterm across a variety of paradigms, we previously argued that naming an ERPterm component the “visual awarenessterm negativity” (VANterm) might be pre-mature (PittspersName et al., 2012 ), although we adopt this nomenclature here for consistency with the literature. If the VANterm turns out to reflect a type of object-basedterm attentionterm instead of awarenessterm per se , this might suggest that an obvious ERPterm correlate of conscious perceptionterm has yet to be discovered (Verleger, 2010 ). This would explain the amplitudeterm increase for the VANterm in task-relevant vs. task-irrelevant conditions, i.e., task-relevanceterm enhances object-basedterm attentionterm. Importantly, this would not mean that a neural correlate of awarenessterm does not exist, but rather that ERPs can only measure a limited set of neuronal events. In any case, studies of ERPs can still be useful in helping to narrow down the time-window for which potential NCCs could be found, while more sensitive techniques such as intra-cranial recordings in human epileptic patients may be necessary to identify NCCs.

A final possibility worth consideration is whether a particular type of interaction between attentionterm and perceptual representation is the underlying neural mechanism of conscious awarenessterm, and the VANterm is a marker of this type of interaction. To explore this idea, future consciousness research might focus efforts on understanding object-basedterm attentionterm, perceptual encoding, and the interaction between the two, rather than searching for neurons or neural networks specifically dedicated to consciousness per se (Cohen and Dennett, 2011 ). Currently, there is not strong evidence for or against the view that the VANterm component reflects object-basedterm attentionterm instead of visual awarenessterm. In addition to developing experimental paradigms which can better isolate NCC-proper from pre-consciousterm and post-perceptualterm activity (the focus of the current special issue), it is imperative that researchers craft experimental designs that improve our chances of distinguishing between neural correlates of object-basedterm attentionterm and neural correlates of awarenessterm.
