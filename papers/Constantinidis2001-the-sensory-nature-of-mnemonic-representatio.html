phenomenondelay-period activity in primate prefrontal cortex during visual working memory
claim-typeexistence
mechanismDorsolateral PFC runs two superimposed processes - a slowly decaying sensory representation plus a response-preparation signal that ramps toward the saccade - whose sum produces the observed delay activity — “Based on these considerations, we propose that dorsolateral PFC encompasses neurons engaged in at least two separate processes: a sensory representation that remains fairly constant or slowly decays during the delay period, and a response preparation that increases toward the onset of the saccade (Fig. 8).”
mechanismThe motor plan emerges from a network of neurons that keep holding sensory information, rather than from a discrete sensory-to-motor transformation stage in single neurons — “Our present findings favor the view that a motor plan is an emergent property of a network composed in part by neurons sustaining sensory information, rather than involving a discrete decision stage in which sensory parameters are transformed into motor variables by single neurons”
relation — “In contrast to these results, previous studies analyzing activity in the delay period of a discrimination task concluded that neuronal responses best correlate with the outcome of a perceptual decision18,19.”
relation — “The present findings are in agreement with previous experiments in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex suggesting that neuronal activity in a mnemonic task represents the remembered visual stimulus, as demonstrated by the anti-saccade paradigm, spatial match-to-sample and conditional response tasks10,28,29.”
effect directiondistractor → delay period activity — no-change — “not significantly modulated by the presence of a distractor outside the receptive field”
holds whendistractor → delay period activity — when the distractor is outside the receptive field and the target inside it — “when the target appeared inside”
holds whencontrast ratio → delay period activity — when the distractor appeared in the receptive field — “when the distractor appeared in the receptive field”
replicationcontested
limitationThe task never required luminance itself to be remembered, only the relative comparison — “Our task did not explicitly require the animals to remember the luminance of the stimuli; the subject needed only to discriminate between their relative difference.”
limitationNot all recorded neurons fitted the sensory account; some reflected only the motor response — “This was representative of our population, although we did observe some neurons, particularly those with strong pre-saccadic responses, that only reflected the animal’s motor response.”
limitationThe two results on the same measure hold under opposite spatial conditions. — “Delay period activity was not significantly modulated by the presence of a distractor outside the receptive field, when the target appeared inside (Fig. 4a; regression analysis, p > 0.3). However, delay period activity was significantly dependent on contrast ratio when the distractor appeared in the receptive field”
limitationThe inference the whole result rests on is reported by the authors as contested. — “Although neurons in the primate prefrontal cortex discharge while a stimulus is maintained in memory, it has been argued that temporal correlation does not necessarily imply involvement of this activity in perceptual memory”
limitationThe analysis population was chosen by an explicit decision not to exclude two neuron types. — “We based the analysis of our results on the entire sample, rather than ignore the contribution of primarily cue and saccade neurons to the delay period activity”
Content tags
content-observations: finding · quantity · variable · definition · key-term — grounded in the full text
findingDelay-period firing of prefrontal neurons was graded by the luminance of the stimulus in the receptive field, i.e. by its sensory attributes rather than by the animal's choice. — “However, delay period activity was significantly dependent on contrast ratio when the distractor appeared in the receptive field (regression analysis, p < 10–5).”
findingThe luminance modulation of delay activity survived control for saccade metrics, showing it was not an artefact of the motor response. — “significant (p < 10–5), indicating that the modulation was independent of the motor response.”
findingMnemonic prefrontal activity was directly correlated with the monkeys' psychophysical performance. — “Activity of neurons in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex during the delay period of a mnemonic task was modulated by the perceived sensory attributes of remembered stimuli, and correlated directly with psychophysical performance in the task.”
findingNeuronal discriminability between target and distractor tracked contrast ratio across the entire delay, not only early in it. — “Our results indicated that the ability to discriminate between a target and distractor was dependent on the contrast ratio throughout the delay period (Fig. 5).”
findingThe graded delay responses contradict the prediction of a pure decision-formation account, which requires responses to converge once sensory evidence has been accumulated. — “Contrary to that prediction, our results indicated a graded pattern of responses related to luminance throughout the delay.”
findingA large minority of neurons kept firing above baseline for a distractor in the receptive field even when the saccade was made away from it. — “The effect was statistically significant for 42% of the neurons (45% and 25% for the two animals respectively; t-test, p < 0.05).”
findingThe contrast effect replicated when the delay length varied unpredictably, ruling out the possibility that it depended on a fixed, predictable delay allowing early motor planning. — “The effect of stimulus contrast across the population was statistically significant when the distractor appeared inside the receptive field (two-way ANOVA, p < 10–5) but not significant when the target appeared in the receptive field (p > 0.3) as was the case when using a fixed delay interval (Fig. 7).”
findingReward expectation cannot explain the pattern, because firing was strongest in the condition of minimum expected reward. — “Indeed, neurons discharged more vigorously when the distractor appearing in their receptive field was identical to the target, the condition that represented the minimum reward expectation (Fig. 4b).”
