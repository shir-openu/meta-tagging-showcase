mechanismUnder multiple-response preparation the subject holds several movement representations ready and must discriminate among them, so the more features the two representations share the longer the discrimination takes. — “subjects choose between alternative movement representations, and the increases with the similarity time required to effect the discrimination”
mechanismUnder individual-response preparation the subject edits the representation of the first response into the second, so the more features distinguish the two responses the longer the modification takes. — “the movement representation entails changing the features that distinguish the representations of the two responses, where the increases with the number of distime to complete the modification tinguishing features (i.e., decreases with the similarity of the representations).”
mechanismHigh response uncertainty shrinks the reaction-time benefit any single readied response can gain, which is why subjects abandon multiple-response preparation for individual-response preparation. — “preferable for the subject not to use multiple response preparation - if, for example, the RT benefit that any one response could enjoy were very small (or possibly even negative).”
relation — “Before we show how the response priming method can be used to investigate the time course of response selection, we should explain why we think the method can overcome the difficulties associated with the movement precuing technique.”
replicationcontested
limitationThe technique's key assumption - that subjects ready only the primed response - was violated in some conditions of this very experiment, so the full set of inferences the method affords could not be drawn. — “We were frustrated in our effort to exploit the technique fully, however, because we were led to believe that subjects readied nonprimed responses as well as primed responses in some conditions of the present experiment.”
limitationThe subtraction analyses rest on an explicitly acknowledged pure-insertion assumption about response latency differences. — “In using the subtractions for this purpose, we are making a “pure insertion” assumption (Stemberg 1969) about latency differences among responses.”

findingOn about 6% of trials where the primed response was correctly made, subjects also made an extra unrequired response, and nearly every extra response used the other hand, indicating that the opposite-hand response had also been readied. — “it was noted that extra responses occurred on about 6% of the trials in which primed responses were required and correctly performed. Of the extra responses, only one was a response that used the same hand as the primed response, and of the remaining extra responses, the finger homologous to the primed-response finger was used about as often as the nonhomologous finger.”
findingReaction times were longer when only the finger had to change from the primed response than when both finger and hand had to change, the opposite of what a simple feature-specification account predicts. — “mean RTs were longer when finger alone had to be changed ( f) (fh).”
findingThe method's core assumption failed in part of the experiment: subjects readied nonprimed responses alongside the primed one in some conditions, which blocked full use of the method's inferential machinery. — “because we were led to believe that subjects readied nonprimed responses as well as primed responses in some conditions of the present experiment.”
findingThe proposed pair of preparation methods reconciles the opposite within-hand and between-hand advantages found across experiments, at the cost of assuming that same-hand responses share more movement features than opposite-hand responses. — “This account of the IRP and MRP methods can explain why different within- and between-hand effects have been obtained in the experiments we have referred to here, although the explanation assumes, perhaps somewhat arbitrarily, that responses performed with fingers of the same hand share more features than responses performed with fingers of opposite hands, whether or not they are homologous.”
