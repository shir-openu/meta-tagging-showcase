mechanismContextual cues trigger already-existing automatic processes, which produce the action directly, so no belief or goal has to be changed for behaviour to change— “None of these methods require any change of cognitions, but instead represent aspects of the choice environment, which trigger already existing cognitive processes that, in turn, produce choice of actions beneficial for the individual.”
mechanismA prime automatically spreads activation to the behaviours, feelings and thoughts historically associated with it, which is why an apparently irrelevant cue can steer action— “The cue or prime automatically activates behaviours, feelings and thoughts that have a history of association”
mechanismPeople infer their own attitudes from watching their own (context-driven) behaviour, so an externally cued behaviour change feeds back into cognitive change— “we form our attitudes, opinions, and other internal states by observing our behaviour and concluding what attitudes must have caused them.”
builds-on Bem's (1967) self-perception theory— “This aspect of RAM incorporates Bem's (1967) self-perception theory, which proposed exactly this – we form our attitudes, opinions, and other internal states by observing our behaviour and concluding what attitudes must have caused them.”

content-observations: finding · quantity · variable · definition · key-term — grounded in the full text
findingThe paper's model claims behaviour change runs through two routes on two different processing systems: the reflective system changes cognitions, while the automatic system responds to changes in the context.— “RAM is a dual-process model which assumes that these two routes rely predominantly on different information processing systems – the reflective system is in charge of changing cognitions and the automatic system responds to changing the context.”
findingThe authors name four context-cued processes that work through the automatic system: salience, norms, affect and priming.— “We also identify four processes: salience, norms, affect, and priming (SNAP), which can bring about behaviour change by relying mainly on the automatic system.”
findingAcross the traditional behaviour-change literature the authors find that every model targets one or more of just three psychological variables: beliefs, goals and habits.— “they attempt to change one or more of three psychological variables: beliefs, goals, and habits.”
findingReviewing cost-effectiveness evidence, the authors conclude that environmental interventions can be substantially more cost-effective than traditional individual behavioural interventions.— “The really interesting result is that environmental interventions have the potential to be substantially more cost-effective than more tradition behavioural interventions”
findingHabits are argued to be a barrier to behaviour change rather than a lever for it, because habit change itself still requires reflective goals.— “In this respect habits more act as constraints on behaviour change, not as mechanisms for behaviour change.”
findingSalience and norm interventions can change behaviour without any accompanying change in beliefs or attitudes.— “such cognitive change is not required for salience and norms for have an effect on behaviour.”
findingContrary to other dual-process theories, which hold that only reflective/central processing yields durable change, the paper argues that changing the environment alone produces sustained behaviour change.— “will result in sustained behaviour change without necessitating any change in cognitions.”
findingThe authors claim RAM is the first application of the dual-process paradigm specifically to behaviour-change interventions rather than to persuasion, attitude change or impression formation.— “first consistent application of the dual-process paradigm specifically to behaviour change interventions.”
findingRAM adds a feedback path from changed behaviour back to cognitions, which traditional one-directional models (theory of planned behaviour, health belief model) lack.— “contains a feedback loop from behaviour back to the underlying cognitions, which is not explicitly discussed in traditional methods”
