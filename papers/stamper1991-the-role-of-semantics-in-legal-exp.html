findingA legal expert system built on propositional logic has essentially no semantics of its own: whoever assigns truth-values to the elementary propositions performs all the interpretation, and the machine merely computes consequences.— “Whoever assigns the truth-values to the elementary propositions makes the interpretations from which the consequences are mechanically determined. This kind of system has virtually no semantics, nor does it pretend to have any.”
findingPropositional logic cannot represent any unit smaller than a whole proposition, so the word-level meanings that lawyers argue about cannot be encoded in it at all.— “Propositional logic does not recognise smaller units than whole propositions, certainly not individual words.”
findingPredicate logic leaves the ontology to the analyst's discretion — the same content can be modelled either as a separate individual or folded into a longer predicate — so what exists in the knowledge base is decided arbitrarily.— “This illustration demonstrates the looseness of the ontology behind predicate logic. What is deemed to exist or not exist is decided at the whim of the analyst!”
findingTwo knowledge engineers formalizing the same legal concept in predicate logic will arrive at the identical expression only by chance, because paraphrases of the same rule need share no common term.— “Thus, the same original concept appearing in two different pieces of legislation analysed by two different engineers will only result in the identical predicate expression by a happy accident.”
findingBecause each application invents its own private predicate language, separate legal knowledge bases cannot be integrated by simply merging their rules.— “The problems caused by inventing a private language for each application not only affects the exchange of data but it makes it impossible to integrate legal expert systems by the obvious, simple method of merging rules.”
findingTruth-functional semantics fails for law because in a dispute truth is the negotiated endpoint of the process rather than a fact available at the start.— “In the case of a legal dispute, truth is what we arrive at, not what we start from.”
findingIn the proposed framework, existence here-and-now replaces truth as the primitive concept, on the grounds that existence can be operationalised whereas truth in social behaviour presupposes agents reaching consensus.— “Existence here and now takes the place of truth as a primitive concept, with the benefit of being a concept that can readily be operationalised.”
findingBecause every realisation in Norma carries an authority, the formalism always points back to the human agents in the social system who are responsible for its constructs.— “In other words, this logic will always point to agents in the social system who are responsible for the use of the constructs expressed in the formalism.”
findingNorma cannot be treated as a closed formal system: every behaviour it represents drags in further associated behaviours (ability, beginning, ending, sign, authority) recursively, so an open system is the only possible representation.— “In Norma, one has no alternative but to represent an open system.”
findingThe MEASUR method of analysis, developed from large numbers of legal and business cases, yielded systems cheaper to build and far cheaper to maintain than those built with conventional methodologies.— “This had led to the evolution of a method of analysis which has proved to be effective in designing systems that are cheaper to build and far cheaper to maintain than those developed using the conventional methodologies.”
