findingIn professional mathematicians, reflection on advanced mathematics activated a bilateral fronto-parietal and inferior temporal network that did not include classical language or general-semantic areas. — “Crucially, these activations spared areas related to language and to general-knowledge semantics.”
findingThe same math network was engaged by all four tested branches of mathematics and even transiently by meaningless mathematical statements. — “It was activated by all domains of mathematics tested (analysis, algebra, topology, and geometry) and even, transiently, by meaningless mathematical statements.”
findingNon-mathematical general-knowledge reflection recruited a separate anterior temporal and angular gyrus semantic network. — “Instead, such problems activated distinct bilateral anterior temporal and angular regions.”
findingA region-of-interest analysis of language areas showed mathematics engaged them no more, and often less, than general semantic reasoning did. — “Thus, if anything, mathematics called less upon those language regions than did general semantic reasoning.”
findingThe high-level mathematics network overlapped the parietal and inferior temporal sites activated by simple calculation and by the mere sight of numerals, and this overlap survived removal of statements containing any numerical reference. — “Thus, the overlapping activations to number and to advanced math cannot be explained by a shared component of numerical knowledge but indicate that high-level mathematics recruits the same brain circuit as basic arithmetic.”
findingThe math-versus-nonmath activation difference could not be attributed to task difficulty, since even the easiest math problems activated the network more than the hardest nonmath problems. — “Thus, those sites were activated even during simple mathematical reflection, and their greater activation for math than for nonmath occurred irrespective of task difficulty.”
findingMathematical expertise was accompanied by expanded number and formula responses in the left visual number form area. — “Thus, mathematical expertise is associated with a small expansion of number representations in the left VNFA and a bilateral recruitment of the VNFA by mathematical formulas.”
findingmathematicians performed equally on math and non-math statements, 63% against 65% correct — “Math and nonmath problems were well-matched in objective difficulty level because mathematicians performed identically on both (63% and 65% correct) ( Fig. 1 B and SI Appendix , Supplementary Results ).”
findingcontrols matched mathematicians on non-math but were near chance on math — “Unsurprisingly, they fell close to chance level with math (37% correct, chance level = 33%; P = 0.014): They managed to perform above chance in detecting which statements were meaningful or meaningless (d′ = 0.67, P = 0.002) but could not identify their truth value (d′ = 0.38, n.s.).”
findingall four branches of mathematics activated the same bilateral parietal, temporal and prefrontal network — “All four domains of mathematics activated those regions, as revealed by a significant intersection of activations to each domain ( Fig. 3 A ) (each at P < 0.001).”
findingthe network stayed active for about fifteen seconds after a math statement and deactivated for non-math — “Examination of the time course of activity indicated that, at all sites of the shared math network, the fMRI signal rose sharply after a mathematical statement and remained sustained for ∼15 s ( Fig. 2 C and SI Appendix , Fig. S1 ). Contrariwise, for nonmathematical statements, a slow deactivation was seen”
findingin controls the math contrast landed on the sites for meaningless statements — “In control subjects, the math > nonmath contrast yielded a different set of regions that overlapped with the sites activated by meaningless nonmath statements ( SI Appendix , Fig. S2 and Table S1 ), suggesting that math statements sounded like gibberish to nonmathematicians.”
findingessentially no intersection between the math and general-knowledge semantic networks, and none in perisylvian language cortex even at a lenient threshold — “Crucially, there was essentially no intersection at P < 0.001 of the areas for meaningful > meaningless math and for meaningful > meaningless nonmath ( Fig. 4 A and SI Appendix , Tables S1 and S3 ). The only small area of intersection, suggesting a role in generic reflection and decision making, was observed outside the classical language network, in bilateral superior frontal (BA 8) and left inferior MFG. Even at a lower threshold ( P < 0.01 uncorrected), the intersection extended to part of posterior parietal and dorsal PFC but spared perisylvian language cortex.”
findinglanguage regions defined by an independent localiser were engaged no more by math than by general reasoning, and often less — “All regions were activated during sentence presentation ( SI Appendix , Fig. S7 ), either identically across conditions, or more for nonmath than for math and/or for controls than for mathematicians ( SI Appendix , Table S5 ). Thus, if anything, mathematics called less upon those language regions than did general semantic reasoning.”
findingthe math network overlapped the areas for arithmetic and for merely viewing numerals — “Both calculation and simple number processing activated bilateral IPS and IT, thus replicating early observations of number-sense and number-form areas ( Fig. 6 ). Remarkably, those activations overlapped entirely with the regions activated by higher level mathematics in mathematicians only”
findingresponses to written formulas were larger in mathematicians, as extra activated volume rather than tighter alignment — “First, the left inferior temporal activation to written mathematical formulas was significantly enhanced in mathematicians relative to controls (−53 −64 −17, t = 4.27) ( Fig. 8 B ). Single-subject ROI analyses verified that this effect was not simply due to greater variance in anatomical localization in controls compared with mathematicians, but to a genuine increase in the volume of bilateral IT cortex activated by mathematical formulas”
findingface responses were reduced in right inferior temporal cortex in mathematicians — “Finally, because literacy has been shown to induce a hemispheric shift in face responses ( 38 ), we also examined face processing in our mathematicians. Although there was no significant difference between the two groups at the principal peak of the right FFA, a whole-brain search indicated that responses to faces were significantly reduced in mathematicians relative to controls in right-hemispheric IT (44 −45 −17, t = 4.72)”
findingthe caudate responded to whichever domain each group was expert in — “First, a group × problem type interaction revealed a striking group difference in the bilateral head of the caudate nucleus ( SI Appendix , Fig. S11 ). This region was active in mathematicians only when they were exposed to math statements and, in control subjects, only when they were exposed to nonmath statements. Thus, the engagement of this subcortical region, which is known to participate in motivation and executive attention, shifted radically toward the subject’s preferred domain.”
findingCONFOUND REVERSED: easy math contrasted against difficult non-math still produced the same sites — “Second, to directly evaluate the impact of difficulty on the observed brain networks, within each subject, we sorted the meaningful math and nonmath statements into two levels of subjective difficulty (easy or difficult: i.e., below or above the subject’s mean of the corresponding category). As expected, the easiest math statements were rated as much easier than the difficult nonmath statements ( Fig. 5 A ). Despite this difference, the contrast of meaningful easy math > meaningful difficult nonmath again revealed the same sites as the ones that were activated for the standard math > nonmath contrast ( Fig. 5 B ). Thus, those sites were activated even during simple mathematical reflection, and their greater activation for math than for nonmath occurred irrespective of task difficulty.”
findingCONTROL NULL: contrasting difficult against easy statements within each domain revealed no significant sites at all — “This result was confirmed by the contrast of difficult > easy math and difficult > easy nonmath, which revealed no significant sites.”
findingORTHOGONAL REPLICATION: the meaningful-versus-meaningless math contrast, which holds words constant, reproduced the whole result — “As a second criterion for brain areas involved in mathematical expertise, we compared the activations during reflection on meaningful versus meaningless mathematical statements. This contrast, which is orthogonal to the previous one and controls for lexical content, fully replicated the above results.”
findingCONTENT REANALYSIS: excluding statements with any indirect numerical reference left the activation virtually unchanged — “Our mathematical statements carefully avoided any direct mention of numbers or arithmetic facts ( SI Appendix ), but some still contained an occasional indirect reference to numbers or to fractions (e.g., ℝ 2 , unit sphere, semi-major axis, etc). We therefore reanalyzed the results after systematic exclusion of such statements. The activation evoked by mathematical reflection remained virtually unchanged ( SI Appendix , Fig. S9 and Table S6 ). Thus, the overlapping activations to number and to advanced math cannot be explained by a shared component of numerical knowledge but indicate that high-level mathematics recruits the same brain circuit as basic arithmetic.”
findingHAZARD NAMED ABOUT THEIR OWN RESULT: group-level overlap can arise from intersubject averaging, so the overlap was retested within subjects — “Because group-level overlap of activation can arise artificially from intersubject averaging, we next turned to more sensitive within-subject analyses.”
findingthe within-subject pattern analyses confirmed that advanced math, arithmetic and numeral viewing share cortical sites in trained individuals — “Overall, these high-resolution single-subject analyses confirm that advanced mathematics, basic arithmetic, and even the mere viewing of numbers and formulas recruit similar and overlapping cortical sites in mathematically trained individuals.”
