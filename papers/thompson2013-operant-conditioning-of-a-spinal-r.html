mechanismAcquiring the new smaller-reflex behaviour disturbs the post-injury spinal equilibrium and thereby triggers widespread adaptive plasticity that settles into a new equilibrium serving both the new reflex and locomotion — “It apparently triggered widespread adaptive plasticity that produced a new equilibrium that both decreased the H-reflex and improved locomotion.”
mechanismIn neurologically normal people, compensatory plasticity protecting already-satisfactory behaviours works against the long-term spinal change, which is why long-term change is smaller in them than in SCI subjects — “For behaviors such as locomotion, which are already satisfactory, these adaptations are likely to impede the long-term plasticity that decreases the H-reflex.”
mechanismInjury to the corticospinal tract, the descending pathway that conditioning depends on, weakens the immediate cortical (task-dependent) component of the reflex change in SCI subjects — “The lesser task-dependent adaptation in subjects with SCI may reflect damage to the corticospinal tract (CST), the spinal cord pathway principally responsible for H-reflex conditioning”
mechanismBetter bilateral modulation of ankle muscle activity yields more effective weight bearing and push-off, which in turn raises walking speed — “These bilateral improvements in the modulation of muscle activity controlling movement about the ankle joint probably resulted in more effective weight bearing and push off”

limitationThe success rate, though not significantly below that of normal subjects, was lower, and applicability to more severe impairment is unknown. — “Although their success rate was not significantly different from that of normal subjects ( Thompson et al., 2009 ), it was lower; and the extent to which reflex conditioning is possible in people with more severe impairments is unclear.”
limitationThe small H-reflex increase seen in the unconditioned control group may be a nonspecific effect of repeated exposure to the measurement protocol. — “This may have been a nonspecific effect of continued exposure over 30 sessions to the baseline protocol of standing, providing soleus background EMG, and having the H-reflex elicited.”
limitationThirteen subjects, of whom only six showed the effect, and the comparison group has four — “The study participants were 13 ambulatory subjects (9 men and 4 women; age range, 28–68 years; mean ± SD age, 48.4 ± 13.9 years)”
limitationAll subjects were AIS grade C or D, so the protocol is untested in more severe injury and requires surviving supraspinal input — “While future improvements in the conditioning protocol may increase success, the need for supraspinal input will remain.”
limitationThe subjects who improved are the same subjects in whom conditioning worked, so improvement and successful conditioning cannot be separated from subject-level differences — “The improvement was evident both in quantitative testing and, most important, to the subjects themselves in their daily lives. It did not occur in people in whom the H-reflex did not decrease, whether they were UC subjects or unsuccessful DC subjects.”
limitationThe breadth of the effect exceeds what the manipulated pathway can explain, and the wider plasticity invoked to cover the gap is inferred rather than measured — “It is unlikely that the plasticity underlying a smaller soleus H-reflex in one leg could itself have such broad beneficial effects on walking, including increasing the locomotor EMG modulation of contralateral muscles.”
limitationThe normal-subject comparison values are drawn from an earlier study rather than a concurrent control group — “The data for normal DC subjects are from Thompson et al. (2009) .”
limitationThe subject comments are unsolicited and unblinded, and the subjects knew which protocol they were on — “Over the 3 months of the study, the subjects were not asked about the current state of their motor function or whether their disabilities had changed in any way.”
limitationThe comparison group's reflex drifted upward over the same period, which the authors attribute to nonspecific exposure rather than measure — “This may have been a nonspecific effect of continued exposure over 30 sessions to the baseline protocol of standing, providing soleus background EMG, and having the H-reflex elicited.”

findingSix of the nine people with spinal cord injury successfully down-conditioned the soleus H-reflex, a 67% success rate not significantly below the 89% seen in neurologically normal subjects. — “The success rate for the subjects with SCI (i.e., 6/9 or 67%) is slightly, but not significantly, less than that for neurologically normal subjects (i.e., 8/9 or 89%)”
findingThe H-reflex decrease was specific to the conditioning protocol: no unconditioned subject's reflex fell, and the two groups' final reflex sizes differed significantly. — “In contrast, the conditioned H-reflex did not decrease significantly in any of the UC subjects; and the DC and UC groups differed significantly in final H-reflex size”
findingTen-metre walking speed rose by an average of 59% in the six subjects whose H-reflex decreased. — “The increase was substantial and significant in the six DC subjects in whom the H-reflex decreased [(mean ± SE) +59 ± 19%; p = 0.03, paired t test].”
findingGait symmetry moved toward the ideal ratio of 1 in every subject whose H-reflex decreased. — “After the 30 conditioning or control sessions, this ratio decreased in every subject in whom the H-reflex decreased, becoming closer to 1”
findingIn subjects whose H-reflex did not decrease, gait actually became less symmetrical over the same 30 sessions. — “In contrast, the ratio increased in every subject in whom the H-reflex did not decrease”
findingSuccessful down-conditioning of one leg's soleus reflex increased step-cycle EMG modulation in ankle and knee flexors and extensors of both legs, not just the conditioned leg. — “Thus, successful H-reflex down-conditioning was associated with a significant increase in the degree to which ankle and knee flexor and extensor muscles of both legs modulated their activity in synchrony with the step cycle.”
findingAlthough the total reflex decrease matched that of normal subjects, its composition differed: SCI subjects showed less immediate task-dependent adaptation (-7% vs -15%) and more long-term across-session change (-24% vs -16%). — “Task-dependent adaptation was significantly less in subjects with SCI than in neurologically normal subjects (−7% vs −15%), while long-term change was significantly more (−24% vs −16%).”
findingUnprompted positive reports of improved walking clustered almost entirely in the six successfully conditioned subjects (25 of 30 comments). — “The contrast is striking: 25 of the 30 positive comments were made by the six DC subjects in whom down-conditioning was successful, and only 5 positive comments by the other seven subjects”
findingThe reflex decrease persisted after training stopped, averaging 65% of baseline at one month and 58% at three months. — “At both 1 month and 3 months, the conditioned H-reflex remained reduced in every subject, averaging (mean ± SE) 65 ± 10% and 58 ± 10% of baseline values, respectively.”
findingThe reflex reduction carried over into walking itself: the H-reflex elicited during locomotion fell to 59% of its baseline size. — “In the successful DC subjects, the average locomotor H-reflex also decreased [to (mean ± SE) 59 ± 17% of baseline value; p = 0.04 by paired t test].”
findingthe conditioned H-reflex fell to the same 69% of baseline in SCI subjects as in normal subjects, but by a different mix of components — “First, the final average value of the conditioned H-reflex (i.e., the average of the last three conditioning sessions) in the subjects with SCI is identical to that in normal subjects [i.e., (mean ± SE) 69 ± 11% and 69 ± 6% of baseline, respectively].”
findingtask-dependent adaptation was smaller and long-term change larger after injury — “Task-dependent adaptation is greater in the normal subjects, while change in the control H-reflex is greater in the subjects with SCI.”
findingreflex decrease was specific to the down-conditioning protocol and absent in the unconditioned group — “Thus, H-reflex decrease was specific to the DC group.”
findingthe unconditioned group's reflex rose slightly but significantly over the same period — “Indeed, it should be noted that the UC group as a whole showed a slight but significant increase in the conditioned H-reflex [to (mean ± SE) 116 ± 7% of baseline; p = 0.05 by paired t test].”
findingwalking speed rose 59% on average in subjects whose reflex decreased and not significantly in those whose did not — “The increase was substantial and significant in the six DC subjects in whom the H-reflex decreased [(mean ± SE) +59 ± 19%; p = 0.03, paired t test].”
findinggait symmetry improved in every subject whose reflex decreased and worsened in every subject whose did not — “After the 30 conditioning or control sessions, this ratio decreased in every subject in whom the H-reflex decreased, becoming closer to 1 ( p = 0.05, paired t test). In contrast, the ratio increased in every subject in whom the H-reflex did not decrease ( p = 0.02).”
findingEMG modulation improved bilaterally, not only in the conditioned leg — “This improvement was bilateral; it was not limited to the muscles of the conditioned leg.”
findingthe reflex decrease persisted at one, three and in one case six months — “At both 1 month and 3 months, the conditioned H-reflex remained reduced in every subject, averaging (mean ± SE) 65 ± 10% and 58 ± 10% of baseline values, respectively.”

Introduction The primary goal of neurological rehabilitation is to restore important motor and cognitive skills that have been impaired by injury or disease. Current therapeutic methods consist primarily of the repeated practice of these skills (e.g., treadmill locomotionterm·dom, reach and grasp actions) ( Wernig and Müller, 1992 ; Edgerton et al., 1997 , 2001 , 2008 ; Harkema et al., 1997 ; Taub et al., 1999 ; Wernig et al., 2000 ; Maegele et al., 2002 ; Taub and Uswatte, 2003 ; Wolf et al., 2006 ), with the expectation that this practice will lead to plasticity that improves function ( Koski et al., 2004 ; Thickbroom et al., 2004 ; Thomas and Gorassini, 2005 ; Yen et al., 2008 ). Although this strategy is logical and often beneficial, it is seldom completely successful. Novel strategies that can complement current methods and thereby enhance skill restoration are needed. The skills that rehabilitation attempts to restore normally depend on plasticity throughout the CNS, from the cortex to the spinal cord ( Drew et al., 2002 ; Nielsen, 2002 ; Hultborn and Nielsen, 2007 ; WolpawpersName, 2010 ; Rossignol and Frigon, 2011 ). Moreover, the location and nature of the damage that impairs performance differ widely from individual to individual, as well as from disorder to disorder. As a result, the plastic changes needed to restore a particular skill (e.g., locomotionterm·dom) are also likely to differ widely across individuals. Thus, new therapeutic methods that can induce plasticity in particular CNS pathways, and can thereby target each individual's particular deficits, might significantly increase the effectiveness of rehabilitation. In both animals and humans, operant conditioningterm·mth protocols can modify specific spinal reflex pathways ( WolpawpersName et al., 1983 ; WolpawpersName and O'Keefe, 1984 ; WolpawpersName, 1987 ; Chen and WolpawpersName, 1995 ; Wolf and Segal, 1996 ; Carp et al., 2006b ; Chen et al., 2006a ; ThompsonpersName et al., 2009 ). Because these spinal pathways participate in important skills such as locomotionterm·dom, conditioning protocols might be used to reduce the functional deficits produced by spinal cord injuries, strokes, and other disorders. An initial animal study supports this hypothesis. In rats in which a lateralized spinal cord injuryterm·dom (SCI) had produced a gait asymmetry, appropriate conditioning of the soleusterm·dom H-reflexterm·dom on the injured side eliminated the asymmetry and restored more normal locomotionterm·dom ( Chen et al., 2006b ). The present study is, to our knowledge, the first effort to use spinal reflex conditioning to improve function in people with SCI. It focuses on people in whom a chronic incomplete SCI has produced a spastic gait disorder characterized by hyperreflexiaterm·dom and abnormal reflex modulation in ankle extensor muscles ( Dietz and Sinkjaer, 2007 ; Nielsen et al., 2007 ). By down-conditioningterm·mth the soleusterm·dom H-reflexterm·dom, the study sought to reduce these abnormalities, and to thereby improve the speed and symmetry of locomotionterm·dom. The results are clear and encouraging. They suggest that operant conditioningterm·mth protocols could provide a valuable new approach to restoring useful function after spinal cord injuries or in other neuromuscular disorders. Electrical stimulation and EMG recording. At the beginning of each session, EMG recording and stimulating electrodes were placed over the leg. EMG activity from soleusterm·dom and its antagonist tibialis anteriorterm·dom (TA) was recorded with surface self-adhesive Ag-AgCl electrodes (2.2 × 3.5 cm, Vermed), amplified, bandpass filtered (10–1000 Hz), digitized (5000 Hz), and stored. To elicit the H-reflexterm·dom, the tibial nerve was stimulated in the popliteal fossa, using surface Ag-AgCl electrodes (2.2 × 2.2 cm for the cathode and 2.2 × 3.5 cm for the anode; Vermed) and a Grass S88 stimulator (with a CCU1 constant current unit and an SIU5 stimulus isolation unit; Astro-Med). The stimulating electrode pair was placed so as to minimize the H-reflexterm·dom threshold and to avoid stimulating other nerves. This placement was accomplished by monitoring the EMG of soleusterm·dom and TA and palpating other lower-leg muscles, such as the peroneal muscle group. To avoid session-to-session variability in electrode placement, their positions were mapped in relation to landmarks on the skin (e.g., scars or moles). The same individuals (A.K.T. assisted by Briana M. Abel) placed the electrodes and conducted every session for every subject. The soleusterm·dom H-reflexterm·dom was elicited by a 1 ms square-pulse stimulus while the subject maintained a natural standing posture with hands resting on a horizontal bar at waist height and with stable levels of soleusterm·dom and TA background EMGterm·mth activity. The stimulus occurred after the subject had maintained rectified soleusterm·dom and TA EMG activity within specified ranges for at least 2 s. Typically, the soleusterm·dom range was 10–20% of a maximum voluntary contraction, and the TA range was 0–7 μV (i.e., resting level). The minimum interstimulus interval was 5 s. Session protocol. At the beginning of each session, an H-reflexterm·dom/M-waveterm·mth (H-M) recruitment curveterm·mth was obtained. All H-reflexterm·dom and M-waveterm·mth measurements were reported in absolute values (i.e., equivalent to rectified EMG). Stimulus intensity was varied in increments of 1.25–2.50 mA from below soleusterm·dom H-reflexterm·dom threshold, to the maximum H-reflexterm·dom (H max ), to an intensity just above that needed to elicit the maximum M-waveterm·mth (M max ) ( Kido et al., 2004b ; Makihara et al., 2012 ). Approximately 10 different intensities were used to obtain each recruitment curveterm·mth. At each intensity, four EMG responses were averaged to measure the H-reflexterm·dom and M-waveterm·mth. The stimulus amplitude used for the subsequent H-reflexterm·dom trials fell on the rising phase of the H-reflexterm·dom recruitment curveterm·mth and typically produced an M-waveterm·mth just above threshold. In each subject, this M-wave sizeterm·var was maintained for the H-reflexterm·dom trials of all the sessions. In the baseline and control sessions ( Fig. 1 B ), the H-M recruitment curveterm·mth was followed by three 75-trial blocks of control trials, in which the subject was not asked to change the H-reflexterm·dom and was not given visual feedback as to H-reflex sizeterm·def (see Visual feedback). In the conditioning sessions ( Fig. 1 B ), the H-M recruitment curveterm·mth was followed by a 20-trial block of control trials identical to those of the baseline or control sessions; and this block was followed by three 75-trial blocks of conditioning trials, in which the subject was asked to decrease H-reflex sizeterm·def and was provided with immediate visual feedback that indicated his or her success in doing so (see below). Visual feedback. The visual feedback screens for control and conditioning trials have been described in detail previously ( ThompsonpersName et al., 2009 ) and are illustrated in Figure 1 C . Briefly, the screen could present two graphs, one for soleusterm·dom background EMGterm·mth activity and one for H-reflex sizeterm·def. In control trials, only the background EMGterm·mth graph was shown: if the subject kept the height of the vertical bar (i.e., soleusterm·dom background EMGterm·mth activity level, in absolute value) in the specified range for 2 s, and at least 5 s had passed since the last stimulus, a stimulus pulse elicited the H-reflexterm·dom and M-waveterm·mth. In conditioning trials, the background EMGterm·mth graph was shown, and, in addition, the H-reflex sizeterm·def graph was shown. This graph constantly showed a heavy horizontal line indicating the subject's average H-reflex sizeterm·def for the six baseline sessions and a shaded area that indicated the H-reflex sizeterm·def range that satisfied the current down-conditioningterm·mth criterion value. Two hundred milliseconds after the stimulus, a vertical bar reflecting H-reflex sizeterm·def appeared. The bar was green (indicating success) when the H-reflexterm·dom fell within the shaded area (i.e., was below the criterion value), and the bar was red (indicating failure) when the H-reflex sizeterm·def was not below the criterion value. In addition, the current success rate (i.e., the percentage of the trials of the current 75-trial block that were successful) was shown below the graph and was updated after each trial. Thus, for each control trial, the visual feedback simply helped the subject maintain the required prestimulus background EMGterm·mth activity. In contrast, for each conditioning trial, the visual feedback also informed subjects as to whether they had succeeded in producing an H-reflexterm·dom small enough to satisfy the size criterion, and it showed the success rate for the current block of trials. In each conditioning session, the criterion value for the first block of 75 conditioning trials was based on the immediately preceding block of 20 control trials, and the criterion values for the second and third blocks of conditioning trials were based on the H-reflexes of the immediately preceding block of 75 conditioning trials. The criterion was selected so that if H-reflexterm·dom values for the new block were similar to those for the previous block, 50–60% of the trials would be successful ( Chen and WolpawpersName, 1995 ). For each block, the subject earned a modest extra monetary reward when the success rate exceeded 50% (for full details, see ThompsonpersName et al., 2009 ). Analysis of conditioned and control H-reflexes. For each session of each subject, we determined the average H-reflex sizeterm·def for the 225 trials of the three 75-trial blocks ( Fig. 1 B ). This value is called the conditioned H-reflex sizeterm·var (regardless of whether the session is from a DC subject or a UC subject). In addition, for each session of each subject we determined the average H-reflex sizeterm·def for 20 control trials. This value is called the control H-reflex sizeterm·var. For the 6 baseline sessions of all subjects and the 30 control sessions of the UC subjects, these 20 control trials were the first 20 trials of the first 75-trial block. For the 30 conditioning sessions and the follow-up sessions of the DC subjects, these 20 control trials were elicited before the three 75-trial blocks of conditioning trials, as indicated in Figure 1 B . H-reflex sizeterm·def was defined as the average absolute value of soleusterm·dom EMG (i.e., equivalent to rectified EMG) in the H-reflexterm·dom interval (typically 30–45 ms after the stimulus) minus the average absolute value of soleusterm·dom background EMGterm·mth. To determine for each subject whether the conditioned H-reflex sizeterm·var changed significantly over the 30 conditioning or control sessions, the average H-reflexes for the 225 trials of the three 75-trial blocks of the final six sessions (i.e., sessions 25–30) were compared with the average H-reflexes for the 225 trials of the three 75-trial blocks of the six baseline sessions by unpaired t testterm·stat (two tailed). To determine for each subject the final conditioned H-reflex sizeterm·var, the average H-reflexes for the 225 trials of the three 75-trial blocks of the final three sessions (i.e., sessions 28–30) were averaged, and the result was expressed as the percentage of the average H-reflexterm·dom for the 225 trials of the three 75-trial blocks of the six baseline sessions. (Thus, a value of 100% indicated no change.) To determine for each subject the final control H-reflex sizeterm·var, the average H-reflexes for the 20 control trials of sessions 28–30 were averaged, and the result was expressed as the percentage of the average H-reflexterm·dom for the 20 control trials of the six baseline sessions. To assess the stability of soleusterm·dom M max , soleusterm·dom M-wave sizeterm·var in control and conditioning trials, and soleusterm·dom and TA background EMGterm·mth levels, a repeated-measures ANOVAterm·stat was applied to the mean values across successive six-session blocks beginning with the six baseline sessions. Soleusterm·dom M max , soleusterm·dom M-wave sizeterm·var during H-reflexterm·dom elicitation, and soleusterm·dom and TA background EMGterm·mth levels remained stable across all the sessions in both the DC and UC groups ( p > 0.33 for all of these measures in both groups, one-way repeated-measures ANOVAterm·stat). These results confirmed the stability of EMG recording and nerve stimulation conditions in this study, and thus supported the validity of the methodology. Assessment of locomotionterm·dom. Locomotionterm·dom was assessed before and after the 30 conditioning or control sessions. These assessments occurred on non-session days. First, the subject was asked to walk 10 m overground at a comfortable speed three times, and the average walking time was determined. Then, locomotor symmetry, EMG activity, and H-reflexterm·dom modulation were measured. For these measurements during locomotionterm·dom, any subject who wore an ankle foot orthosis was asked to remove it. Surface EMG was recorded from the soleusterm·dom, TA, vastus lateralis (VL), and biceps femoris (BF) muscles of both legs. Footswitch cells inserted between the subject's shoe and foot detected foot contact (typically, heel or toe contact). For locomotor H-reflexterm·dom measurement, single 1 ms square-pulse stimuli were delivered at different points in the step cycleterm·dom to evaluate phase-dependent H-reflexterm·dom modulation ( Capaday and Stein, 1986 ; Stein and Capaday, 1988 ; Ethier et al., 2003 ; Kido et al., 2004b ). The stimulus interval was set to be long enough to have at least one full unstimulated step cycleterm·dom between successive stimuli. Those subjects who were able to walk on a treadmill for several minutes with a consistent stepping rhythm did so twice at a comfortable speed: once without H-reflexterm·dom elicitation and once while tibial nerve stimulation elicited the soleusterm·dom H-reflexterm·dom. Subjects who were not able to walk on the treadmill repeated 10 m overground walking without stimulation until at least 50 steps were obtained. They then repeated 10 m overground walking with H-reflexterm·dom elicitation until at least 50 stimulated steps were obtained. During these measurements, the subjects took sitting breaks as often as needed. The data were assessed as described below, and the locomotor measurements obtained before and after the 30 conditioning or control sessions were compared. For analysis of locomotor EMG activity, the complete step cycleterm·dom was divided into 12 bins of equal duration ( Kido et al., 2004a ; Makihara et al., 2012 ). For the muscles of the conditioned leg, the step cycleterm·dom went from the conditioned leg's foot contact (cFC) to the next cFC; for the muscles of the contralateral (i.e., nonconditioned) leg, the step cycleterm·dom went from the nonconditioned leg's foot contact (nFC) to the next nFC. For each muscle of each subject, the average rectified EMG amplitude in each of the 12 bins was determined and expressed as a percentage of the amplitude in the bin with the highest amplitude. The degree to which each muscle's activity was modulated during locomotionterm·dom was determined by calculating its modulation indexterm·mth (MI) in percent as: 100 × [(highest bin amplitude − lowest bin amplitude)/highest bin amplitude] ( Zehr and Kido, 2001 ; Zehr and Loadman, 2012 ). Thus, an MI of 0% indicated that a muscle did not modulate its activity at all over the step cycleterm·dom. To assess gait quality, we examined step-cycle symmetryterm·mth (i.e., the ratio of the time between the nFC and the cFC to the time between cFC and nFC). A ratio of 1 indicates a symmetrical gait. Spontaneous subject comments. Over the 3 months of the study, the subjects were not asked about the current state of their motor function or whether their disabilities had changed in any way. Nevertheless, many volunteered comments when they came in for sessions. We kept a record of these spontaneous comments and when they were first made. They fall into distinct categories and tell a clear story both in their nature and in their timing. Thus, they are presented as a unique and important component of the results. Discussion This study asked two questions. First, can people with chronic SCI and impaired locomotionterm·dom decrease the soleusterm·dom H-reflexterm·dom in response to an operant conditioningterm·mth protocol? Second, if so, is H-reflexterm·dom decrease associated with improved locomotionterm·dom? The results answer these questions clearly. First, people with incomplete SCI and impaired locomotionterm·dom decreased the H-reflexterm·dom in response to an operant down-conditioningterm·mth protocol (DC subjects). Their success rate and magnitude of reflex change were comparable to those of people who were neurologically normal. H-reflexterm·dom decrease was specific to the down-conditioningterm·mth protocol; it did not occur in subjects in whom the H-reflexterm·dom was simply elicited without feedback (UC subjects). Second, H-reflexterm·dom decrease was associated with faster and more symmetrical locomotionterm·dom. The improvement was evident both in quantitative testing and, most important, to the subjects themselves in their daily lives. It did not occur in people in whom the H-reflexterm·dom did not decrease, whether they were UC subjects or unsuccessful DC subjects. Indeed, locomotionterm·dom became less symmetrical in every subject in whom the H-reflexterm·dom did not decrease. (This greater asymmetry, combined with their slight increase in walking speed, suggests that these subjects expected to be walking faster after the 30 sessions, and thus walked slightly faster by taking more asymmetrical steps.) These results indicate that reflex-conditioning protocols might help to restore motor function after SCI or in other disorders. They also provide insight into the factors shaping the plasticity associated with H-reflexterm·dom conditioning and into its potential therapeutic applications. H-reflexterm·dom conditioning in people with or without spinal cord injuryterm·dom The subjects with SCI were not significantly different from neurologically normal subjects ( ThompsonpersName et al., 2009 ) in the probability of successful down-conditioningterm·def, and they were identical in the average magnitude of their H-reflexterm·dom decrease (i.e., 31%). This finding is consistent with results for soleusterm·dom H-reflexterm·dom conditioning in spinal cord-injured rats ( Chen et al., 2006b ) and biceps spinal stretch reflex conditioning in people with SCI ( Segal and Wolf, 1994 ). The subjects with SCI did differ significantly from normal subjects in the composition of their final H-reflexterm·dom change. Conditioned H-reflexterm·dom change is composed of task-dependent adaptationterm·dom (i.e., within-session difference between the control H-reflexterm·dom and the conditioned H-reflexterm·dom) plus long-term changeterm·dom (i.e., across-session change in the control H-reflexterm·dom) ( ThompsonpersName et al., 2009 ). The lesser task-dependent adaptationterm·dom in subjects with SCI may reflect damage to the corticospinal tractterm·dom (CST), the spinal cord pathway principally responsible for H-reflexterm·dom conditioning ( Chen et al., 1996 , 1999 , 2002 ; Chen and WolpawpersName, 2002 ), and may account for the slightly slower course of H-reflexterm·dom decrease (i.e., over 30 sessions vs 24 in normal subjects) ( ThompsonpersName et al., 2009 ). It may result from the fact that task-dependent adaptationterm·dom affects the H-reflexterm·dom pathway only during the conditioning protocol, while long-term changeterm·dom affects it continuously, and thus has much wider effects. Because the spinal cord serves many behaviors, spinal cord plasticityterm·dom affects many behaviors. In neurologically normal subjects, the spinal cord plasticityterm·dom responsible for the long-term changeterm·dom in the H-reflexterm·dom is likely to disturb behaviors such as locomotionterm·dom, which are already satisfactory; and it may thereby lead to additional plasticity that compensates for the change in the H-reflexterm·dom pathway. Potential therapeutic applications of reflex-conditioning protocols This study sought to down-condition the soleusterm·dom H-reflexterm·dom on the rationale that reducing the activity of this pathway would reduce the hyperreflexiaterm·dom that impaired locomotionterm·dom in these subjects with incomplete SCI ( Dietz and Sinkjaer, 2007 ; Nielsen et al., 2007 ). Furthermore, the locomotor behaviors of knee and ankle extensor and flexor muscles in both legs became more strongly modulated in synchrony with the step cycleterm·dom, which presumably contributed to the improvement in walking speed and symmetry. These encouraging results are surprising in their breadth. It is unlikely that the plasticity underlying a smaller soleusterm·dom H-reflexterm·dom in one leg could itself have such broad beneficial effects on walking, including increasing the locomotor EMG modulation of contralateral muscles. This broad impact, combined with the animal data discussed above, implies that in these subjects with SCI, H-reflexterm·dom conditioning triggered additional plasticity in other pathways important in locomotionterm·dom, and thereby changed the entire behavior. These subjects had been injured 0.7–10 years earlier, and their locomotor deficits were stable. In this setting, the acquisition of a new behavior, a down-conditioned soleusterm·dom H-reflexterm·dom, disturbed the postinjury equilibrium that the injured spinal cord had reached. It apparently triggered widespread adaptive plasticity that produced a new equilibrium that both decreased the H-reflexterm·dom and improved locomotionterm·dom. Because they can target particular spinal pathways and can either weaken or strengthen the activity of these pathways, reflex-conditioning protocols can be designed to focus on each individual's particular deficits. The present study down-conditioned the soleusterm·dom H-reflexterm·dom because locomotionterm·dom was impaired by hyperreflexiaterm·dom. In contrast, the study by Chen et al. (2006b) in spinal cord-injured rats up-conditioned the soleusterm·dom H-reflexterm·dom because locomotionterm·dom was impaired by weak right stance. In both cases, the intervention was effective. This flexibility and specificity are distinctive and desirable features of this new therapeutic approach; and they distinguish it from less focused interventions such as botulinum toxin or baclofen, which simply weaken muscles or reflexes and may have undesirable side effects ( Dario and Tomei, 2004 ; Dario et al., 2004 ; Sheean, 2006 ; Ward, 2008 ; Thomas and Simpson, 2012 ). Reflex-conditioning protocols might supplement therapies that involve repetition of complex behaviors [e.g., body-weight supported treadmill training ( Edgerton et al., 2008 ) and constraint-induced movement therapy ( Taub and Uswatte, 2003 ; Wolf et al., 2006 )]. The results of the present study also encourage therapeutic exploration of other reflex-conditioning protocols. For example, in rats, reciprocal inhibition of soleusterm·dom by common peroneal nerve stimulation can be increased or decreased by operant conditioningterm·mth ( Chen et al., 2006a ). Certainly, the dependence of reflex conditioning on the CST ( Chen et al., 1996 , 1999 , 2002 ; Chen and WolpawpersName, 2002 ) will affect its efficacy in people with SCI. On the American Spinal Injury Association impairment scaleterm·mth (AIS), the disabilities of the present subjects were rated C or D. Although their success rate was not significantly different from that of normal subjects ( ThompsonpersName et al., 2009 ), it was lower; and the extent to which reflex conditioning is possible in people with more severe impairments is unclear. While future improvements in the conditioning protocol may increase success, the need for supraspinal input will remain. On the other hand, reflex conditioning may also prove useful for disorders in which the CST is not affected. For example, in rats in which peripheral nerve transection and reinnervation have produced disordered afferent connections, H-reflexterm·dom up-conditioningterm·mth can help to restore more normal reflex function ( Chen et al., 2010 ).
