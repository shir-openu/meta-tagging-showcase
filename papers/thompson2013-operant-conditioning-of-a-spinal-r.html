phenomenonoperant down-conditioning of the soleus H-reflex— “To explore the therapeutic application of these protocols, we studied the impact of down-conditioning the soleus H-reflex in people with impaired locomotion caused by chronic incomplete spinal cord injury.”
claim-typemethod— “They suggest that operant conditioning protocols could provide a valuable new approach to restoring useful function after spinal cord injuries or in other neuromuscular disorders.”
method — evidence— “After a baseline period in which soleus H-reflex size was measured and locomotion was assessed, subjects completed either 30 H-reflex down-conditioning sessions (DC subjects) or 30 sessions in which the H-reflex was simply measured [unconditioned (UC) subjects], and locomotion was reassessed.”
object of study — evidence— “To explore the therapeutic application of these protocols, we studied the impact of down-conditioning the soleus H-reflex in people with impaired locomotion caused by chronic incomplete spinal cord injury.”
research question — evidence— “This study asked two questions. First, can people with chronic SCI and impaired locomotion decrease the soleus H-reflex in response to an operant conditioning protocol? Second, if so, is H-reflex decrease associated with improved locomotion?”
mechanismAcquiring the new smaller-reflex behaviour disturbs the post-injury spinal equilibrium and thereby triggers widespread adaptive plasticity that settles into a new equilibrium serving both the new reflex and locomotion— “It apparently triggered widespread adaptive plasticity that produced a new equilibrium that both decreased the H-reflex and improved locomotion.”
mechanismIn neurologically normal people, compensatory plasticity protecting already-satisfactory behaviours works against the long-term spinal change, which is why long-term change is smaller in them than in SCI subjects— “For behaviors such as locomotion, which are already satisfactory, these adaptations are likely to impede the long-term plasticity that decreases the H-reflex.”
mechanismInjury to the corticospinal tract, the descending pathway that conditioning depends on, weakens the immediate cortical (task-dependent) component of the reflex change in SCI subjects— “The lesser task-dependent adaptation in subjects with SCI may reflect damage to the corticospinal tract (CST), the spinal cord pathway principally responsible for H-reflex conditioning”
mechanismBetter bilateral modulation of ankle muscle activity yields more effective weight bearing and push-off, which in turn raises walking speed— “These bilateral improvements in the modulation of muscle activity controlling movement about the ankle joint probably resulted in more effective weight bearing and push off”
mechanismConditioned H-reflex change decomposes into a within-session task-dependent adaptation, read as cortical influence, plus an across-session long-term change, read as spinal cord plasticity— “Conditioned H-reflex change is composed of task-dependent adaptation (i.e., within-session difference between the control H-reflex and the conditioned H-reflex) plus long-term change (i.e., across-session change in the control H-reflex) ( Thompson et al., 2009 ).”
mechanismThe two components split differently after injury: less task-dependent adaptation, more long-term change, summing to the same total decrease as in normal subjects— “The greater decrease in the control H-reflex in the subjects with SCI combines with their lesser task-dependent adaptation to yield a decrease in the conditioned H-reflex that is identical to that found in normal subjects.”
mechanismReduced task-dependent adaptation after injury is attributed to damage to the corticospinal tract, on which reflex conditioning depends— “The lesser task-dependent adaptation in subjects with SCI may reflect damage to the corticospinal tract (CST), the spinal cord pathway principally responsible for H-reflex conditioning”
mechanismIn an uninjured cord, compensatory plasticity protects behaviours that are already satisfactory and thereby limits the long-term reflex change; after injury there is nothing to protect, so the change proceeds further— “For behaviors such as locomotion, which are already satisfactory, these adaptations are likely to impede the long-term plasticity that decreases the H-reflex.”
mechanismAcquiring the new reflex disturbs the equilibrium the injured cord had settled into, triggering wider plasticity that changes locomotion as a whole rather than one pathway— “It apparently triggered widespread adaptive plasticity that produced a new equilibrium that both decreased the H-reflex and improved locomotion.”
mechanismAfter injury the long-term change is doubly rewarded - it satisfies the conditioning criterion and it improves walking - which is offered as why it goes further than in normal subjects— “In these SCI rats, as in the subjects of this study, the long-term change in the H-reflex is doubly adaptive: it increases reward probability in the conditioning protocol, and it also improves locomotion.”
