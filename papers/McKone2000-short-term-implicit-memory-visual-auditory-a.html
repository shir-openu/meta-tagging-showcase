phenomenonshort-term repetition priming
claim-typemechanism
mechanismbecause continuous speech is ambiguous and many apparent nonwords later resolve into words, the phonological lexicon must keep pseudoword-status items active as long as word-status items, producing slow auditory decay for both — “The phonologicallexi con, therefore, has need to hold items assigned pseudo word status active for as long as those assigned word sta tus: In continuous speech, many "nonwords" will later turn out to be real words.”
mechanismbecause the written signal is far less ambiguous, the orthographic lexicon can discard a nonword as soon as the next item arrives, producing the fast visual nonword decay — “The orthographic lexicon can afford, therefore, to rapidly discard a non word when the next item is presented.”
mechanismeach stimulus is obligatorily recoded into the other perceptual form, and the secondary trace left by that recoding supports the weak cross-modal short-term priming — “The results sug gest that each stimulus undergoes obligatory recoding into the alternative perceptual form (i.e., orthographic to phonological and, more interestingly, vice versa), and that a secondary trace of this recoding can contribute to short-term priming.”
relation — “First, the results of McKone (1995) were replicated when both presentations were visual, although the list used here contained mixed auditory and visual stimuli.”
relation — “Our experiment was similar to that ofKirsner and Smith, but specifically targeted the region in which the lag X lexicality interaction is found with visual presentation.”
relation — “Few studies have used auditory targets, but we note that our small ef fect appears to conflict with Kirsner and Smith's (1974) large cross-modal reduction in long-term priming in lex ical decision (83 vs. - 14 msec, for words at lags 15 and 63).”
replicationreplicated
limitationBecause no lags beyond 6 were tested, the authors cannot show that the long-term asymptote had actually been reached for auditory items. — “Priming at lag 6 reached the 20-50 msec range, at which we might expect long-term priming to stabilize for low-frequency words, but the in clusion of no longer lags means that there is no direct ev idence that the long-term value was attained by lag 6.”
limitationThe orthographic locus previously assumed for visual short-term priming was never established directly, since words and pseudowords also differ phonologically and semantically. — “However, we have no direct evidence that the locus for written stimuli is specifically orthographic: Words and pseudowords also differ in their phonological and semantic familiarity status.”
limitationThe locus assumed in the authors' own earlier explanations is stated to have no direct evidence, and a confound is named in the same sentence. — “However, we have no direct evidence that the locus for written stimuli is specifically orthographic: Words and pseudowords also differ in their phonological and semantic familiarity status.”
limitationThe obvious definition of the measured quantity is rejected outright, because unprimed response times differ by modality, and a different baseline is substituted. — “Thus, priming cannot be taken as the difference in RTs between the first and second presentations of the same item, particularly in the cross-modal conditions. Instead, we followed the procedure of Kirsner and Smith (1974) in choosing the baseline for priming to be the mean of all unprimed trials of the appropriate lexicality and modal ity.”

content-observations: finding · quantity · variable · definition · key-term — grounded in the full text
findingWith visual stimuli, short-term priming decayed faster for nonwords than for words (a lag by lexicality interaction), whereas with auditory stimuli both item types decayed smoothly and slowly. — “Same-modality repetition priming showed a lag x lexicality inter action for visual stimuli (nonwords decayed faster), but not for auditory stimuli (longer lasting smooth decay for both words and nonwords).”
findingShort-term priming lasted longer when both presentations were spoken than when both were written. — “Second, short-term auditory priming was shown to be longer lasting than short-term visual priming.”
findingAuditory nonword priming survived at least three intervening trials (about 8 seconds), while visual nonword priming fell to its long-term level after a single intervening item. — “Third, the increased duration for auditory items was particularly striking for nonwords: whereas vi sual presentation produced decay to the long-term value with a single intervening item, auditory presentation pro duced a short-term priming effect for nonwords that en dured at least three intervening trials (8 sec) and decayed in exactly the same manner as for words.”
findingSwitching modality between prime and target reduced short-term priming but never abolished it, for words and nonwords alike. — “Fourth, short term priming was reduced by modality shift, but did not disappear completely; this general pattern was observed for both words and nonwords.”
findingCross-modal short-term priming persisted to lag 1 for word targets but disappeared after one intervening item for nonwords. — “Short-term cross modal priming endured to lag I when the target was a word, but cross-modal priming for nonwords dropped to the long-term value with a single intervening item.”
findingThe surviving cross-modal priming implies that a stimulus is recoded into the other perceptual form rather than that priming is stored centrally. — “Weak cross-modality short-tenn priming was present for words and nonwords, indicating recoding between perceptual fonns.”
findingAt lag 6, modality mismatch cut long-term priming from 31 to 2 msec for visual targets but left auditory targets essentially unaffected. — “Collapsing over lexicality, the cross-modal reduction in priming was significant for visual targets [31 vs. 2 msec, t(21) = 3.57, p < .005], but not for auditory targets (36 vs. 27 msec, t < 1).”
findingAccuracy improved rather than worsened on repeated presentations, so the reaction-time priming was not a speed-accuracy trade-off. — “Error rates dropped from 11.3% on first presentation to 6.0% on second presentation for words, and from 5.9% to 5.7% fornonwords.”
findingUnprimed lexical decisions were roughly 320-350 msec faster for written than for spoken items, so priming could not be computed as a raw first-versus-second difference. — “Unprimed responses were much faster to visual stim uli than to auditory stimuli (words: V = 620 msec, A = 966 msec; nonwords: V = 688 msec, A = 1,007 msec).”
findingResponses to an item were faster when the immediately preceding trial was in the same modality, which biases the lag 0 baseline if it is ignored. — “A post hoc split of our data showed that first presentation RTs were consistently faster to trials that matched the preceding trial in modality.”
findingThe main locus of short-term priming is the perceptual subsystem in which the item was presented: orthographic for written items, phonological for spoken ones. — “Our results from the same-modality conditions indi cate that the primary locus for short-term priming is the perceptual subsystem to which the stimulus is presented (i.e., orthographic lexicon for V, phonological lexicon for A).”
