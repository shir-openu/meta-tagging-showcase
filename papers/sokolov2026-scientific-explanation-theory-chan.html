mechanismObjectivity is produced, not presupposed: structuring methods and communities so that criticism, transparency and diverse perspectives circulate prevents any single bias or standpoint from going unchecked, which is what makes objective claims possible in a socially organised practice — “It is what becomes possible when social and methodological arrangements are structured so that no single bias or perspective can easily dominate unchecked.”
mechanismRealist commitment survives theory change because what is carried across revolutions is the structural content of superseded theories — their relational organisation, mathematical form and dependency patterns — rather than their full ontology — “If older successful theories were later rejected, perhaps what survives across scientific revolutions is not the full ontology of those theories but their structural content: the relational organization, mathematical form, or dependency patterns they captured.”
mechanismThe pluralisation of explanation was driven by the demand for rigour itself: holding accounts answerable to actual scientific practice forced the abandonment of a single universal schema — “This transformation did not occur because philosophers lost interest in rigor. It occurred because rigor itself required closer responsiveness to the sciences.”
relation — “The Stanford Encyclopedia entry on scientific realism makes this point directly by observing that debates about realism are connected to almost everything else in philosophy of science because they concern the very nature of scientific knowledge, while the entry on scientific objectivity similarly treats objectivity as an ideal that underwrites the authority of science in society.”

findingThe article's central claim is that philosophy of science should be read not as a hunt for one timeless criterion of rationality but as the study of how explanation, empirical adequacy, objectivity, progress and ontological commitment are coordinated and contested within changing scientific practice. — “The central argument advanced here is that philosophy of science is best understood not as a search for one timeless criterion of scientific rationality, but as an inquiry into how multiple epistemic ideals—explanation, empirical adequacy, objectivity, progress, and ontological commitment—are coordinated, contested, and reinterpreted within changing scientific practices.”
findingEpistemic boundaries in science are neither arbitrary nor permanent: they are negotiated limits shaped by explanatory ideals, representational practice, historical change, social organisation and domain-specific standards of success. — “The contemporary discussions surveyed in earlier blocks instead suggest that epistemic boundaries are negotiated limits.”
findingExplanation is best treated as a family of related epistemic achievements — lawful derivation, causal-mechanistic production, unification, empirical adequacy and pragmatic usefulness — rather than as one formal relation. — “Contemporary philosophy of science increasingly treats explanation as a family of related epistemic achievements rather than a single formal relation.”
findingRealism, objectivity and progress can still be defended, but only in reconstructed form: realism becomes selective and model-sensitive, objectivity becomes procedural, social and critical, and progress becomes multi-dimensional rather than cumulative. — “Realism becomes more selective and model-sensitive. Objectivity becomes procedural, social, and critical rather than purely perspective-free. Progress becomes multi-dimensional and historically informed rather than simply cumulative.”
findingProgress should be measured along several dimensions at once — empirical adequacy, explanatory reach, conceptual articulation, representational power, intervention, and critical self-correction — which need not advance together. — “Science can progress in empirical adequacy, in explanatory reach, in conceptual articulation, in representational power, in manipulation and intervention, and in critical self-correction, even if these dimensions do not always move together.”
findingThe article's twofold conclusion is that monistic images of science misdescribe the heterogeneity of its models, methods and history, and that this heterogeneity nevertheless does not license epistemic despair about scientific authority. — “First, science should not be philosophically understood through monistic images that ignore the heterogeneity of models, methods, and historical development. Second, this heterogeneity does not entail epistemic despair.”
findingBecause observation is never fully independent of theoretical commitment, explanation cannot be modelled as a one-way derivation from pure data but must be understood as organising phenomena within conceptual frameworks that themselves need assessment. — “If observation is never entirely independent of theoretical commitments, then explanation cannot be understood as a simple one-way derivation from pure empirical givens.”
findingThe article characterises the resulting stance of the field as fallibilist without cynicism, historically informed without anti-rationalism, and pluralist without indiscriminateness. — “Its characteristic posture is fallibilist but not cynical, historically informed but not anti-rational, pluralist but not indiscriminate.”
definitionscientific realism — “The contemporary SEP entry on scientific realism defines realism as a positive epistemic attitude toward the content of our best theories and models, recommending belief in both observable and unobservable aspects of the world described by the sciences.”
definitionconstructive empiricism — “The SEP entry devoted to it states van Fraassen’s core thesis plainly: science aims to give us theories that are empirically adequate, and acceptance of a theory involves belief only that it is empirically adequate.”
definitionunderdetermination — “The SEP entry on underdetermination states the core idea succinctly: the evidence available at a given time may be insufficient to determine what beliefs we should adopt.”
definitionscientific objectivity — “The SEP entry on scientific objectivity states that objectivity is an ideal according to which scientific claims, methods, and results should not be influenced by particular perspectives, values, or biases.”
definitionincommensurability — “The SEP entry on incommensurability clarifies that the term originally referred to lack of common measure and that later philosophical discussion distinguished conceptual and methodological incommensurability.”
definitionscientific progress — “The SEP entry on scientific progress defines progress as result-oriented, concerning success relative to some goal, and explicitly distinguishes it from quality in scientific work.”
definitionscientific pluralism — “The SEP entry on scientific pluralism states that plurality is now often treated not as a defect to be overcome but as a productive feature of successful science.”
definitionsyntactic, semantic and pragmatic views of theory structure — “The SEP entry on the structure of scientific theories distinguishes three major families of views: the syntactic view, the semantic view, and the pragmatic view.”
definitioncovering-law / deductive-nomological model of explanation — “The basic intuition of that model is that to explain an event is to show that it was to be expected given general laws and initial conditions.”
