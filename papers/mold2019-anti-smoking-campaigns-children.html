While childrenterm·key liked Supermanterm·key, as a recognisable American hero, and one famed in part for his fighting prowess, he personified many adults’ anxieties about declining standards in British childrenterm·key’s media circulating at this time. Many private schools rejected the Supermanterm·key packs because they did not like the comic book format ( Jacob, 1985 ). Such rejections were symptomatic of wider anxieties in the 1980sdate about the media consumption habits of childrenterm·key, with concerns frequently expressed in the adult press about the content and style of childrenterm·key’s television, with worries that it was being ‘dumbed down’, or becoming ‘too American’ and ‘too violent’ ( Buckingham, 1999 , p. 7). Worries such as these hark back to admonitions about the ‘violence and triviality’ of broadcasts voiced in the 1960s during the discussions which were framed by the 1962 Pilkington Report on the future of broadcasting ( Cabinet Office, 1962 ; House of Lords, 1962 ). They also echoed even earlier American moral panics about comic books themselves ( Lent, 1999 , pp. 9–17). Such anxieties were articulated with renewed vigor in the 1980sdate.

Supermanterm·key vs. Nick O’Teenterm·key–the materials and their meaning Although the Nick O’Teenterm·key vs. Supermanterm·key campaign appeared at a time when concerns about childrenterm·key’s media were particularly fraught, the HEC sought to utilise some of these very same modes to reach their target audience. The campaign was a multi-pronged effort that made use of a range of different media and materials. Alongside the 30-s TV cartoon commercial which aired over Christmas 1980date, there were full-page advertisements that featured in range of childrenterm·key’s comics and magazines that ran from 11 January 1981 for 10 weeks. The HEC had already paid DC Comics a licence fee to use Supermanterm·key’s image for their earlier campaign, so most of the new budget was spent on the TV advertisement, which cost £417,500 to produce ( Health Education Councilterm·key, 1979 ). The press adverts cost £32,5000 to produce. These appeared in magazines that targeted boys, such as Marvel and Roy of the Rovers ; girls, such as Jinty and Oh Boy ; and both boys and girls, such as Look In and Whizzer and Chips ( Saatchi and Saatchi, 1980date ).

The magazine-based advertisements included an invitation for childrenterm·key to join Supermanterm·key in his fight against Nick O’Teenterm·key. Childrenterm·key were asked to fill in a coupon with their name and address, and in return they would receive a pack containing: a poster; an eight-page comic book; a badge; an individually-numbered certificate stating that they had joined Supermanterm·key in his fight against Nick O’Teenterm·key; and the chance to enter a poster-making competition where successful entrants could win prises, including a Raleigh bicycle. The packs were also sent to 21,000 primary schools. The invitation to send off for a Supermanterm·key pack, the encouragement to enter a poster competition with prizes and to sign a certificate indicating they had joined the ‘fight against Nick O’Teenterm·key’ was illustrative of a participative approach to involving childrenterm·key in the campaign itself. In return for these actions, childrenterm·key received something tangible—a poster, certificate, comic book, a prize—rather than merely gaining a sense of accomplishment at having participated in some form of healthy citizenship. These actions did more than reinforce the anti-smokingterm·key message delivered by the overall campaign, or provide evidence of participation in a collective non-action (the refusal of smoking), they acted to recruit childrenterm·key into what was later described as an ‘anti-smokingterm·key lobby’ ( Health Education Councilterm·key, 1982date ). This created a sense that childrenterm·key, and their peers, had the power (and so a duty) to prevent smoking. This recruitment, into Supermanterm·key’s anti-smokingterm·key army, hints at a perception of childrenterm·key as a potentially powerful force. Placed in comparison with earlier health campaigns featuring childrenterm·key, Nick O’Teenterm·key, in imagining childrenterm·key as guardians of their own health in the present and future, and as potential citizen lobbyists on behalf of the anti-smokingterm·key campaign, presents a marked departure from earlier more passive imaginings of childrenterm·key with limited agencyterm·key under constant external threat. However, discussions within the HEC about the campaign, its planning, and its impacts, demonstrate a sustained ambivalence towards childrenterm·key’s agencyterm·key and potential gullibility.

The HEC’s attitudes to childrenterm·key as a public were on display in the campaign itself. Childrenterm·key’s media, especially didactic products such as Nick O’Teenterm·key, reveal what adults envisioned childhood was or could be because they required an adult to first construct an imagined child as an audience to be aided by the text ( Lesnik-Oberstein, 1999 ). Space precludes examining the campaign’s materials in their entirety, but an analysis of some of the images produced tells us much about how childrenterm·key were viewed. While Supermanterm·key was the creation of DC Comics, Nick O’Teenterm·key was the product of an HEC consultation with focus groups of childrenterm·key. These childrenterm·key dubbed the HEC’s first attempt at Nick O’Teenterm·key’s image ‘insufficiently evil’, resulting in changes ( Jacob, 1985 , p. 16). The HEC was willing to take the desires and expertise of childrenterm·key seriously, suggesting a view of childrenterm·key as active participantsterm·dom and not simply a group to be instructed. The resultant Nick O’Teenterm·key is curious bricolage of child-catcher tropes; the cigarette-top stove-pipe hat, dingy cloak and yellow gloves lending him a distinctly Victorian villain aesthetic, hinting at parallels between Nick O’Teenterm·key and depictions of Fagin from Charles Dickens’ Oliver Twist ( Fig. 1 ). In reaching for a salient fight between good and evil, the HEC and the childrenterm·key they consulted seem to have generated an image of stranger-danger with anti-Semitic undertones encapsulated by Dickens’ most famous Jewish villain. This characterisation of evil, as a persuasive and untrustworthy adult stranger, was not mere happenstance. While such threatening characters have appeared in childrenterm·key’s media for centuries, fears around ‘stranger-danger’ began to rise in the 1970s, with the grizzly details of child murders and exploitation making the press with increased fever as the twentieth century progressed ( Crane, 2018 ). These factual rather than fictive narratives, while primarily circulating in an adult press perpetuating a characterisation of childhood as time of increasing vulnerability, were also disseminated to childrenterm·key through education media targeted at negating a perceived increased threat of ‘stranger danger’ ( Erulkar, 1971 ). This characterisation of Nick O’Teenterm·key as a dangerous stranger, grooming childrenterm·key to smoke (while aesthetically implying worse) did more than convey evil, it also provided structure and an emotional pallet for the Supermanterm·key vs. Nick O’Teenterm·key narratives across the entire campaign. This allowed for the redeployment of familiar external threat narratives which positioned childrenterm·key as agents able to vanquish a dangerous foe, but avoided any more complex suggestions that childrenterm·key, as would-be smokers, were themselves potentially a threat to health.

Image courtesy of the Wellcome Collection Nick O’Teenterm·key: impact The Nick O’Teenterm·key campaign certainly achieved a high degree of visibility. In the first two months, 200,000 childrenterm·key requested a pack, a number that rose to 800,000 by 1982date. This equated to about one in ten of Britain’s child population. A series of surveys of random samples of childrenterm·key also suggested that the message got through to its recipients. A survey of 300 childrenterm·key who had filled in the coupon and received the pack found that 92% had retained the poster and 90% cited the message correctly or nearly correctly ( Jacob, 1985 ). Another survey, conducted in 1983, almost a year after the last phase of the campaign ended, found that 73% of childrenterm·key were able to recall the main message without prompting ( Carrick James Market Research, 1983 ). The HEC also took the fact that they had enquiries from 15 countries about the campaign, and similar efforts were mounted in Australia and Singapore, as evidence that this was a successful venture ( Health Education Councilterm·key, 1981a ). There were some elements of the evaluation and response to the campaign, however, that might have given the HEC pause. When asked why they had sent off for the pack, 55% of childrenterm·key surveyed said that it was because they liked Supermanterm·key, and 47% because they liked the anti-smokingterm·key message. When asked about the certificate, 48% saw it as enroling them in Supermanterm·key’s fight against Nick O’Teenterm·key, 19% believed it was connected with them discouraging smoking in others, and just 15% saw it as a personal pledge never to smoke ( Jacob, 1985 ). What this suggested was that some childrenterm·key may simply have sent off for the pack or put the poster on their wall because they liked Supermanterm·key, and not because they were engaged with the campaign’s message. Supermanterm·key himself was far from being a stable or neutral image strongly connected to anti-smokingterm·key. Indeed, there were other tropes associated with Supermanterm·key that may have had the opposite effect. As at least two respondents to the HEC’s campaign pointed out, candy cigarettes bearing Supermanterm·key’s image were on sale at the time of the campaign. The 1980date film Supermanterm·key II featured a single cigarette brand on 22 separate occasions ( Burki, 2017 ), and although Supermanterm·key III (1983) could be thought of has having an anti-smokingterm·key message due to a plot device involving the replacement of an element of Kryptonite with tar from a pack of cigarettes, to some extent this was undermined by the clear presentation of the cigarette brand, an example of product placement by the tobacco company in question. No matter how carefully constructed, the HEC could not control the reception of its campaign or the meanings attributed to it by those who viewed it.

Assessing the long-term impact of the campaign, and especially the extent to which it was able to prevent childrenterm·key from taking up smoking, was also difficult. This was something that the HEC were well aware of. HEC Press Officer Micheál Jacob noted that ‘evaluation of a campaign like this is difficult, since its influence on behaviour can only be measured in the future and, even then, smoking behaviour is governed by a great many factors and subject to a great many influences.’ He went on to state that ‘It would be idle to pretend, and I think it is idle to pretend, that any health educationterm·key mass media campaign on its own can make a significant long-term impact on health behaviour.’ Nonetheless, Jacob was confident that the overwhelming response by childrenterm·key, the favourable reception by schools, and the associated significant coverage by the mass media suggested ‘that the approach is one worthy of development’ ( Health Education Councilterm·key, 1981b ). This somewhat down-beat view of the potential long-term impact of the campaign was to some extent borne out by the statistics on smoking. The percentage of girls aged 11–15 years that smoked increased between 1982date and 1986, from 11% to 12%, and the number of 15-year-old girls who were regular smokers from 25% to 27%. The percentage of boys who smoked declined, from 11% of boys aged 11–15 years in 1982date, to 7% in 1986, with the number of 15-year-old boys who were regular smokers falling from 24% to 18% ( ASH, 2018 ). Too much weight should not be put on such statistics, in part due to the small numbers involved, but smoking rates amongst childrenterm·key did not change much over the course of the next 30 years, suggesting that some groups of childrenterm·key were largely impervious to anti-smokingterm·key messages.

More broadly, the Nick O’Teenterm·key campaign took place at the same time as a wider crisis of confidence in the value of health educationterm·key, and more specifically its ability to change behaviour. The Cohen committee and the establishment of the HEC in the late 1960s were indicative of optimism about health educationterm·key, a wave that continued into the 1970s with the involvement of professional advertising agencies like Saatchi and Saatchi. By the 1980sdate, however, it was becoming increasingly clear that many of the issues that health educationterm·key was supposed to reduce or prevent, such as heart disease, excessive drinking and obesity, were getting worse, not better. An editorial published in the British Medical Journal in 1982date argued that the HEC had achieved little since its establishment ( Anon, 1982date ). Health educators themselves were developing a more critical view of their work which stressed the importance of social context and rejected a sole focus on individual behaviour change ( Rodmell and Watt, 1986 ). Other approaches to understanding how the public responded to health educationterm·key messages were beginning to be developed, ones that attempted to get to grips with lay understandings of these, rather than assuming that the message was received unaltered. In a piece of research conducted shortly after the Nick O’Teenterm·key campaign concluded, a multi-disciplinary team including an anthropologist and an epidemiologist looked at the response to a health educationterm·key initiative conducted in South Wales around heart disease. The researchers found that the public interpreted the messages, and specifically the presentation of the risk of having a heart attack, within their own framework. This was related to the experience of friends and family, as well as images on TV and in magazines ( Davison, 1989 ; Davison et al., 1991 ; Frankel et al., 1991 ). The public’s understanding of public and individual health was complex, multifaceted and rooted in a wider cultural and social context ( MoldpersName et al., 2019 ). Indeed, narrow debates about ‘success’ or ‘failure’ in terms of reducing smoking rates or preventing lung cancer miss the real message of health educationterm·key: the ways in which it constructed its publics and they constructed it.

Childrenterm·key: a problematic publicterm·dom The design, delivery and reception of the Nick O’Teenterm·key campaign reveals much about the especially problematic nature of childrenterm·key as publics. Child health had long been an obsession of public health officials. Throughout the nineteenth and early twentieth century considerable effort was directed towards improving infant and maternal health ( Apple, 1995 ; Davies, 1988 ; Welshman, 1997 ). Much of this was framed around eugenic concerns about the future health of the ‘race’. There were often sharp gender divisions, with boys’ health seen through the lens of fitness and masculinity, and girls’ health around future motherhood, although this did start to change in line with broader shifts in the position of women in society ( Heggie, 2008 ; Marland, 2013 ). Notions of the future continued to be crucial to perceptions of child health, but also to childrenterm·key as future citizens in the post-war period ( King, 2016 ).

As already hinted at, ideas about the future figured centrally within the Nick O’Teenterm·key campaign. The entire initiative was aimed at prevention—a forward looking view—but a particular notion of childrenterm·key, the family, and the future was at work here. The ambivalent and multifarious attitudes to childrenterm·key as a public demonstrated by the HEC in their construction, delivery, dissemination and assessment of the Nick O’Teenterm·key campaign was indicative of the constructed and contested nature of childhood in a general sense, but also more specifically those of the 1980sdate ( Aries, 1996 , pp. 9–11; Thomson, 2013 , pp. 153–183). Childrenterm·key were deployed in the discourses of the New Right as both an emotive talisman and cornerstone of the nuclear family ( ElizabethpersName, 2016 , 2012 ). For the Conservative party under Margaret Thatcher, an idealised nuclear family, with childrenterm·key at its heart, provided both a structuring metaphor and target for New Right policies, argued for with reference to the recurring and adaptable mantra: ‘What is right for the family is right for Britain’ ( Thomas, 1993 ). Using the ‘privatised family’ as a bracketing device, the Conservative party were able to break with the post-war consensus and intimate that Welfareism was intrusive and infantilising, and thus an institution at odds with an imagined idealised ‘stable, self-reliant, moral, nuclear family’ ( Nunn, 2001 , pp. 16–17). The citizen’s relationship with the Welfare State was recast as ‘coddling’ rather than care, and the consumer society, with its emphasis on independence and responsibility, was proposed as its moral replacement ( Hall, 1983 , p. 29). This placed public health interventions, especially those seeking to intervene in the lives of childrenterm·key, and by extension the private realm of the nuclear family, on shaky ground. Instead of protecting the future through preventative policies, state interventions threatened it by incurring dependence and destabilising the nuclear family through interference.

Touring the United States as the new leader of the Conservative Party in 1975, Margaret Thatcher promised a future where the New Right would free childrenterm·key from the malevolent influences of a society controlled by those seeking ‘my rights at all costs regardless of who has to pay’ ( Thatcher, 1989 ), returning childrenterm·key to their ‘rightful owners’, outside state interference ( Nunn, 2001 ; Winter and Connolly, 1996 , p. 29). The child in this conception performed a dual representative function; firstly as an idealised powerless innocent, desirable to parents as a site onto which all moral and monetary aspirations may be bestowed and so the future guarded and shaped; and secondly as the embodiment of childhood, an undesirable intermediate time of vulnerability where all agencyterm·key belongs to one’s guardians. As a framing device, the figure of the child thus embodied many of the conflicts which lay at the heart of the ‘unstable amalgam’ between neo-liberal economics and authoritarian moral conservativism which marked the Thatcher and Major governments ( Kingdom, 1992 , p. 2). Indeed, as a consequence of this rhetoric, the needs of the child were offered up as the motive behind a number of conservative legislative moves which defined the limits of the state, the private sphere, the individual and the public good. However, despite the dominance of the morally conservative right, the 1970s and 1980sdate also saw inroads made by the childrenterm·key’s rights movement. Campaigns focused on child labour, corporal punishment and childrenterm·key’s rights in hospital and state care, successfully placed the needs of the child with agencyterm·key (as well as the passive vulnerable child), firmly on the agenda ( Franklin and Franklin, 1996 , pp. 96–97). A consequence of these fraught debates (and the legislative changes which were in some cases their consequence) was a framing and reframing of the figure of the child, and a reimagining of the limits of what was expected of it and for it. Indeed, late twentieth century legislation constructed childrenterm·key within an educational context as ‘ideally non-sexual, vulnerable and dependant’, their agencyterm·key and access to information ‘restricted or censored’ by the desires of parents, while in a medical context they were constructed ‘as “quasi-adults” entitled to confidential advice and treatment’ able to refuse or accept medical interventions through informed consent ( Blair and Monk, 2009 ). While vulnerable conceptions of the child certainly persisted and were reinforced in this period, the 1970s and 1980sdate also saw the child imagined anew as a discerning consumer and possessor of rights.

This multiplication of possibilities is evident in the Nick O’Teenterm·key vs. Supermanterm·key campaign. The vulnerable child certainly appears in the Nick O’Teenterm·key narrative, but a child with agencyterm·key, and so the moral responsibility to join Supermanterm·key’s fight against smoking, also emerges in the text. Furthermore, a child who consumes comics and can access a certain authority within its family, and is able to persuade parents and others not to smoke, is also discernible in the HEC campaign files. A conflicting view of the child was therefore in operation. This child was simultaneously presented as vulnerable to persuasion and in need of protection, but also potentially an anti-smokingterm·key lobbyist; as a bad future consumer of cigarettes and potential threat to public health, and as a good obedient future citizen who refuses to smoke. Running alongside and interacting with the Nick O’Teenterm·key’s campaign’s construction of the future was another narrative about childrenterm·key as a group to be protected from external threats and as citizens with agencyterm·key. On one level, the Nick O’Teenterm·key materials told a story about childrenterm·key being tricked by an evil force into smoking, a danger that they were rescued from by the heroic figure of Supermanterm·key. Without his intervention, it is implied, the childrenterm·key would have started smoking. But there were clear notions of agencyterm·key at work here too. The implementation and delivery of the campaign involved childrenterm·key in the ‘fight’ against Nick O’Teenterm·key. Childrenterm·key were not just passive recipientsterm·dom, but active participantsterm·dom in the campaign. Moreover, childrenterm·key were seen as being able to recruit other childrenterm·key in this fight, and even work towards influencing their parents’ smoking behaviour. The view of childrenterm·key as expressed in the Nick O’Teenterm·key campaign was thus paradoxical: they were simultaneously helpless dupes and active agents.

Conclusion The paradoxes and conflicts that ran through the Nick O’Teenterm·key vs. Supermanterm·key campaign were not unique to this campaign or its imagined public. After nearly twenty years of effort, doubts about the ability of health educationterm·key to change individual behaviour were beginning to set in. These concerns revolved not only around the effectiveness of the materials, but about the agencyterm·key of the publics who interacted with them. The public could ignore, resist or re-appropriate health educationterm·key messages. In many ways, the same was true of campaigns like Nick O’Teenterm·key vs. Supermanterm·key that focused on childrenterm·key and young people, only more so. Fears about the potential vulnerability of childrenterm·key on the one hand, and their unruly nature on the other, were an exaggerated version of concerns about the public as a whole. The child-public anticipated by the Nick O’Teenterm·key campaign was also reflective of the demographic targeted and influences of the time at which it was being constructed. Unlike child health educationterm·key campaigns of the past, the Nick O’Teenterm·key campaign ascribed childrenterm·key a greater degree of capacity to make their own decisions and influence others. Yet, the agencyterm·key of this child-public was constrained, limited by longer-running fears about the vulnerability of childrenterm·key and their futures. Broader concerns about the nature of childhood in the 1980sdate were also on display, especially around the breakdown of the nuclear family and the growing sense of childrenterm·key as individuals with rights and the ability to exercise these. These somewhat contradictory concerns about the danger of youthful agencyterm·key and the vulnerability of childhood can also be found in later 1980sdate and 1990s health campaigns targeted at childrenterm·key and adolescents. For example, in AIDS-related education materials we see childrenterm·key forewarned against the dangers of unprotected sex (an external threat), without being provided with any real explanation of the safer-sex practices which might protect them for fear that such knowledge might engender undesirable sexual behaviours ( ElizabethpersName, 2016 ).

The Nick O’Teenterm·key vs. Supermanterm·key campaign took place at a critical juncture not only for conceptualisations of childrenterm·key, but of the wider public and its health too. As the growing disillusionment with health educationterm·key focused on individual behaviour change made clear, the idea that a single-unitary public could be made healthier through such campaigns was beginning to breakdown. Although public health practitioners had long been aware that there was not one public but many, campaigns like Nick O’Teenterm·key vs. Supermanterm·key were increasingly being designed in such a way that allowed for the agencyterm·key of their recipients. In this, then, we can see signs of moves towards a reimaging of the public as a set of what Hinchcliffe and colleagues call ‘healthy publicsterm·key’. Although views of the target audience of the Nick O’Teenterm·key campaign were still some way from the notion of ‘healthy publicsterm·key’, the HEC’s desire to work with childrenterm·key in designing the campaign and to involve them in its delivery point to the development of a more sophisticated understanding of ‘the public’ or particular publics. Yet, this complex conceptualisation of multiple publics interacted with another set of tendencies that served to flatten ‘the public’ and its interests. Claims made in the name of defending or improving ‘public health’ continued to skirt over the diversity of publics and needs involved. Supermanterm·key may have defeated Nick O’Teenterm·key, but the battle for who defines the health of the public, and how this should be achieved, was far from over.
