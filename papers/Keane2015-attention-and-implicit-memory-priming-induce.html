phenomenonpriming-induced performance costs and benefits in picture naming— “The present study examined the effect of divided attention on priming-induced performance costs and benefits in a speeded picture-naming task.”
claim-typeexistence— “Experiment 1 shows that the costs, but not the benefits, are eliminated by division of attention at encoding.”
method — evidence— “In the implicit memory task, attention at encoding (full vs. divided) was manipulated between groups, and study condition (old vs. new vs. lure) was manipulated within groups.”
mechanismA lure picture activates the perceptually similar studied item as a competing incorrect response alongside the correct one, so naming requires selection among alternatives.— “By contrast, the lure condition (which elicits the cost) resembles production priming tasks in that it entails response competition at test: Because participants have studied a picture that is perceptually similar to the test cue, it is likely that the test cue elicits activation of that (incorrect) response, as well as the correct (but…
mechanismBecause only the cost involves response competition at test, only the cost depends on attention at encoding, in line with the identification-production account.— “Thus, the absence of an attentional effect for the benefit, and the presence of such an effect for the cost, may be linked to the absence or presence of response competition at test, respectively, in accord with the identification-production account.”
contradicts unitary-source accounts of priming costs and benefits (Rouder, Ratcliff & McKoon, 2000; Marsolek, 2008)— “The present finding that costs and benefits in priming are differentially affected by divided attention therefore poses a challenge to both theories insofar as it suggests that the source of these two effects, or the process by which they are manifested, is not identical.”
replicates Gabrieli et al. (1999) on divided attention not reducing the picture-naming priming benefit— “This aspect of our results is consistent with prior findings in a picture-naming task ( Gabrieli, et al., 1999 ).”
replicates Cave & Squire (1992) and Verfaellie et al. (1996) on normal picture-naming priming benefits in amnesia— “Prior studies have shown that the priming-induced performance benefit in picture naming is normal in amnesia ( Cave & Squire, 1992 ; Verfaellie, Gabrieli, Vaidya, Croce, & Reminger, 1996 ), and we expected to replicate that finding in Experiment 2.”
sensePRIMING WITH THE SIGN REVERSED, and a field-level gap named in the abstract: the literature has focused EXCLUSIVELY on priming effects that are beneficial and has OVERLOOKED those that are costly, `wherein performance is harmed by prior exposure to task stimuli`. Nearly every other priming record in this corpus reports a facilitation. If `priming` is defined as an influence of prior exposure rather than as a benefit, then half the phenomenon has gone unstudied because of what the word suggests— “This work, however, has focused exclusively on priming effects that are beneficial in nature (wherein performance is enhanced by prior exposure to task stimuli), and has overlooked priming effects that are costly in nature (wherein performance is harmed by prior exposure to task stimuli)”
sensecost and benefit as a SYMMETRIC PAIR of operationalisations - both are response times against the same NEW baseline, in the same task, from the same study phase: benefit = faster to OLD than to new, cost = slower to LURE than to new. Because the two share a baseline and differ only in the relation between studied and test item (identical versus merely similar), a dissociation between them cannot be explained by task or by measure— “The performance benefit in this task is manifested as faster response times in the test phase to old than to new pictures, and the cost as slower response times to lure than to new pictures”
sensethe amnesia control, and it is chosen for the ALTERNATIVE IT KILLS. Dividing attention damages explicit memory as well, so eliminating the cost that way leaves open that the cost was explicit all along. Amnesic participants have no explicit memory to lose and the costs are INTACT - so the Experiment 1 result cannot be an artifact of reduced explicit memory. A population used as a control condition rather than as a subject of study— “Experiment 2 shows that the costs (as well as the benefits) in this task are intact in amnesic participants, demonstrating that the elimination of the cost in the divided attention condition in Experiment 1 was not an artifact of the reduced availability of explicit memory in that condition”
senseattention required WHERE A SELECTION MUST BE MADE - the paper's account of why one effect needs attention and the other does not is not about strength or depth but about whether the test cue leaves a CHOICE. The lure activates a wrong response alongside the right one; the old item points at a single correct response. Offered as a suggestion (`We suggest`, `may be linked`), and weighted here as one— “Thus, the absence of an attentional effect for the benefit, and the presence of such an effect for the cost, may be linked to the absence or presence of response competition at test, respectively, in accord with the identification-production account”
controlthree FILLER PICTURES resembling none of the critical stimuli placed at the beginning and end of the study list — held — rules out that memory for the critical items is inflated or depressed by primacy and recency— “Three filler items (pictures that did not resemble any of the critical stimuli) appeared at the beginning and end of the list to blunt any primacy and recency effects on later memory for the stimuli.”
controlitems counterbalanced across participants so each pair appeared equally often in the old, lure and new conditions — held — rules out that a condition effect is an item effect - that the old, lure and new sets simply contain different pictures— “Across participants, items were counterbalanced across conditions such that each pair appeared equally often in the old, lure, and new conditions.”
controlwhich member of each pair became the target and which the mate was decided at random — held — rules out that the target items are systematically more memorable than their mates— “(See sample stimuli in Figure 1 .) We randomly designated one member of each pair as the “target” item (which was the item that was always presented in the test phase) and the other member as the “mate.” The 30 pairs were divided into three lists of 10 for purposes of counterbalancing across the old, new and lure conditions.”
