phenomenonhippocampal ensemble reactivation during sleep
claim-typeexistence
mechanismWaking experience drives associative synaptic modification, which leaves cells that were coactive during behaviour more likely to be reactivated together in subsequent slow-wave sleep — “This effect, which declined gradually during each post-behaviorsleep session, may resultfrom synaptic modificationduringwaking experience.”
mechanismEvent memory is first stored by rapid synaptic modification in the hippocampus, and is then replayed during slow-wave sleep (when further hippocampal modification is suppressed) so that the information is gradually transferred to neocortex — “During subsequent slow-wave sleep, synaptic moditication within the hippocampus itself is suppressed (16) and the neuronal states encoded within the hippocampus are "played back" as part of a consolidation process by which hippocampal information is gradually transferred to, the neocortex (6, 17).”
mechanismInitial storage of the event occurs through rapid synaptic modification primarily within the hippocampus itself — “According to the proposed hypothesis, initial storage of event memory occurs through rapid synaptic modification, primarily within the hippocampus.”
relation — “This difTerence is consistent with previous theoretical models of autoassociative memory (6), in which recurrent excitation is used to reproduce a previously stored activity pattern, even when the input contains very little of the relevant information.”
replicationuntested
limitationThe increased correlation implies associative synaptic change somewhere in the system, but not necessarily inside the hippocampus. — “The inaeased melation is consistent with an underlying associative synaptic modification at some stage in the system, although not necessarily in the hippocampus.”
limitationNeocortical or subcortical changes are an alternative source of the increased correlations. — “Changes in neocortical or subcortical pathways might underlie the increased correlations.”

findingHippocampal CA1 cells that fired together during a spatial behavioural task showed increased tendency to fire together in the sleep that followed, relative to the sleep that preceded the task. — “Cells that fired together when the animal occupied particular locations in the environment exhibitedan increasedtendencyto fire together duringsubsequent sleep, in comparison to sleep episodes precedingthe behavioraltasks.”
findingThe increase was specific to coactive pairs: cells silent during behaviour, and active cells whose place fields did not overlap, showed no such increase. — “Cells that were inactive during behavior, or that were active but had non-overlappingspatial firing, did not show this increase.”
findingCoactive (overlapping place field) pairs rose significantly above their pre-behaviour correlation level, whereas pairs of spatially firing cells with non-overlapping fields did not. — “Cells that were coactive during behavior showed a significant increase in correlation from their PRE level compared to pairs in which both cells displayed spatial firing but not in the same region of space (11).”
findingThe reactivation effect decayed across the post-behaviour sleep session with a time constant of about 12 minutes. — “This selective reactivation of correlated states declined during the POST phase with a time constant of approximately 12 min (1 2).”
findingThe sleep correlations were concentrated in the synchronized population bursts (ripples) rather than in the intervals between them. — “Correlations were signiiicantly larger during ripples than in the intervening intervals (14).”
findingDuring running, cells with overlapping place fields were strongly positively correlated while non-overlapping pairs were not, confirming the grouping used for the sleep comparison. — “During the RUN phase, as expected, cells with overlapping place fields exhibited highly positively correlated activity, whereas cell pairs with nonoverlapping fields did not exhibit such activity (Fig. 3).”
