limitationThe paper's results are correlational; it explicitly declines to claim the causal mechanisms it speculates about. — “While our results cannot confirm such causal links, they nonetheless highlight a new role for frequency and polysemy in language change and the importance of distributional models in historical research.”
limitationThe polysemy proxy (local clustering coefficient in a PPMI co-occurrence network) is biased towards rating contextually diverse function words as highly polysemous. — “However, we found that it was slightly biased towards rating contextually diverse discourse function words (e.g., also) as highly polysemous, which needs to be taken into account when interpreting our results.”
limitationPolysemy has no agreed ground truth to validate the measure against. — “Measuring word polysemy is a difficult and fraught task, as even “ground truth” dictionaries differ in the number of senses they assign to words (Simpson et al., 1989; Fellbaum, 1998).”
limitationThe main corpora contain artifacts from shifting sampling biases over time. — “The Google N-Gram datasets are extremely large (comprising≈6% of all books ever published), but they also contain many corpus artifacts due, e.g., to shifting sampling biases over time (Pechenick et al., 2015).”
limitationPart of the polysemy result (the negative curvature at high polysemy) is not robust across datasets or methods. — “The negative curvature for polysemy—which is significant only at high d(wi)—varies across datasets and was not present with SGNS, so it is not as robust as the clear linear trend that was seen with all methods and across all datasets.”
limitationThe paper's second law is stated in two logically different forms, two pages apart. — “independent of frequency, words that are more polysemous have higher rates of semantic change”
limitationOne of the three methods is stated to be prone to false discoveries. — “PPMI is clearly worse than the other two methods; it performs poorly on all the benchmark tasks, is extremely sensitive to rare events, and is prone to false discoveries from global genre shifts”
limitationThe choice between the two remaining methods is left unsettled by the paper's own benchmarks. — “Between SVD and SGNS the results are somewhat equivocal, as both perform best on two out of the four tasks”

findingUsing diachronic word embeddings (word2vec/PPMI/SVD) over six corpora, four languages, two centuries, two statistical laws of semantic change emerge: (i) law of conformity — change rate scales with an inverse power-law of word frequency; (ii) law of innovation — more polysemous words change faster, independent of frequency. — “we propose two quantitative laws of seman- tic change: (i) the law of conformity—the rate of semantic change scales with an inverse power-law of word frequency; (ii) the law of innovation—independent of frequency, words that are more polysemous have higher rates of semantic change.”
