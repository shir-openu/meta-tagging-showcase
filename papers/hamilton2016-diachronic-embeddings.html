Our work builds upon a wealth of previous research on quantitative approaches to semantic changeterm·dom, including prior work with distributional methods (Sagi et al., 2011; Wijaya and Yeniterzi, 2011; Gulordava and Baroni, 2011; Jatowt and Duh, 2014; Kulkarni et al., 2014; Xu and Kemp, 2015), as well as recent work on detecting the emergence of novel wordterm·key senses (Lau et al., 2012; Mitra et al., 2014; Cook et al., 2014; Mitra et al., 2015; Frermann and Lapata, 2016). We extend these lines of work by rigorously comparing different approaches to quantifying semantic changeterm·dom and by using these methods to propose new statistical laws of semantic changeterm·dom. The two statistical laws we propose have strong implications for future work in historicalterm·key semantics. The law of conformityterm·dom —frequent words changeterm·key more slowly—clarifies frequencyterm·key’s role in semantic changeterm·dom. Future studies of semantic changeterm·dom must account for frequencyterm·key’s conforming effect: when examining the interaction between some linguistic process and semantic changeterm·dom, the law of conformityterm·dom should serve as a null model in which the interaction is driven primarily by underlying frequencyterm·key effects. The law of innovationterm·dom —polysemousterm·key words changeterm·key more quickly—quantifies the central role polysemyterm·dom plays in semantic changeterm·dom, an issue that has concerned linguists for more than 100 years (Br´eal, 1897). Previous works argued that semantic changeterm·dom leads to polysemyterm·dom (Wilkins, 1993; Hopper and Traugott, 2003). However, our results show that polysemousterm·key words changeterm·key faster, which suggests that polysemyterm·dom may actually lead to semantic changeterm·dom. Overall, these two factors—frequencyterm·key and polysemyterm·dom—explain between 48% and 88% of the variance10 in rates of semantic changeterm·dom (across conditions). This remarkable degree of explanatory power indicates that frequencyterm·key and polysemyterm·dom are perhaps the two most crucial linguistic factors that explain rates of semantic changeterm·dom over time. These empirical statistical laws also lend themselves to various causal mechanisms. The law of conformityterm·dom might be a consequence of learning: perhaps people are more likely to use rare words mistakenly in novel ways, a mechanism formalizable by Bayesianterm·stat models of wordterm·key learning and corresponding to the biological notion of genetic drift (Reali and Griffiths, 2010). Or perhaps a sociocultural conformity bias makes people less likely to accept novel innovations of common words, a mechanism analogous to the biological process of purifying selection (Boyd and Richerson, 1988; Pagel et al., 2007). Moreover, such mechanisms may also be partially responsible for the law of innovationterm·dom. Highly polysemousterm·key words tend to have more rare senses (Kilgarriff, 2004), and rare senses may be unstable by thelaw of conformity. While our results cannot confirm such causal links, they nonetheless highlight a new role for frequencyterm·key and polysemyterm·dom in language changeterm·key and the importance of distributional models in historicalterm·key research.
