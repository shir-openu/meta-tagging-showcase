The clustering analysis indicated that the population of neurons as a whole did sustain the colorterm informationterm throughout the entire trial ( Figure 3A ). Higher loads produce divisive normalizationterm·def-like neuronal responses

We next wanted to understand the neuronal mechanisms behind the informationterm loss at higher WMterm·dom loads. For that, we analyzed how the responses of individual sampleterm and delayterm neurons changed when the loadterm increased from one colorterm to two colors. For the ‘sampleterm populations’ and the ‘delayterm populations’, an increasing number of items reduced the amount of encoded informationterm about the colorterm identity ( Figure 4 ). This effect was due to neurons that had a large difference of firing rates between the colorterm A and colorterm B at loadterm 1 (high PEV, i.e., informationterm about colorterm), and reduced differentiation at loadterm 2 (small PEV, no or little informationterm about colorterm, e.g., Figure 2A ). ‘Divisive-normalizationterm·dom-like regularization’ (DNRterm·mth; Carandini and Heeger, 2011 ) can explain this effect. DNRterm·mth describes the computation that takes place when two stimuli are presented simultaneously. In a simplified case a neuronal response becomes normalized, analogous to vector normalizationterm·dom, with a normalizationterm·dom factor consisting of the simultaneous stimuli ( Carandini and Heeger, 2011 ). Applied to our context, a consequence of DNRterm·mth would be a reduced differentiation between two colorterm identities at loadterm 2 because differences in firing rate (for each stimulus by itself) at loadterm 1 would be normalized at loadterm 2 (resulting in informationterm loss, e.g., Figure 2A ). We, therefore, hypothesized that DNRterm·mth was observable for neurons with significantterm informationterm at loadterm 1. We tested for DNRterm·mth in the NCLterm by calculating a selectivity index (SE) and a sensory interactionterm index (SI) for each neuron for the sampleterm phase and the memory delayterm phase ( Reynolds et al., 1999 , see Materials and methods for details). SE indicates how strongly the neuronal response is driven by a colorterm at the favorite location of the neuron (reference) in relation to a selected probe colorterm (when either is presented alone). SI indicates how the probe colorterm interacts with the reference colorterm when both are presented simultaneously. Values of both indices, SE and SI, lie between –1 and +1. The addition of a probe colorterm influences the response to the reference colorterm by either suppressing the firing rate of the reference colorterm (if the reference elicits a higher firing rate than the probe, i.e., SE <0), or increasing the firing rate for the reference colorterm (if the probe elicits a higher firing rate than the reference, i.e., SE >0). If DNRterm·mth was present, this influence to suppress or enhance neuronal responses should be an even mixture at the population level, resulting in a significantterm regression between SE and SI with a slopeterm of around 0.5 ( Bouchacourt and Buschman, 2019 ). We compared regressions for the sampleterm and delayterm phase (each as one bin, see Materials and methods for details) for two groups of neurons: informationterm-carrying neurons (significantterm informationterm at loadterm 1), and non-informative neurons (no informationterm at loadterm 1 or at loadterm 2; Figure 5—figure supplement 1 ). We found that DNRterm·mth was present in both sampleterm and delayterm phases ( Figure 5A ). Informationterm-carrying sampleterm neurons had a fitted slopeterm of 0.47 ( R 2 adj 0.39, F (1,838) = 547.69, p < 0.001, CI = [0.43 0.51]) and delayterm neurons had a slopeterm of 0.50 ( R 2 adj 0.34, F (1,342) = 175.60, p < 0.001, CI = [0.43 0.58]). As the slopes were not significantly different from 0.5, this indicates that reference and probe colorterm had an equal influence on neuronal responses. We thus show that DNRterm·mth was observable in the neuronal population, and as a consequence of this computation, neurons had generally less informationterm about the colorterm identity at loadterm 2.

Gain of informationterm at loadterm 2 can be explained by neuronal normalizationterm·dom

Some neurons showed encoding of colorterm identity at higher loads, instead of loss of informationterm. These neurons were abundant in both the sampleterm phase and the delayterm phase ( Figure 3C ). For example, the neuron shown in Figure 2B did not differentiate between colorterm identities at loadterm 1 but did so for loadterm 2, thus, representing a case of informationterm gain (instead of loss) at a higher loadterm. We wanted to understand if DNRterm·mth, the mechanism that we found reduced colorterm informationterm at loadterm 2, could also produce colorterm differentiation. The ‘normalizationterm·dom modelterm of attentionterm·dom’ ( Reynolds and Heeger, 2009 ) incorporates divisive normalizationterm·def, and can explain how attentionterm·dom can modulate neuronal responses. By attending a preferred (or non-preferred) second colored square in the loadterm 2 condition the neuronal response of a neuron to the target location (i.e., to colorterm A and to colorterm B at the favorite location) might be altered. As a result a difference between colorterm A and B may arise even though each colorterm by itself elicited a similar response. In other words, the interactionterm between the additional colorterm and the target colorterm is unequal. Neurons without a colorterm differentiation at loadterm 1 that gained differentiation at loadterm 2 through this process (e.g., if the interactionterm of probe colorterm A with reference colorterm A is larger than the interactionterm of probe colorterm A with reference colorterm B, see Figure 5—figure supplement 2 for an example) should have a population regression slopeterm smaller than 0.5. We thus hypothesized that the population of neurons showing informationterm at loadterm 2, but not at loadterm 1 (e.g., Figure 2B ), would have a smaller slopeterm than the neurons that lost informationterm ( Figure 5A ). Sampleterm neurons had a slopeterm of 0.19 ( R 2 adj 0.05, F (1,446) = 23.0, p < 0.001, CI = [0.11 0.27], Figure 5B ), and delayterm neurons had a slopeterm of –0.04 ( R 2 adj –0.015, F (1,62) = 0.08, p = 0.78, CI = [–0.29 0.22], Figure 5B ). Both slopes were significantly smaller than 0.5 and smaller than the slopes of the non-informative neurons ( Figure 5—figure supplement 1 ). This indicates that these neurons were influenced more strongly by the reference colorterm, and that the addition of the probe colorterm at loadterm 2 resulted in an unequal interactionterm. Therefore, DNRterm·mth was also computationally responsible for a gain of informationterm at loadterm 2, in a specific subset of neurons.

In brief, we found that the WMterm·dom capacityterm·dom of crows is limited to about four items, and that the two visual hemifields are largely independent (i.e., the number of items on one side does not affect change detection performance on the other side). Within each hemifieldterm, performance dropped gradually with the addition of a second and third item but remained above chance. Fittingly, on the neuronal level, we found a markedly reduced amount of colorterm informationterm when the number of colored squares was increased from one to two (roughly 50% reduction in correct trials). This suggests that WMterm·dom could be conceptualized as a continuous resourceterm·dom that has to be divided between the two items ( Bays and Husain, 2008 ; van den Berg et al., 2012 ; Wilken and Ma, 2004 ), rather than two ‘simple’ slots that would each have the same amount of informationterm irrespective of the memory loadterm. This is also consistent with results of human neuroimaging that report decreased signal amplitude and precision with increasing memory loadterm ( Emrich et al., 2013 ; Sprague et al., 2014 ). In contrast, the hemispheric independence we observed would fit a slotterm·dom-like modelterm, in which the hemispheres as a whole act like discrete slots. A more nuanced version of the slotterm·dom modelterm (‘slots and averaging’; Zhang and Luck, 2008 ) could also account for graded amounts of informationterm within a limited number of slots ( Fukuda et al., 2010 ; Zhang and Luck, 2008 ), as we found here. The mix of discrete and independent hemispheres with a graded allocation of informationterm between items that we found is comparable to results by Buschman et al., 2011 , observed in monkeyterm PFCterm. On the neuronal level, recurrent connections between neurons within a hemisphere may reduce item differentiation when multiple items are present simultaneously, creating capacityterm·dom limitations within the hemisphere ( Matsushima and Tanaka, 2014 ). A lack of interhemispheric recurrent connections would make processing in the other hemisphere independent. Like in monkeys, WMterm·dom capacityterm·dom in crows may therefore result from neuronal activity patterns governed by multiple individual items. We probed the WMterm·dom capacityterm·dom of crows using colored squares, based on the taskterm design of Buschman et al., 2011 . Using the identical taskterm allowed us to directly compare our neuronal results of WMterm·dom capacityterm·dom from NCLterm to results from PFCterm of monkeys. Taskterm similarity is very important for such cross species comparisons as even small changes in taskterm parameters may introduce substantial differences in neuronal responses, leading to potentially different conclusions. In a taskterm similar to the one used here, Lara and Wallis, 2014 , have found that neurons in the PFCterm of monkeys encoded nearly no informationterm about colorterm, but instead about location. In their taskterm monkeys had to memorize the colorterm of squares at two locations on a screen, and were again confronted with a colored square at one of the two locations after a delayterm. The monkeys then had to indicate if the colorterm at that location had changed. Lara and Wallis, 2014 , discuss the absence of colorterm informationterm in the neurons they recorded in relation to the taskterm of Buschman et al., 2011 , who like us, did find colorterm informationterm. In brief, the exact taskterm design may determine the neuronal encoding of taskterm relevant informationterm ( Lara and Wallis, 2014 ). Similar to the complex contribution of PFCterm neurons to WMterm·dom, neurons of NCLterm can also encode a wide range of very different taskterm relevant aspects, like colorterm (this study), spatial locations ( Rinnert et al., 2019 ; Veit et al., 2017 ), and more abstract items like rules ( Veit and NiederpersName, 2013 ) and numerosities ( Ditz and NiederpersName, 2015 ).

Attentional processes guide WMterm·dom allocation and maintenance

One way to circumvent WMterm·dom failure when item loadterm increases is to allocate attentionterm·dom. Our results suggest that attentionterm·dom may play an important role in crowterm WMterm·dom. Capacityterm·dom limitation became apparent during encoding, as the amount of informationterm at the end of the sampleterm period was affected by the stimulus loadterm. Adding a second and third item to the ipsilateralterm stimulus array reduced the amount of colorterm informationterm encoded by NCLterm neurons that carried over into the memory delayterm. Furthermore, neuronal activity in trials in which the birds made an incorrect response showed only weak encoding during the sampleterm phase without informationterm maintenance during the memory delayterm. This fits studies of human WMterm·dom that have shown attentive filtering during encoding of stimuli influencing WMterm·dom capacityterm·dom ( Bays and Husain, 2008 ; Vogel et al., 2005 ; Vogel and Machizawa, 2004 ), and neuronal correlates of this have been reported for monkeys as well ( Buschman et al., 2011 ). Beyond the domain of sensory signals, attentionterm·dom and WMterm·dom may be directly linked. Neuronal correlates of WMterm·dom and attentionterm·dom overlap in PFCterm neurons, for example, Lebedev et al., 2004 , found that a substantial amount of PFCterm neurons encode either an attentional signal, or a memory signal, and some (hybrid) neurons do both. A purely mnemonic function of PFCterm thereby seems unlikely. Indeed, very recently, Panichello and Buschman, 2021 , have reported that at the population level neurons of PFCterm encode ‘both the selection of items from working memory and attentionterm·dom to sensory inputs’ (p. 2), rather than just memory content. The independence of hemifields that we observed on the behavioral level (this study and BalakhonovpersName and RosepersName, 2017 ) and found in the neuronal responses could also be related to attentionterm·dom. Adding stimuli in the contralateralterm hemifieldterm affected neither performance nor informationterm maintained by NCLterm neurons, whereas additional ipsilateralterm stimuli strongly reduced both. This fits the influence of attentionterm·dom on WMterm·dom and hemifieldterm independence, which is consistently accentuated in studies in which attentionterm·dom had to be divided between the two hemifields ( Alvarez and Cavanagh, 2005 ; Buschman et al., 2011 ; Cavanagh and Alvarez, 2005 ; Delvenne, 2005 ; Delvenne et al., 2011 ). Finally, the DNRterm·mth computation may explain the responses of the neurons that gained informationterm at loadterm 2 through attentional processes predicted by the ‘normalizationterm·dom modelterm of attentionterm·dom’ ( Reynolds and Heeger, 2009 ). This may appear counter-intuitive and contradictory, considering that the same process is also responsible for the loss of informationterm. However, when attentionterm·dom is overtly directed to a specific (preferred or non-preferred) item within the receptive field of a neuron, the DNRterm·mth computation shifts its weighting of the normalized response toward the response of the attended item ( Reynolds et al., 1999 ; Reynolds and Heeger, 2009 ). This weighted normalizationterm·dom can produce a difference in the neuronal response to both colorterm identities at loadterm 2, even if the neuronal response was non-informative at loadterm 1. At the population level we were able to observe such an effect as the reduced slopeterm of the selectivity/interactionterm fit. Thus, an attentive process might have enhanced informationterm in WMterm·dom at higher loads. It is important to clarify that, as we did not use any form of attentional cueing in our study, we cannot explicitly test for such an attentionterm·dom effect. However, we do know that the animals participating in this study can use attentional cues to enhance their WMterm·dom ( FongaropersName and RosepersName, 2020 ). The attentionterm·dom cues used by FongaropersName and RosepersName, 2020 , positively affected not only encoding but also the maintenance and retrieval of the informationterm held in WMterm·dom, comparable to results from monkeys and humans ( Brady and Hampton, 2018 ; Souza and Oberauer, 2016 ). We, therefore, want to emphasize that our data is in line with the interpretation that the birds possibly attended a loadterm 2 stimulus array differently than a loadterm 1 stimulus array in order to enhance their performance in trials with higher loads.

Modern models of mammalianterm WMterm·dom capacityterm·dom are applicable to crows

Our neuronal recordings offer a mechanistic explanation for the behavioral effects, as we found clear evidence of DNRterm·mth governing the neuronal responses tied to WMterm·dom capacityterm·dom that is in accordance with mammalianterm models of WMterm·dom capacityterm·dom ( Bouchacourt and Buschman, 2019 ; Lundqvist et al., 2016 ; Lundqvist et al., 2011 ). The loss of informationterm about colorterm identity (i.e., neuronal response differentiation between colors) can be accounted for by DNRterm·mth when an item is added to a neuron’s receptive field. The normalizationterm·dom of neuronal firing rate diminishes the differentiation between colorterm identities. As such it is analogous to neurophysiological responses from visual areas ( Carandini et al., 1997 ; Reynolds et al., 1999 ) and to the PFCterm during spatial WMterm·dom ( Matsushima and Tanaka, 2014 ). The WMterm·dom modelterm of Bouchacourt and Buschman, 2019 , is based solely on data from monkeyterm electrophysiologyterm·mth, and thus implicitly tied to the layered columns of the neocortexterm. The results we report here show that the modelterm also fits the neurophysiology of WMterm·dom in crows. However, the picture is incomplete since important aspects of monkeys’ WMterm·dom are still not investigated in crows. Oscillations of local field potentials are relevant for how informationterm enters WMterm·dom and how it is maintained ( Miller et al., 2018 ), and have been tied to normalizationterm·dom and competition between items in WMterm·dom ( Lundqvist et al., 2018b ). Thus, the oscillatory interplay of the layers and different regions of the mammalianterm neocortexterm are important fields of research to further our understanding of WMterm·dom. Such aspects are so far completely unknown in crows and their non-layered associative areas. This encourages further investigation into the neuronal circuits of WMterm·dom in birds. There is also ongoing debate about the role of sustained activity during delayterm periods and how it relates to WMterm·dom ( Constantinidis et al., 2018 ; Lundqvist et al., 2018a ; Miller et al., 2018 ). We cannot report of any neuron that showed persistentterm activity comparable to those reported by classical WMterm·dom studies in PFCterm (e.g., Funahashi et al., 1989 ; Fuster and Alexander, 1971 ), or in NCLterm ( Diekamp et al., 2002b ; Veit et al., 2014 ; Veit and NiederpersName, 2013 ). This may be reconcilable with some other contemporary models of WMterm·dom. One major type of those models implements ‘synfire chains’, where individual neurons fire sequentially (and transiently) to bridge temporal gaps and maintain taskterm relevant contents ( Rajan et al., 2016 ). This has, for example, been reported to be the case in posterior parietal cortex of mice performing a T-maze taskterm that required WMterm·dom for cued spatial locations to be maintained ( Harvey et al., 2012 ). The transient activity of neurons that we report ( Figures 2 and 3A ) might fit into such models. However, our results can only be compared very cautiously to this (since even small changes in taskterm design significantly alter neuronal responses, e.g., Lara and Wallis, 2014 ). Therefore, while we cannot, yet, fully equate crowterm and monkeyterm WMterm·dom, our results raise two important questions about how WMterm·dom is implemented on the level of neuronal networks that have implications for our comparative view of crowterm WMterm·dom. The first regards the neuronal computations underlying WMterm·dom. Is there a common canonicalterm computation governing WMterm·dom, or are there different solutions based on different neuronal architectures? Recent work has shown that the sensory areas of mammals and birds show remarkably similar circuit organization ( Stacho et al., 2020 ). However, higher-order associative areas involved in WMterm·dom, like the LPFC in mammals and the NCLterm in birds, have distinctly different architectures ( Stacho et al., 2020 ). The fact that differently organized areas like LPFC and NCLterm produce strikingly similar physiological responses points to shared computational principles. Modeling work already suggests that the competing WMterm·dom capacityterm·dom models can be accommodated into a unifying framework based on theoretical neuronal informationterm sampling, where stochastic informationterm sampling (assumed for continuous resourceterm·dom models) can account for item limitations better than fixed informationterm sampling (assumed by the slots and averaging models) ( Schneegans et al., 2020 ). Similarly, DNRterm·mth is already considered to be a general, canonicalterm computation of the nervous system, present in evolutionarily distant phyla, for example, fruit flies and monkeys ( Carandini and Heeger, 2011 ). The second question regards the tradeoff between WMterm·dom flexibility and capacityterm·dom ( Bouchacourt and Buschman, 2019 ). Is the WMterm·dom of a crowterm as flexible as that of a monkeyterm? Our results show that the computations by individual neurons that result in WMterm·dom capacityterm·dom limitations are virtually the same in crows and monkeys, highlighting a further aspect of WMterm·dom that is similar between these animal groups ( NiederpersName, 2017 ). Ultimately, our results were in line with different modern models of WMterm·dom that implement DNRterm·mth to explain capacityterm·dom ( Bouchacourt and Buschman, 2019 ; Lundqvist et al., 2016 ; Lundqvist et al., 2011 ; Schneegans et al., 2020 ). However, the data we presented cannot carry a definitive conclusion about which of the different models fits best. For example, a tradeoff between flexibility and capacityterm·dom ( Bouchacourt and Buschman, 2019 ) might be present, but further investigation into the models’ predictions is required. We do, however, show that mammalianterm models of WMterm·dom are in line with WMterm·dom in birds, which implies that fundamental aspects of WMterm·dom are shared between these animal groups.

Conclusion

Together, all these facets of crowterm WMterm·dom capacityterm·dom suggest that the different intricate neuronal architectures that carry out the computations in monkeys and crows have likely been shaped by convergentterm·dom evolution – into systems that yield similar cognitive performances. The systems may share the same basic mechanisms and thus limitations. Further investigation into the oscillatory dynamics of WMterm·dom in the avian brain may elucidate if birds also share the prominent limitation of a tradeoff between flexibility and capacityterm·dom.

Materials and methods

Subjects

Two hand-raised carrion crows ( C. corone ) of 2 years of age served as subjects in this study. The birds were housed in spacious aviaries in social groups. During the experimental procedures, the animals were held on a controlled food protocol with ad libitum access to water and grit. All experimental procedures and housing conditions were carried out in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were authorized by the national authority ( LANUV ).

Experimental setup

We used operant training chambers (50 × 50.5 × 77.5 cm 3 , width × depth × height) equipped with an acoustic pulse touchscreen (22’’, ELO 2200L APR, Elo Touch Solutions Inc, Milpitas, CA) and an infrared camera (Sygonix, Nürnberg, Germany) for remote monitoring. The birds sat on a wooden perch so that the distance between the birdterm’s eye and the touchscreen was 8 cm. Food pellets were delivered as a reward via a custom-made automatic feeder (plans available at http://www.jonasrose.net/ ). The position of the animal’s head was tracked online during the experiment by two open-source computer vision cameras (‘Pixy’, CMUcam5, Charmed Labs, Austin, TX) that reported the location and angle between two LEDs. For tracking, we surgically implanted a lightweight head-post and used a lightweight 3D-printed mount with LEDs that was removed after each experimental session. The system reported the head location at a frame rate of 50 Hz and data was smoothed by integrating over two frames in MATLAB using custom programs on a control PC. All experiments were controlled by custom programs in MATLAB using the Biopsychology ( RosepersName et al., 2008 ) and Psychophysics toolboxes ( Brainard, 1997 ). Digital input and output of the control PC were handled by a microcontroller (ODROID C1, Hardkernel co. Ltd, Anyang, South Korea) connected through a gigabit network running custom software (available at http://www.jonasrose.net/ ).

Behavioral protocol

The behavioral protocol was identical to the one described in BalakhonovpersName and RosepersName, 2017 . We trained the birds to perform a delayed change localization taskterm that had previously been used to test the performance under different WMterm·dom loads in primates ( Buschman et al., 2011 ). Each trial started after a 2 s inter-trial-interval, with the presentation of a red dot centered on the touchscreen (for a maximum of 40 s). The animals initiated the trial by centering their head in front of the red dot for 160 ms. This caused the red dot to disappear and a stimulus array of two to five colored squares to appear ( Figure 1A , ‘sampleterm’). The colored squares were presented for a period of 800 ms, during which the animals had to hold their head still and center their gaze on the screen (‘hold gaze’, no more than 2 cm horizontal or vertical displacement, and no more than 20 degrees horizontal or vertical rotation). Failure to hold the head in this position resulted in an aborted trial. This sampleterm phase was followed by a memory delayterm of 1000 ms after which the stimulus array reappeared with one colorterm exchanged. The animal had to indicate the location of the colorterm change by pecking the respective square. Correct responses were rewarded probabilistically (BEO special pellets, in 55% of correct trials, additional 2 s illumination of the food receptacle in 100% of correct trials). Incorrect responses to colors that had not changed or a failure to respond within 4 s resulted in a brief screen flash and a 10 s timeout. The stimuli were presented at six fixed locations on the screen (1–6, Figure 1A ). In each session, one pair of colors was assigned to each of the six locations. Each location had its own distinct pair. These pairs were randomly chosen from a pool of 14 colors (two colorterm combinations were excluded since the animals did not discriminate them equally well during a pre-training). Let us consider Figure 1A as an example. The colorterm change occurs in the middle-left where turquois (T) is presented during the sampleterm and orange (O) during the choice. In this particular session the middle-left could thus show either of the following colors during the sampleterm and choice: T-O (shown in Figure 1A ); O-T; O-O; T-T; None-None. On the next session a new random pair of colors was displayed at this location.

For identification and analysis an arbitrary label was assigned to each of the randomly drawn colors at the start of each session (i.e., left middle location: ‘colorterm A’, or ‘colorterm B’; left top location: ‘colorterm A’, or ‘colorterm B’; etc.). These indices do not refer to the order of presentation of the colors at any time, but were held constant for neuronal analysis. The order of presentation of colors within a pair, the target location (where the colorterm change occurred), and the number of stimuli in the array (two to five) were randomized and balanced across trials so that each condition had an equal likelihood to appear. The order of presentation of colors within a pair, the target location (where the colorterm change occurred), and the number of stimuli in the array (two to five) were randomized and balanced across trials so that each condition had an equal likelihood to appear. The colorterm squares had a width of 10 degrees of visual angle (DVA) and were placed on the horizontal meridian of the screen and at 45.8 DVA above or below the meridian at a distance of 54 and 55.4 DVA from the center. This arrangement in combination with the head tracking ensured that all stimuli appeared outside of the binocular visual field of crows (37.6 DVA; Troscianko et al., 2012 ).

Surgery

Both animals were chronically implanted with a lightweight head-post to attach a small LED holder during the experiments. Before surgery, animals were deeply anesthetized with ketamine (50 mg/kg) and xylazine (5 mg/kg). Once deeply anesthetized, animals were placed in a stereotaxic frame. After attaching the small head-post with dental acrylic, a microdrive with a multi-channel microelectrode was stereotactically implanted at the craniotomy (Neuronexus Technologies Inc, Ann Arbor MI, DDrive). The electrode was positioned in NCLterm (AP 5.0, ML 13.0) of the left hemisphere (coordinates for the region based on histological studies on the localization of NCLterm in crows; Veit and NiederpersName, 2013 ). After the surgery, the crows received analgesics.

Electrophysiological recordings

Extracellular single neuron recordings were performed using chronically implanted multi-channel microelectrodes. The distance between recording sites was 50 µm. The signal was amplified, filtered, and digitized using Intan RHD2000 headstages and a USB-Interface board (Intan Technologies LLC, Los Angeles, CA). The system also recorded digital event codes that were sent from the behavioral control PC using a custom IO device (details available at http://www.jonasrose.net/ ). Before each recording session, the electrodes were advanced manually using the microdrive. Recordings were started 20 min after the advancement, and each recording site was manually checked for neuronal signals. The signals were recorded at a sampling rate of 30 kHz and filtered with a band-pass filter at recording (0.5–7.5 kHz). The recorded neuronal signals were not pre-selected for taskterm involvement. We performed spike sorting using the semi-automatic Klusta-suite software ( Rossant et al., 2016 ), which uses the high electrode count and their close spacing to isolate signals of single neurons. For spike sorting, we filtered with a high pass of 500 Hz and a low pass of 7125 Hz. The software utilizes the spatial distribution of the recorded signal along the different recording sites to untangle overlapping signals and separate signals with similar waveforms but different recording depths.

Trials were classified as error trials if the birdterm chose a location where no change of colors had appeared.
