4. Discussion In order to understand whether the inclusionterm·dom of persons with disabilities is being achieved, and to operationalise the ICF approach towards disabilityterm·key, requires the use of tools to measure disabilityterm·key in censuses and surveysterm·mth. Using three different measures of disabilityterm·key on one population—in this case Syrian refugeesterm·key in Turkey—enables us to analyse how research results change with varying definitions and ensuing measures of disabilityterm·key. First, we note that the disabilityterm·key prevalenceterm·key in the population changes with each measure. This corroborates earlier research and general disability statisticsterm·key [ 7 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 ]. Thus, any research, census or statistics that publish data on persons with disabilities should always note which measure and model of disabilityterm·key was used.

In addition, we see that the characteristics of those categorised as disabled change depending on the measure used: older people who are considered disabled using a functional measure of disabilityterm·key are more likely than younger people to also be considered disabled using a limitations measure of disabilityterm·key; those with mental health conditions such as anxiety or depression are more likely to be categorised as disabled using the WGES and the 2QSterm than the WGSSterm. People can thus experience disabilityterm·key differently based on different identities, such as age, sex or refugeeterm·key status, and thus possibly also the intersection of multiple identities. Depending on the measure used, certain demographic groups may thus disproportionately be categorised as disabled or not disabled; this could exacerbate exclusion if these measures are used to decide on resource allocation for individuals, health interventions or as disability statisticsterm·key to inform policy decisions.

We also see that the specific individuals who are categorised as disabled are not the same for each measure of disabilityterm·key. For example, while 8.8% of the population were categorised as disabled for both the WGSSterm and the 2QSterm measure, 4.7% were categorised as disabled using the WGSSterm but not the 2QSterm and 13.6% by the 2QSterm but not the WGSSterm. This aligns with earlier studies [ 17 , 25 ]. We conclude, as Beno Schraepen writes, that there is no one person with a disabilityterm·key but that different people have different characteristics that lead to exclusion or segregation [ 12 ] (p. 26). Each of the measures picks up people at greater risk of exclusion, but who the various measures categorise as being disabled deserves further attention.

Lastly, we see that, consequently, the conclusions drawn on the inclusionterm·dom and participationterm·key of persons with disabilities slightly vary based on the disabilityterm·key measure used. In this study, we used no schooling, being married/living together and having paid work as our indicators of participationterm·key. For all outcomes, we see that there are inequalities between people with and people without disabilities. However, the magnitude of that inequality varies based on the disabilityterm·key measure used, reflecting, again, that each of the measures captures a different group of people. This is important, as such disaggregated data inform decision makers in developing interventions, policies and programmes.

To understand whether society, policies, programmes and interventions are inclusive of persons with disabilities, we need to demonstrate how persons with disabilities are doing in relation to their peers. This requires comparing persons with and without disabilities using the same indicators. However, using different definitions of disabilityterm·key categorises a different group of individuals as disabled: although the WGE and the 2QSterm find a similar prevalenceterm·key of persons with disabilities in the population, the specific group that is categorised as disabled is not the same.

The findings demonstrate that there is no single, uniform category of “persons with disabilities” and that this has implications for subsequent outcomes on indicators of participationterm·key. Using different tools changes the extent of observed inequalities in key participationterm·key outcomes—in this case, education, paid work and partnership. This has significant implications, particularly when monitoring international frameworks such as the SDGs and the UNCRPDterm·dom, whose aim is to achieve equality for those with and without disabilities. We need to acknowledge that “different methodologies lead to a range of understandings of disabilityterm·key” [ 32 ] (p. 5118). Further research is needed to understand who the people are that different measures of disabilityterm·key are categorising as disabled and how this is of relevance for policies, programmes, (health) interventions and the monitoring of the UNCRPDterm·dom and SDGs.

Future research could delve into further examining how different measures of disabilityterm·key may impact the rights, social standing, participationterm·key, resource allocation and access to (disability-specific) resources of persons with disabilities. This involves conducting comparative studies using various disabilityterm·key measures to assess differences in access to legal protections, social services, and participationterm·key in societal activities.

It is important to recognise the diversity of disabilityterm·key and that the relationship between disabilityterm·key and participationterm·key will be shaped by intersecting characteristics and social identities. Data disaggregation by age, sex and type of impairment was not possible in our study due to limited sample size. However, a future analysis comparing measures should consider further disaggregation to provide a more in-depth understanding of these measures. Further research should also be conducted to analyse whether multiple measures of disabilityterm·key could be incorporated into data collection to capture a more comprehensive picture of the population that could be categorised as having a disabilityterm·key and are at risk of exclusion. This might be necessary to identify and address the needs of individuals who may be overlooked if only one measure of disabilityterm·key is used for categorisation, identification and resource allocation.

Limitations This study has several limitations. The measures of participationterm·key were restricted to schooling, marital status, and employment, which, while important, capture only a narrow subset of societal inclusionterm·dom. Further, although employment is an indicator of current participationterm·key, for education it is not known whether the onset of disabilityterm·key was before, during or after school age. Broader dimensions of participationterm·key, such as social connectedness, access to healthcare, and civic engagement, were not collected on the full study population and therefore could not be included in this analysis. This deserves attention in future research as these additional indicators could provide a more nuanced understanding of the barriers and opportunities experienced by persons with disabilities, particularly in contexts of displacement. The prevalenceterm·key of mental health conditions, including anxiety and depression, within the refugeeterm·key population studied may have influenced how individuals perceived and reported physical and sensory functioning. This could result in an overestimation of difficulties, particularly in a population already exposed to significant stressors and trauma (see [ 30 ] for more information). This study provides evidence that participationterm·key measures differ depending on the measure of disabilityterm·key used. It would be of interest to replicate this amongst a non-humanitarian population.

5. Conclusions The findings of this study underscore the importance of carefully selecting and clearly defining disabilityterm·key measures in both research and policy contexts, as it impacts the levels of participationterm·key measured when studying the inclusionterm·dom of persons with disabilities. Since definitions can both marginalise and empower individuals, it is crucial to understand and communicate the implications of the chosen measures. There is no “gold standard” to measure disabilityterm·key, but the most appropriate measure would be dependent on the intended purpose with eligibility for, for example, health screening programmes, requiring a different measure than monitoring the participationterm·key of those at risk of exclusion in social protection policies. Studies and statistics on disabilityterm·key should therefore always include the specific measure or definition used.
