sensedecomposability - Nunberg et al.'s sense: an idiom whose meaning, once known, can be distributed among its parts— “The former are idioms whose meaning, once known, can be distributed among their parts. A typical example is spill the beans, where spill roughly means `reveal' and beans roughly means `secrets'.”
sensetransparency - the relation between an idiom's literal and idiomatic meaning, which the source proposal explicitly warns must not be confused with decomposability— “They do however explicitly warn against confusing decomposability with transparency, which they define as the relation between the literal and idiomatic meaning.”
senseparaphrasability - whether the idiom can be paraphrased by a similarly structured phrase, explicitly distinguished from decomposability— “An additional distinction is made between decomposability and paraphrasability. The fact that the meaning of an idiom can be paraphrased using a phrase of a similar argument structure does not necessarily indicate that it is decomposable.”
senseflexibility - the categorical sense inherited from Nunberg et al., on which an idiom is either transformationally productive or transformationally deficient— “They propose that the semantic distinction between decomposable and non-decomposable idioms accounts for the difference between "transformationally productive" and "transformationally deficient" idioms.”
senseflexibility - this paper's sense: a continuum, with idioms exhibiting varying degrees rather than falling into two classes— “Rather, we envision a continuum with idioms exhibiting varying degrees of flexibility, possibly dependent on their semantic properties.”
sensefiguration - the degree to which the idiom can be assigned a literal meaning that conjures a picture— “Figuration reflects the degree to which the idiom can be assigned a literal meaning. Transparency (or opacity) relates to how easy it is to recover the motivation for an idiom's use, or, in other words, to explain the relationship between its literal meaning and its idiomatic one.”
