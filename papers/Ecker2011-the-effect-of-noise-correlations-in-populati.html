Unfortunately, under some conditions potentially relevant for neural coding this bound is not tight, which means there exists no estimator that achieves this performance ( Bethge et al., 2002 ; Berens et al., 2011 ). Setting κ = 0 results in a homogeneousterm populationterm in which all neurons have identical tuningterm curves. We set κ = 0.25 as determined from a dataset of 408 orientation tuningterm curves of neurons recorded in macaque primary visual cortex ( EckerpersName et al., 2010 ). Discussion Many theoretical studies have investigated the effect of noiseterm correlations on encoding accuracyterm ( Snippe and Koenderink, 1992 ; Zohary et al., 1994 ; Abbott and Dayan, 1999 ; Sompolinsky et al., 2001 ; Wilke and Eurich, 2002 ; Shamir and Sompolinsky, 2004 , 2006 ; Josić et al., 2009 ). One of the main conclusions has been that limited range correlations are detrimental for a population codeterm compared with independence or other correlationterm structures (e.g., uniform) ( Abbott and Dayan, 1999 ; Sompolinsky et al., 2001 ; Wilke and Eurich, 2002 ). Because of this, it is often assumed that decreasing correlations improves any population codeterm—as it becomes more similar to the independent case ( Zohary et al., 1994 ; Abbott and Dayan, 1999 ; Sompolinsky et al., 2001 ). In this paper, we showed that these results hold only under the assumptionterm of homogeneousterm populations of neurons or very small numbers of neurons. In the range of biologically plausible parameters (heterogeneous tuningterm curves, nonadditive noiseterm, thousands of neurons), increasing correlations can substantially increase the Fisher informationterm ( Fig. 4 B ). This increase in accuracy is mainly due to an overall reduction of the noise entropyterm that is associated with stronger correlations. When correlations are increased, the noiseterm power increases in a few low-frequencyterm Fourierterm·mth components while it decreases in all higher-frequencyterm components (it quickly asymptotes to 1 − c 0 with increasing frequencyterm; Fig. 1 D ). Because in homogeneousterm populations the stimulusterm encoding is confined to the low-frequencyterm Fourierterm·mth components, higher correlations have detrimental effects. In heterogeneous populations, in contrast, the stimulusterm encoding is distributed among all frequencies and the high-frequencyterm components have a better signal-to-noiseterm ratio if correlations are strong. These two mechanisms compete, leading to the non-monotonic correlationterm dependence of the Fisher informationterm of heterogeneous populations. As the populationterm sizeterm is increased, the high-frequencyterm components dominate because their number grows linearly with the populationterm sizeterm. As a consequence, in the large n case, increasing correlations is almost always beneficial while a decrease is beneficial only for small populationterm sizes and small enough correlations. This result raises two important questions. First, if higher correlations improve the accuracy, why does the brain not implement a population codeterm with strong correlations? Second, why do experimental studies find reduced noiseterm correlations under experimental conditions, in which an improved population codeterm is expected ( Gutnisky and Dragoi, 2008 ; Cohen and Maunsell, 2009 ; Mitchell et al., 2009 ; Gu et al., 2011 )? With regard to the first question, we suggest that the level of correlations should not be interpreted as a free parameterterm that can be optimized independently while all other parameters, such as tuningterm functions and variances, are kept fixed. Under such assumptions, increasing the level of correlations to the maximum (i.e., c 0 = 1) leads to maximum Fisher informationterm ( Figs. 4 B , 6 ). The noiseterm power in the high frequencies vanishes (as it asymptotes to 1 − c 0 ; Fig. 1 D ), allowing for effectively noiseterm-free decodingterm·mth of any signal that is encoded in these directions. However, in real neural populations, the amount of noiseterm in the response cannot be arbitrarily small because input noiseterm cannot be removed by processing. Thus, finding the optimal level of correlations under the assumptionterm of fixed tuningterm functions and variances leads to a degenerateterm solution, which is not biologically plausible. To avoid the noiseterm-free case and better constrain the problem, we also analyzed populationterm codes in which the total amount of noiseterm (the noise entropyterm) is kept constant and only the correlationterm structure is changed. Our results demonstrate that, under this constraintterm, the performance of populationterm codes in large populations is primarily determined by the amount of heterogeneityterm in the tuningterm functions of the neurons and by the overall noise entropyterm ( Fig. 10 D , Eq. 46 ), while the specific structure of the noiseterm does not appear to be as important as commonly assumed. Whether the noise entropyterm is of the same importance for predicting the accuracy of a population codeterm also for other scenarios not considered explicitly in this study—such as stimulusterm-dependent ( Josić et al., 2009 ) and heterogeneous correlations, or simultaneous encoding of multiple stimulusterm dimensions ( Zhang and Sejnowski, 1999 )—remains to be investigated. The second question raised above was how to reconcile our theoretical results with empirical findings of reduced correlations ( Gutnisky and Dragoi, 2008 ; Cohen and Maunsell, 2009 ; Mitchell et al., 2009 ; Gu et al., 2011 ). One possibility is that the sizeterm of the populationterm that is read out is small enough to be in the low-correlation regimeterm·def. Although we did not assess this in detail by taking into account all relevant parameters from these studies, Figures 5 and 8 B suggest that the relevant populationterm sizeterm would have to be on the order of at most a few hundred cells. Given the number of cells even in a single cortical column, this seems rather unlikely. A second explanation is related to the neural readoutterm mechanism. Because we currently do not know how information is read out by downstream neurons (or populations thereof), we quantified the maximum amount of information that can be extracted from the populationterm response. Of course, the effect of correlations can be different for other, computationally constrained readouts. If, for instance, a linearterm·mth readoutterm is assumed, the conclusions would be different. Even though J mean is often referred to as the portion of the information that can be read out by linearterm·mth methods, this notion is problematic—at least in the framework of stimulusterm reconstruction considered here. To illustrate this point, we estimated the performance of the OLE decodingterm·mth the activity of a heterogeneous populationterm of neurons. The efficiency of the OLE does not converge to the inverse of J mean ( Fig. 12 A ). In addition, in contrast to the Fisher informationterm and the maximum-likelihoodterm·mth estimatorterm·mth, the accuracy of the OLE does not increase with increased correlations, not even for large populationterm sizes ( Fig. 12 B ). The reason for this behavior is the fact that the OLE is a biased estimatorterm·mth, for which the Cramér–Rao boundterm·stat is not simply the inverse of the Fisher informationterm but the biasterm has to be taken into account. Because the estimatorterm·mth biasterm depends on the correlations, the dependence of the mean squared errorterm·stat on correlations is not captured well by the Fisher informationterm. Assuming optimal readoutterm, a third possibility is that the improved performance is not exclusively due to reduced correlations. For instance, under attentionterm Fano factors decrease and firing rates increase ( Cohen and Maunsell, 2009 ; Mitchell et al., 2009 ). Although it has been argued that these changes are small compared with the relative changes in correlationterm strength and their effect is negligible for large populations, this argument is problematic. The effect of different factors was assessed using highly suboptimal poolingterm·mth rules ( Cohen and Maunsell, 2009 ; Mitchell et al., 2009 ) and the conclusions derived from these poolingterm·mth models do not generalize to other (e.g., optimal) readoutterm mechanisms—as our above results show. Our analysis suggests an alternative interpretation: the higher firing rates under attentionterm increase the signal while the reduced Fano factors and correlations indicate the suppression of a common noiseterm source, which reduces the noise entropyterm and therefore leads to improved coding accuracy. For example, if the response of a populationterm of neurons with unit variances and weak correlations (〈 r 〉 = 0.05) is confounded by a common noiseterm source with varianceterm 0.05, removing this common noiseterm source reduces noiseterm correlations from ∼0.1 to ∼0.05 (a 50% change) while reducing the variances from ∼1.05 to 1 (a 5% change). In this situation, however, the changes in correlations cannot be separated from the changes in variances and neither of both is more important than the other. In fact, considering each one in isolation is not meaningful. In summary, the notion that reducing correlations leads to a more accurate encoding is not a general principle but is only true under certain conditions. Assumptions about the sizeterm of the populationterm and the way information is read out can strongly affect the conclusions. For optimal decodingterm·mth of large populations, the total amount of noiseterm—as measured by the noise entropyterm—is more important than the specific noiseterm correlationterm structure.
