phenomenoncrop-yield growth that is too slow and spatially uneven to double global production by 2050 — “Numerous studies have shown that feeding a more populated and more prosperous world will roughly require a doubling of agricultural production by 2050 [1] – [7] , translating to a ∼2.4% rate of crop production growth per year. We find that the top four global crops – maize, rice, wheat, and soybean – are currently witnessing average yield improvements only between 0.9 to 1.6 percent per year, far slower than the required rates to double their production by 2050 solely from yield gains. This is because yield improvements are below ∼2.4% per year in many areas of our most important agricultural lands. At these rates maize, rice, wheat and soybean production may increase by ∼67%, ∼42%, ∼38%, and ∼55% respectively, by 2050 globally. There is a 90% chance that the total global production increase from yields alone would be between 34–101% for maize, 21–59% for rice, 4–76% for wheat, and 13–84% for soybean by ∼2050. Thus, if these yield change rates do not increase, land clearing possibly would be needed [3] if global food security is to increase or even maintained”

contribution — evidence — “Here we employ ∼2.5 million statistics from a newly developed crop yield and area harvested database covering ∼13,500 political units globally from 1961 to 2008, focusing on trends in the recent two decades [4] , [23] . We determine the rates of yield change in each political unit for the top four global crops: maize, rice, wheat, and soybean. These four crops together produce about two-thirds of current harvested global crop calories [3] , [18] . Using these data, we estimate the best-fit linear, non-compounding rates of yield change between 1989 and 2008 for these crops in each of these political units. Yield change is commonly modeled as a linear function of time [24] – [28] and such models have been used to project future crop yields [28] – [30] . We provide local, country, and global-scale rates of recent crop yield changes to determine where the rates of yield increase could double production by 2050, and where they are insufficient. The impact of negative or even slow rates of yield change in these crops could be severe, especially for low-income countries with rapidly rising population. The underlying data, period analyzed, statistical approach, and comparisons of yield projections are described in the Methods section below, with additional details and analysis in Text S1”

mechanismPOPULATION GROWTH can absorb yield-driven production gains, leaving per-capita harvest unchanged or declining — “In the short term, due to population increases from ∼6.7 billion in 2008 to ∼8.0 billion in 2025 [5] , the 1.6% and 1.3% per year global maize and soybean yield improvements may result in no significant change to the per capita global maize and soybean harvests. However, by 2050 there could be an increase. The much lower rates of rice and wheat yield increases, 1.0% and 0.9% per year, respectively, may result in no change to the per capita rice and wheat harvests to 2050. Thus, if we are to boost the production in these top four global crops that are now responsible for directly providing ∼43% of the global dietary energy and ∼40% of its daily protein supply [31] from yield increases alone, we have to immediately determine where and exactly by how much yields are changing. To further understand the yield trend patterns, we also track the rates of yield change within ∼13,500 political units and report the results at the local and country scales”

Introduction

The world is experiencing rising demands for crop productionterm·dom, stemming from three key forces: increasing human populationterm·dom, meat and dairy consumptionterm·dom from growing affluence, and biofuel consumptionterm·dom [1] – [5] . By 2050date, global agricultural productionterm·dom may need to be increased by 60%–110% to meet these increasing demands [3] , [6] – [7] as well as to provide food securityterm·dom to the ∼870 million now chronically undernourished [8] . The only peer-reviewed estimate [3] suggests that crop demand may increase by 100%–110% between 2005 and 2050date. Numerous authors have suggested that increasing crop yieldsterm·dom, rather than clearing more land for food production, is the most sustainable path for food securityterm·dom [2] , [4] , [9] – [14] . Moreover, crop yield growth has been shown as an effective tool in reducing global poverty and undernourishment, as farmers themselves constitute the vast majority of the poor and the undernourished [15] – [17] .

However, several recent studies indicate that yields may no longer be increasing in different regions of the globe [18] – [23] . Yields are no longer improving on 24–39% of our most important cropland areas [23] . Many of these areas are in top crop producing nations, having rising population, increasing affluence, or some combination of these factors [3] , [5] , [19] , [21] – [23] . This may increase difficulty of meeting future crop production goals but key unknowns remain for developing and targeting strategies: how are crop yieldsterm·dom changing across the world, where gains in crop yieldsterm·dom are able to meet growing demands, and where crop yieldsterm·dom are falling behind.

Here we employ ∼2.5 million statistics from a newly developed crop yield and area harvested database covering ∼13,500 political unitsterm·mth globally from 1961 to 2008, focusing on trends in the recent two decades [4] , [23] . We determine the rates of yield change in each political unit for the top four global crops: maizeterm·dom, riceterm·dom, wheatterm·dom, and soybeanterm·dom. These four crops together produce about two-thirds of current harvested global crop calories [3] , [18] . Using these data, we estimate the best-fit linear, non-compounding rates of yield changeterm·mth between 1989 and 2008 for these crops in each of these political unitsterm·mth. Yield change is commonly modeled as a linear function of time [24] – [28] and such models have been used to project future crop yieldsterm·dom [28] – [30] . We provide local, country, and global-scale rates of recent crop yield changes to determine where the rates of yield increase could double production by 2050date, and where they are insufficient. The impact of negative or even slow rates of yield change in these crops could be severe, especially for low-income countries with rapidly rising population. The underlying data, period analyzed, statistical approach, and comparisons of yield projections are described in the Methods section below, with additional details and analysis in Text S1 .

Discussion and Conclusions

Numerous studies have shown that feeding a more populated and more prosperous world will roughly require a doubling of agricultural production by 2050date [1] – [7] , translating to a ∼2.4% rate of crop production growth per year. We find that the top four global crops – maizeterm·dom, riceterm·dom, wheatterm·dom, and soybeanterm·dom – are currently witnessing average yield improvementsterm·dom only between 0.9 to 1.6 percent per year, far slower than the required rates to double their production by 2050date solely from yield gainsterm·dom. This is because yield improvementsterm·dom are below ∼2.4% per year in many areas of our most important agricultural lands. At these rates maizeterm·dom, riceterm·dom, wheatterm·dom and soybeanterm·dom production may increase by ∼67%, ∼42%, ∼38%, and ∼55% respectively, by 2050date globally. There is a 90% chance that the total global production increase from yields alone would be between 34–101% for maizeterm·dom, 21–59% for riceterm·dom, 4–76% for wheatterm·dom, and 13–84% for soybeanterm·dom by ∼2050date. Thus, if these yield change rates do not increase, land clearing possibly would be needed [3] if global food securityterm·dom is to increase or even maintained ( Table 1 ).

We found that the top three riceterm·dom and wheatterm·dom producing nations are witnessing very low yield growth rates. China, India and Indonesia are witnessing riceterm·dom yield increases of only 0.7%, 1.0%, and 0.4% improvement per year. China, India, and the U. S., the top three wheatterm·dom producers similarly were witnessing yield increases of only 1.7%, 1.1%, and 0.8% per year, respectively. At these rates we found that yield driven production growth in India and China could result in nearly unchanged per capita riceterm·dom harvests, but decline steeply in Indonesia.

In many of the smaller crop producing nations, maizeterm·dom, riceterm·dom, or wheatterm·dom yield improvement rates are below the 2.4% doubling rate. Unfortunately, a high percentage of total calories consumed in these countries are from these four crops. This is particularly true for maizeterm·dom throughout much of Africa (e.g., Kenya, Zambia, Zimbabwe), Central America (e.g., Guatemala, Nicaragua, Panama), and parts of Asia (e.g., Nepal, Georgia).

Riceterm·dom provides ∼19% of dietary energy globally. Riceterm·dom provides a higher percentage of total calories consumed in countries such as Dominican Republic, Costa Rica, Haiti, Sierra Leone, Nigeria, and North Korea, yet yields are declining, −0.1% to −3.2% per year. Elsewhere riceterm·dom yields are increasing too slowly to overcome the impact of their population growthterm·dom. In some of the world's top riceterm·dom producers, e.g. India and China, the per capita production may remain nearly unchanged. In numerous smaller riceterm·dom producers across the world where riceterm·dom is an important significant provider of daily dietary energy such as in Peru, Ecuador, Bolivia, Benin, Togo, Myanmar, Philippines, Malaysia, South Korea, Nepal, and in Sri Lanka, the per capita production may also remain unchanged.

Wheatterm·dom provides ∼19% of global dietary energy. Wheatterm·dom comprises an even larger portion of the diet in some countries where yields are declining, particularly Eastern European countries of Bulgaria, Hungary, Czech Republic, Moldova, Romania, Slovakia, and Ukraine. In many countries, such as Bolivia, Peru, Paraguay, Afghanistan, and Iraq, wheatterm·dom yield increases are too low to maintain their current per capita harvests.

Our analysis identifies where yield improvementsterm·dom are on track to double production and where investments should be targeted to increase yields. The observed rates of yield change result from several location-specific, socio-economic, and biophysical factors that are described elsewhere [23] . Many studies illustrate that intensification can be unsustainable [32] – [36] , but several notable projects in Africa [37] and elsewhere [38] have shown that sustainable intensificationterm·dom is possible and necessary to boost global crop productionterm·dom.

Clearly, the world faces a looming and growing agricultural crisis. Yields are not improving fast enough to keep up with projected demandsterm·dom in 2050date. However, opportunities do exist to increase productionterm·dom through more efficient use of current arable landsterm·dom [4] and increasing yield growth rates by spreading best management practicesterm·dom and closing yield gapsterm·dom under different management regimes [38] – [42] across the globe. A portion of the production shortfall could also be met by expanding croplandsterm·dom, but at a high environmental cost to biodiversityterm·dom and carbon emissionsterm·dom [4] , [43] – [45] . Alternatively, additional strategies, particularly changing to more plant-based dietsterm·dom and reducing food wasteterm·dom [4] , [46] – [48] can reduce the large expected demand growth in foodterm·dom [3] , [4] .
