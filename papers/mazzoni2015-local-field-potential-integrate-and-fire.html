Introduction Models of recurrently connected networks of leaky integrate-and-fireterm·mth (LIFterm·mth) neurons are well established tools for studying brain function [ 1 , 2 ]. The equations describing the single LIFterm·mth neuron are simple and can be easily adapted to generate complex dynamics [ 3 , 4 ]. Despite their simplicity, LIFterm·mth network models have proved able to describe a wide spectrum of different cortical dynamics and cortical functions, from the emergence of up and down states [ 5 – 7 ], working memory [ 8 – 10 ], attention [ 11 , 12 ], decision making [ 13 ], rhythmogenesis [ 14 ], and sensory information coding [ 11 , 15 , 16 ]. In some cases it is possible to describe the dynamics of LIFterm·mth networks analytically [ 17 , 18 ], thus providing deeper insights into how spiking neuronal networks may implement the basic cerebral computational mechanisms [ 19 ].

Models can only be properly tested against experimental evidence when they can predict empirical measures quantitatively. Local cortical activity is often recorded in vivo or in vitro using the local field potentialterm·dom (LFPterm·dom), a measure obtained by low-pass filtering (below a few hundred hertz) the electrical potential recorded from extracellular electrodes. The LFPterm·dom signal reflects mass neural activity arising within a few hundred micrometers or more from the recording electrode [ 20 – 25 ]. This spatial scale is highly relevant for LIFterm·mth network models, which typically aim to describe the activity of thousands or tens of thousands of cells. The recording of LFPsterm·dom has a prominent role in systems neuroscience, and such recordings have been used extensively to investigate cortical network mechanisms involved in sensory processing [ 26 ], motor planning [ 27 ], and higher cognitive processes [ 28 ].

LFPterm·dom is generated by transmembrane currentsterm·dom in the neurons in the vicinity of the recording electrode [ 23 ] and depends on morphological features of the contributing cells, the positioning of synapses, as well as the correlation level of synaptic inputs [ 20 , 21 , 29 , 30 ]. Under reasonable assumptions about the extracellular milieu the cellular LFPterm·dom contributions can be computed as a weighted sum of the transmembrane currentsterm·dom in multi-compartmentterm·key neuron models [ 31 – 34 ]. This allows for detailed numerical investigations of spatial, as well as spectral features of the LFPterm·dom signals [ 35 ]. In particular, such simulationsterm·mth of large populations of morphologically detailed neurons have provided insight into how the neuronal activity at the population level influences the spatial reach and laminar variation of the LFPterm·dom signal in vivo [ 20 , 21 , 33 , 34 , 36 ] the relative importance of active and passive currents [ 37 ], and the population LFPterm·dom signal measured from cortical slices in microelectrode arrays (MEAs) [ 38 , 39 ].

However, it has been unclear how best to use LIFterm·mth networks to model and provide understanding of LFPterm·dom recordings. This is because extracellular potentials arise in biological tissue due to a spatial separation of inward (sinks) and outward (sources) transmembrane currentsterm·dom of the neurons, and neuron models used to compute an LFPterm·dom signal must thus have a minimum of two spatially separated compartments in order to generate a potential [ 32 ]. In LIFterm·mth models, however, a single compartment is typically used as an approximation of the entire neuron, including the spatially extended dendritic structure, and individual cells within a population are not assigned to a specific spatial position.

One possible way to compute LFPsterm·dom from LIFterm·mth network is to project the spiketerm·dom times generated by the LIFterm·mth network under consideration onto morphologically detailed 3D neuron models and then compute the field that the currents flowing through these 3D networks generate. However, this approach would require the modeler to set up a cumbersome and computationally expensive network model based on multi-compartmentterm·key model neuron. As a much simpler alternative, we here instead search for a general and easy-to-use proxy to predict the time course of the LFPterm·dom based on variables available directly from the LIFterm·mth network simulationsterm·mth. Here we investigate and evaluate different strategies to compute an LFP proxyterm·key directly from the output of standard LIFterm·mth network simulationsterm·mth without the use of multi-compartmentterm·key neuronal morphologies.

Our approach is as follows: we first simulate an LIFterm·mth point-neuron network model and record the output spiking activity, membrane potentialsterm·key, and synaptic currentsterm·dom. Next, we compute a realistic ground-truth estimate of the LFPterm·dom that the same LIFterm·mth network activity would generate. We do this by injecting distributed synaptic currentsterm·dom corresponding to the stored LIFterm·mth synaptic events, onto a population of multi-compartmentterm·key neurons with realistic distributions of dendrites and synapses (we call this population the “3D network”). We then compare this simulated ground-truth LFPterm·dom signal to a number of LFPterm·dom proxies computed directly from measures of activity of the point-neuron LIFterm·mth network. These proxies include those previously proposed in the literature (e.g., the average firing rateterm·key [ 11 , 14 , 40 ], the average membrane potentialterm·key [ 24 , 41 – 44 ], the sum of synaptic currentsterm·dom [ 7 , 45 ], and the sum of absolute values of synaptic currentsterm·dom [ 15 ]), as well as others proposed here. By separating the spiking dynamics generated by the LIFterm·mth network from the LFPterm·dom generated by the 3D network, we are also able to investigate how different assumptions regarding cell morphology, synaptic distributions and recording positions influence the accuracy of the different LFPterm·dom proxies.

We find that a simple linear combination of excitatory (AMPAterm·key) and inhibitory (GABAterm·key) synaptic currentsterm·dom extracted from the point-neuron LIFterm·mth network provides a proxy for the LFPterm·dom that closely matches the temporal features of the signals resulting from the morphologically realistic LFPterm·dom model generated by the 3D network. Even with a small set of fixed parameters this LFP proxyterm·key is able to account for the LFPterm·dom signal with a high degree of precision under most investigated conditions.

Results A morphological model for a cortical LIFterm·mth network Our goal was to understand how to compute a simple yet accurate approximation (denoted as “proxy” in the following) of the LFPterm·dom that would be generated by the time series of synaptic activity of an LIFterm·mth network if its neurons had a realistic spatial structure and arrangement. We therefore first simulated an LIFterm·mth network (known to reproduce several features of cortical dynamics). Next, we injected the synaptic activity it generated into a synthetic three-dimensional network model (3D network) of a layer of a cortical column that employed multi-compartmental neurons with realistic morphology, spatial distributions of somata and synapses, and computed the extracellular potentials generated by this synaptic activity.

We selected an LIFterm·mth network (adapted from [ 14 ] and refined in [ 15 , 16 , 46 , 47 ]) that has been shown to reproduce a number of important features of the dynamics of visual primary cortical neural population recorded in vivo during naturalistic sensory stimulation, including a realistic spectrum of cortical dynamics and of its modulation with the visual stimuli, including low-frequency (1–12 Hz) and gamma (50–100 Hz) oscillations [ 15 , 46 ]. Moreover, when using a simple proxy (which is demonstrated below to perform well) to compute an LFPterm·dom from synaptic currentsterm·dom, this LIFterm·mth network reproduced quantitatively several important properties of recorded extracellular potentials, including LFPterm·dom power spectra and spectral information content [ 15 ], and cross-frequency and spike-field relationships [ 16 , 46 ]. Thus, the LIFterm·mth network seemed to generate a sufficiently realistic dynamics to provide synaptic input for the generation of biologically plausible LFPsterm·dom in the 3D network.

The LIFterm·mth network model ( Fig 1A ) was composed of 4000 excitatory and 1000 inhibitory LIFterm·mth neurons that were randomly connected with a pair-wise connection probability of 0.2 (for further details see Methods ). The LIFterm·mth network received two kinds of external inputs: a “thalamic” synaptic input thought to carry the information about the external stimuli and a stimulus-unrelated input representing slow ongoing fluctuations of activity. Synaptic dynamics and parameters are reported in Tables 1 and 2 , and further details can be found in the Methods. Importantly, as is the case for most LIFterm·mth network models to date, our LIFterm·mth network did not have any spatial structure: the individual neurons were not assigned to a specific spatial position and consequently the connectivity had a random and sparse structure.

A summary of the properties of the 3D network is reported in Table 3 , while the synaptic parameters are listed in Table 4 (see Methods for further details). In order to set up the 3D network we were required to make additional assumptions regarding the spatial positioning of cells, the shape and size of their dendritic structures, as well as the synaptic distributions. We focused on computing the LFPterm·dom generated by one cortical layer (in terms of soma positions) that comprised both inhibitory and excitatory neurons. In our default setting, we assumed all neurons in the 3D network to be inside two cylinders with 250 μm radius and 250 μm height that were stacked one above the other to resemble the vertical structure of layer 2/3 ( Fig 1B ). Note that this spatial scale is similar to the size of the neuronal pool contributing to the recorded LFPterm·dom, the so-called spatial reach, in the case of uncorrelated synaptic activity driving a neuronal population [ 20 , 21 , 35 ], and resulted in a neuronal density consistent with known estimates of 50000 neurons per mm 3 in the cortex [ 48 ]. While our two model populations most directly resemble a pair of excitatory and inhibitory populations in cortical L2/3, we show in subsection “Dependency of the LFPterm·dom signal on dendritic morphology” that our results also pertain to the LFPterm·dom generated by neuron morphologies found in other cortical layers.

Given these geometrical constraints, we created the multi-compartmental cell models in the 3D network in the following way: soma locations for all cells were homogeneously distributed within the lower cylinder ( Fig 1B ). Next, we placed artificial straight axons that were distributed at random cortical depths and random orientations within both cylinders. They served as targets in an algorithmic generation of dendrites, through which pyramidalterm·key cell dendrites were connected to all axons within a specified reach distance while optimizing the following wiring conditions: short conductions times, short total cable length and synaptic democracy (i.e., equal impact of synaptic inputs at the site of dendritic integration [ 49 , 50 ]). This procedure has been shown previously to reproduce pyramidal-cell-like dendrites [ 51 ]. The number of axons and their length were set so that the resulting cell morphologies matched the membrane surface distribution of real cortical layer 2/3 pyramidalterm·key cell reconstructions [ 52 ] within the constraints of the simplified columnar arrangement that was chosen for this study. This procedure also provided good matches for total cable lengths and number of branch points (compare membrane surface distribution in S1 Fig and see Methods for more details). Note that the virtual axons used for the generation of the morphologies were subsequently discarded. Since the membrane area (and consequently the transmembrane currentterm·dom) of the axons is very small compared to the dendrites, we expected them to have a negligible contribution to the present 3D network LFPterm·dom generation.

Stellate cell dendrites were generated in a similar manner, but were only connected to axons in the lower cylinder. This resulted in stellate cell morphologies with realistic bush-like dendrites. Fig 1C illustrates the overall structure of the resulting 3D network.

To further validate the simulationterm·mth results obtained with these morphologies, we also built an alternative 3D network with anatomically reconstructed morphologies (see Methods ) and checked that the results were essentially the same as for algorithmically grown morphologies (see subsection “ Performance of LFPterm·dom proxies in different dynamic network states ”).

Finally, AMPAterm·key synapses were homogeneously distributed over the whole neuronal surface while GABAterm·key synapses were located only in the lower cylinder, closer to the soma ( Fig 1B , see Methods for details). Alternative synaptic distributions are explored in the “Dependency of the LFPterm·dom signal on the distribution of synapses” subsection.

For each neuron in the LIFterm·mth network we randomly assigned a multi-compartmental neuron model with a unique dendritic structure in the 3D network. The connectivity of the LIFterm·mth network determined which postsynaptic spikesterm·dom in the LIFterm·mth network simulationterm·mth should serve as input spikesterm·dom for each multi-compartmentterm·key neuron. We then used these spiketerm·dom times together with the external input (see above) to activate synaptic currentsterm·dom in the 3D network (see Methods ). In this way we assured that the synaptic input in a multi-compartmentterm·key neuron was identical to its LIFterm·mth neuron counterpart. The synaptic dynamics in the 3D network was identical to that in the LIFterm·mth network. In a subsequent step, we took into account all transmembrane currentsterm·dom in the neurons of the 3D network to compute the LFPterm·dom by means of well-established volume conduction theory and the so called line-source method [ 31 , 34 ] (see Methods ).

We observed that all power spectra recorded outside this noise-dominated region had similar shapes ( Fig 2C and 2D ), suggesting that LFPterm·dom fluctuations could be roughly approximated by the same time series rescaled by the numerical value of the LFPterm·dom amplitude shown in Fig 2B . The observation that LFPsterm·dom recorded in different spatial positions had similar temporal behavior and differed mainly by a scaling factor, suggested that a single LFP proxyterm·key could work for recordings at different depths and positions in the horizontal plane, provided that it is properly scaled. Such a factorization of spatial and temporal dimensions can be expressed (see [ 30 ]) as L F P p r o x y ( r , d , t ) = f p r o x y ( r , d ) * g p r o x y ( t ) (1) where d is the depth and r the distance from the population center. The term f proxy ( r , d ) then gives the amplitude of the signal as a function of the electrode position (as in Fig 2B ) while the dimensionless g proxy ( t ) has variance equal to one and describes the temporal features of the LFPterm·dom signal.

We first focused on finding the optimal g proxy ( t ) for an LFPterm·dom signal recorded at selected depths along the central vertical axis ( X/R = 0 ) of the 3D network. However, we found (see subsection “ New class of LFPterm·dom proxies ”) that the identified optimal LFP proxyterm·key was applicable also to other depths and radial distances of the populations (given an appropriate overall scaling of the signal amplitudes, cf. Fig 2B ).

The contribution to the LFPterm·dom signal from synaptic inputs onto the interneurons (and their associated return currents) was negligible both in amplitude ( Fig 3A ) and in determining the LFPterm·dom spectrum ( Fig 3B ). This was due to the different morphologies of the two types of neurons: consistently with what was shown previously for stellate cells with symmetrically placed synapses [ 20 ] (i.e., a so-called close-field arrangement [ 53 ]), the contribution from the interneurons to the LFPterm·dom was negligible ( Fig 3 ). Further, the associated power spectrum of this contribution was closer to colored noise and did not display gamma fluctuations. We investigate this in detail in the subsection “Dependency of the LFPterm·dom signal on dendritic morphology” .

The proxies given by the combination of two synaptic parameters (WS and RWSterm·mth) have four free parameters (scale as described by the function f in Eq 1 and following, AMPAterm·key and GABAterm·key delays, relative amplitude of AMPAterm·key and GABAterm·key contribution) while the other proxies have only two free parameters (scale, delay). We assessed by means of the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC, [ 54 ], see Methods for details) whether the benefit in terms of improved performance of the models based on the linear combinations of synaptic currentsterm·dom was worth the increase in model complexity due to the higher number of parameters. We found that, according to this model selection criterion, RWSterm·mth outperforms all previous proxies and WS outperforms RWSterm·mth and all other proxies ( S3 Fig ), demonstrating the power of the RWSterm·mth and WS models. However, the optimal WS parameters are by construction different for each recording position and, as we will see in the following, for various network structures and states. Thus comparison of LFPterm·dom predictions from use of the WS proxy requires detailed knowledge about recording position as well as the characteristics of the underlying network, and will thus have limited practical use. On the other hand, as the parameters of the RWSterm·mth proxy are fixed, it can be used directly for all locations in space. As seen in the following, the RWSterm·mth proxy performs well for a broad set of conditions (input intensity, neuron morphology, synaptic distribution), and this means crucially that the proxy can be used also under weak assumptions about the spatial structure of the underlying network. We thus conclude that RWSterm·mth is the best LFP proxyterm·key based on LIFterm·mth network variables. In the following we will test its robustness for different dynamic network states, spatial architectures and synaptic properties.

However, LIFterm·mth networks can generate a variety of different dynamic network states when the frequency of external inputs is varied [ 14 , 15 , 18 ]. Dependency of the LFPterm·dom signal on dendritic morphology We hypothesized that the negligible contribution of inhibitory neurons was due to the weak dipole moment created by the symmetrically placed synapses on the dendrites of stellate cells [ 29 ]. To test this hypothesis we investigated in the following the effect of neuron shape on the LFPterm·dom generation by systematically altering the morphology of the interneuron population while keeping its inputs fixed. This manipulation also tested the robustness of the LFPterm·dom proxies to the specific choice of the neuronal morphology. We started with two overlapping cylinders (distance = 0 μm) describing the stellate cell morphology. Then we progressively increased their “pyramidalness”, i.e., the distance between the two dendritic bushes and generated a new interneuron population for each cylinder distance ( Fig 8A ; see Methods for details). The generated morphologies ranged from pure stellate cells (the interneuron used in the reference case), to cells corresponding to layer 2/3 pyramidalterm·key cells where the two cylinders were juxtaposed (the pyramidalterm·key neuron used in the reference case), to cells where the two areas were parted by several hundred micrometers (as in layer 5 pyramidalterm·key neurons). In all cases GABAterm·key synapses were distributed only on dendrites located inside the lower cylinder, while AMPAterm·key synapses were distributed over the entire dendritic tree ( Fig 1B ).

We found that the LFPterm·dom signal from the 1000 interneurons was very weak for cylinder distances less than about 100 μm, corresponding to a 40% overlap between the two cylinders (see Fig 8B and 8C ). The amplitude of the LFPterm·dom signal increased with the cylinder distance together with the current dipole moment ( Fig 8C and 8D ; see Methods ). The “transition distance” of about 100 μm is seen to be associated with the appearance of an inversion point in the LFPterm·dom ( Fig 8C ) and with the establishment of a sizable dipole moment ( Fig 8D ). Above this transition distance the LFPterm·dom became larger with larger cylinder separations, yet saturating somewhat for distances above about 250 μm, corresponding to our reference model of layer 2/3 pyramidalterm·key cell. This demonstrates that the lack of a sizable contribution to the overall LFPterm·dom from our interneurons in the reference model was due to their stellate morphologies.

Below the inter-cylinder transition distance all proxies performed poorly with average fraction of variance explained across depths smaller than 70% (for 100 μm the range was <R 2 > between 0.37 and 0.64), but <R 2 > quickly saturated as soon as the dipole appeared ( Fig 8E ). <R 2 > was smaller for all proxies compared to the reference case (since the noise was larger due to the smaller number of neurons, i.e., 1000 neurons versus 4000 neurons for the reference case), but the ranking of performances for different proxies remained roughly the same: above the transition distance the fraction of variance explained by WS was 83%, RWSterm·mth and the sum of absolute values of currents both explained 80%, the membrane potentialterm·key and sum of synaptic currentsterm·dom 59%, while firing rateterm·key explained only 47% of the variance. Note that for inter-cylinder distances above the transition distance, the stable performance of the proxies were accompanied by stable values of the optimal coefficient α ( Fig 8F ). This result implies that the RWSterm·mth we have found for populations of layer-2/3-like pyramidalterm·key cells, likely also can be applied to pyramidalterm·key cell populations with different morphologies, as long as they produce a dipolar LFPterm·dom.

In order to verify that the assumptions we made to algorithmically construct the neuronal morphologies in the 3D network did not bias the results, we also did simulationsterm·mth using realistic morphologies obtained from anatomical reconstructions (see Methods ). The spatiotemporal dynamics of these LFPterm·dom signals was found to be qualitatively very similar to the one previously shown, and the agreement with proxies was even higher, with RWSterm·mth reaching R 2 = 0.95 ( S4 Fig ). This result indicates that our conclusions are not strongly dependent on the detailed branching patterns within the basal and apical dendritic bushes.

Discussion The main aim of this work was to develop an accurate, robust and an easy-to-use method to synthesize the LFPterm·dom signal from output from a model network of LIFterm·mth neurons. We simulated a biophysically realistic LFPterm·dom signal using a population of morphologically detailed multi-compartmental neuron models and compared this LFPterm·dom signal with several LFP proxyterm·key candidates extracted from the LIFterm·mth network simulationsterm·mth. We found that a linear combination of summed and time-shifted AMPAterm·key and GABAterm·key currents in the LIFterm·mth network explained a large fraction of the variance of the LFPterm·dom of the 3D morphologically accurate network in nearly all conditions considered. Specifically, we identified a specific set of parameters ( Eq 4 ), the so-called reference weighted sum LFP proxyterm·mth (RWSterm·mth), which accurately predicted the LFPterm·dom time course for all considered electrode positions ( Fig 5 ), and across different dynamic network states ( Fig 6 ).

The fraction of LFPterm·dom variance explained by the RWSterm·mth proxy was only moderately affected by changes in neural morphology ( Fig 8 , S4 Fig ), in synaptic distribution ( Fig 9 ), or in synaptic dynamics ( Fig 10 ). This LFP proxyterm·key was found to be very accurate for every condition considered in which the dipole generated by the synaptic currentsterm·dom was sizable and hence the amplitude of the LFPterm·dom substantial. This LFP proxyterm·key only performed poorly in situations where the amplitude of the LFPterm·dom signal itself was very small, i.e., at the inversion point or when the resulting current dipole moments from synaptic activation are small (homogeneous synaptic distributions ( Fig 9 ), low activity ( Fig 6 )). Furthermore, we showed that despite the complexity of our LFPterm·dom simulationterm·mth setup (with 5000 different morphologies with realistic dendritic structures) the temporal evolution of the LFPterm·dom was well captured by the RWSterm·mth proxy based on synaptic currentsterm·dom with adjustment of only three parameters, the relative weight of the contributions from the two synaptic currentsterm·dom and the two synaptic delays. Our results further suggested that the RWSterm·mth proxy can be used for a wide range of LIFterm·mth network models and pyramidal-neuron morphologies to synthesize biophysically plausible LFPterm·dom signals that can be compared with experimental LFPterm·dom recordings. Thanks to its robustness, our proxy can expectedly be applied to models of any brain area in which the LFPterm·dom is likely to be generated by one dominant population (as in the hippocampus with a single population of pyramidalterm·key cells). When there are two or more populations giving a significant contribution to the LFPterm·dom (as is likely in whole cortical column model taking into account several pyramidalterm·key neuron populations, e.g., layer 2/3, layer 5, layer 6), the total LFPterm·dom can be approximated as a suitable linear combination of individual contributions if information on the depth positions of the populations relative to the recording electrode is available. Comparison of the model LFPterm·dom with experimental results might then be used to estimate the relative weights of the LFPterm·dom contributions from the different populations.

Comparison of candidate proxies A major difference between the accurate LFPterm·dom proxies using synaptic currentsterm·dom (sum of currents, WS, RWSterm·mth) compared to the less accurate proxy based on firing ratesterm·key is that a spiketerm·dom is a very local event in time, while the postsynaptic currentterm·dom following after a spiketerm·dom (as well as the contribution to the LFPterm·dom) lasts for many milliseconds. So an instantaneous firing rateterm·key proxy like the ones we are considering based on firing ratesterm·key cannot be expected to perform well (even with a fixed delay). In laminar population analysisterm·mth (LPAterm·mth, [ 30 ]) the LFPterm·dom time course was rather assumed to be given by the measured firing ratesterm·key convolved with a suitable (i.e., delayed exponential) kernel, the rationale being that spikesterm·dom causally drive synaptic currentsterm·dom which in turn set up the LFPterm·dom. The present RWSterm·mth proxy is similarly constructed, effectively corresponding to a suitable weighted sum of exponentially convolved presynaptic spiketerm·dom rates corresponding to excitatory and inhibitory synaptic currentsterm·dom. The postsynaptic soma membrane potentialsterm·key following presynaptic spiking is more low-pass filtered than the synaptic currentsterm·dom (and also the transmembrane return currents in the case of multicompartmental models) [ 29 ], and LFPterm·dom proxies based on this dynamical variable will generally fail to predict the most rapid temporal changes in the LFPterm·dom.

Spectral properties An interesting result is that all the proxies tested here displayed largely the same modulation of the LFPterm·dom gamma power as a function of input intensity, both in terms of relative power modulation and peak frequency ( Fig 7D and 7E ). This is encouraging since we did not specifically aim to find a good prediction of the power spectrum when constructing the LFPterm·dom proxies and estimating their parameters. We note however that no proxy is fully able to account for the low-frequency end of the spectrum ( Fig 7A and 7B ), which is overestimated by the membrane potentialterm·key proxy and underestimated by the other proxies. If one is interested in a highly detailed reproduction of the whole LFPterm·dom spectrum, preliminary results hint to the possibility of designing a WS fit optimized to match the spectrum instead than the spatiotemporal features and to define an LFP proxyterm·key that slightly differs from the RWSterm·mth discussed above. However, the fraction of spectral variance explained by the RWSterm·mth is already 0.91 (average over all stimulus intensities above 0.5 spikesterm·dom/ms, standard morphology and synaptic condition) which likely is sufficient for most purposes.

Model assumptions and limitations In the present work we have focused on how the relationship between LIFterm·mth variables and ground-truth LFPterm·dom change when the 3D model features change, keeping the LIFterm·mth model fixed. While different LIFterm·mth networks would generate different activity and hence different synaptic currentsterm·dom, we expect roughly the same relationship between these synaptic currentsterm·dom and the generated LFPterm·dom. Therefore, for any LIFterm·mth network generating enough correlated activity to result in a sizeable LFPterm·dom, we expect RWSterm·mth to be a good proxy.

Our strategy had the advantage that we could vary the assumptions in the LFP-generating model, e.g., the distribution of synapses or neuronal morphologies, without affecting the spiking dynamics. The disadvantage of this approach is, however, that the 3D network does not match the LIFterm·mth network in every respect; for instance, even though the synaptic input currents were identical in the two models, the resulting soma potentials in the multi-compartmental neurons were not identical to those in the LIFterm·mth neurons (due to passive dendritic filtering). It is, however, unlikely that imposing identical somatic potentials, or identical currents entering the soma, in the two models would result in a more realistic LFPterm·dom since large synaptic currentsterm·dom would be needed to overcome the passive filtering for distant synapses. Instead one could consider changing the synaptic weight distribution in the LIFterm·mth network simulationterm·mth to make the two models match better. Our focus here was to use LIFterm·mth models as commonly used in the literature (typically using homogeneous weight distributions), but it would be an interesting topic for future studies to extract effective point-neuron synaptic weight distributions from the multi-compartmental population and use these in the LIFterm·mth network simulationsterm·mth in order to make the two simulationterm·mth environments even more similar.

We did not test different LIFterm·mth network architectures or sizes, but we expect the RWSterm·mth proxy to be applicable as long as the network displays a sufficient level of correlation. We have found in previous modeling studies [ 20 , 21 ] that correlated synaptic activity is necessary to create a sizable LFPterm·dom signal, and in this case all cells in the dominant LFP-generating population will contribute. Making the network size larger or altering its connectivity would therefore likely not qualitatively change the form of the best LFP proxyterm·key (as long as a sufficient level of spiking correlations is maintained in the network). The LFPterm·dom generated by larger populations, however, should be tested in further studies taking into account the summed effect of several cortical populations, across layers as well as heterogeneous spatial structure in the horizontal direction.

A limitation of the presented simulationterm·mth setup is that it models only AMPAterm·key and GABAterm·key synapse contributions. However, most of our results pertaining to the proxy do not depend on the particular feature of the synapses and are therefore likely to extend to different synapses as well. For instance, it should not matter for the quality of our suggested proxy whether or not the synaptic weights are changing due to plasticity since the weight changes will be reflected in the synaptic currentsterm·dom extracted from the LIFterm·mth network as well. Including slower synapses, such as NMDA synapses in the model setup, will on the other hand affect the LFPterm·dom frequency content, particularly at low frequencies. This effect could be captured by a proxy including NMDA in the sum of synaptic currentsterm·dom with a weight depending on the number and spatial distribution of NMDA synapses. As with the synaptic weight distributions discussed above, the inclusion of NMDA synapses when computing the LFP proxyterm·key presupposes that it is also included in the LIFterm·mth network model (which was beyond the scope of this study). Moreover, we did not model subthreshold active dendritic conductance [ 55 ], nor the active channels behind spiketerm·dom generation. The contributions from the latter is expectedly negligible for at least the low frequencies of the LFPterm·dom [ 56 ] (but see [ 57 – 59 ]), while the effect of the former should be explored in future projects.

The present suggested proxy assumes the LFPterm·dom contribution following spikesterm·dom to be spatiotemporally separable, i.e., factorizable into a product of a function of time with a function of space [ 30 ]. Due to, for example, the intrinsic filtering effect [ 29 , 36 , 60 ] this is not strictly true as the spatial distribution of the transmembrane currentsterm·dom setting up the LFPterm·dom depends to some extent on the frequency. However, if warranted the present proxy can be extended, for example by assuming a more detailed proxy consisting of a sum of such spatiotemporally separable kernels.

Importance of this work for analytical studies of LFPsterm·dom Recently, we presented an analytical method to estimate the LFPterm·dom spectrum from the dynamics of a LIFterm·mth network [ 61 ] using as LFP proxyterm·key the sum of the absolute values of synaptic currentsterm·dom. By fitting a recurrent excitatory-inhibitory LIFterm·mth network model to LFPterm·dom recordings from monkeys presented with visual stimuli, we were able to estimate the LIFterm·mth model that best fitted the observed LFPterm·dom, and to predict at least in part the observed firing rateterm·key and some of the visual features in the receptive field that elicited the observed neural activity. In this recent work [ 61 ], the time evolution of the LFPterm·dom was computed analytically from the LIFterm·mth network as a function of the external input by applying linear response theory to the mean-field approximations of each kind of synaptic currentterm·dom separately and then summing their absolute values over pyramidalterm·key neurons (as in [ 15 ] and in Eq 2 ). In principle, it is possible to extend this analytical calculation by using the more efficient proxy presented here by simply changing the coefficients in the final sum of the synaptic currentsterm·dom. This paves the work for obtaining realistic analytical estimations of LFPsterm·dom from recurrent LIFterm·mth networks. As discussed in [ 35 ], an efficient analytical approach could be at the heart of the development of model-based analysis methods for performing inferential statistics of network models on LFPsterm·dom, analogous to the role played by Dynamic Causal Modelling [ 62 , 63 ] in the analysis of EEG and fMRI recordings.

Outlook Here we studied proxies for the LFPterm·dom produced by a local 3D network, corresponding to a single cortical layer. Experimentally recorded LFPsterm·dom, however, are most likely containing contributions from several layers [ 20 ]. Therefore, a natural extension of this work would be to study the LFPterm·dom generated by several connected 3D networks forming a full cortical columns [ 64 , 65 ] and determine how LFPterm·dom proxies should be designed in this context. Since electrical potentials in the nervous tissue are assumed to add linearly, we expect LFPterm·dom proxies to be constructed in largely the same manner as presented here, by summing synaptic contributions from different cortical layers, possibly with a weighting depending on the recording depths. Constructing the LFPterm·dom signal from a full cortical column model [ 65 ] is the topic for a separate ongoing project [ 66 ].

We expect our proxy to also work well for other brain structures where pyramidalterm·key neurons are elongated and arranged in an almost parallel way, such as the CA1 and CA2 regions of the hippocampus. On the other hand, many subcortical structures have a neuronal architecture so different from the cortex that we that we cannot a priori expect the present rules of LFPterm·dom prediction to be applicable. A possible future line of research will be to apply the combination of LIFterm·mth dynamics and 3D morphology we used in this work to investigate such areas to find a compact way to study the mechanisms generating the LFPterm·dom observed there.

We focused in the present study on the LFPterm·dom signal, but finding good models for relating activity in spiking network models and experimentally measured signals is relevant also for other types of commonly recorded signals such as the EEG, MEG and VSD. Since the biophysical mechanisms generating these signals are in principle known, we believe our framework could be extended to also study other measurement modalities in the future.

Methods Leaky integrate-and-fireterm·mth (LIFterm·mth) recurrent network model We summarize here the structure of the LIFterm·mth network that generated the spiking dynamics. We refer to [ 15 , 46 ] for full details. The network was composed of LIFterm·mth neurons with current-based synapses whose time evolution was modeled as difference between exponentials (see below), fixed threshold, fixed refractory time [ 67 ], and fixed conduction delay of 1 ms. Morphological model of a simplified layered cortical column In order to compute the transmembrane currentsterm·dom that lead to an LFPterm·dom signal, we constructed 3D morphological neuron models that captured the main morphological features of the cortical network described by point neurons in the LIFterm·mth model. The algorithm used to construct the model morphologies was based on the fact that dendrites connect to their presynaptic partners in a manner minimizing their total length and conduction times from all synapses to the soma [ 71 ]. In such a framework, pyramidalterm·key cell dendrites can be seen as tree structures connecting as directly as possible to axons that are distributed in two distinct layers [ 51 ]. The generation of synthetic trees and subsequent analysis were performed using the TREES toolbox [ 71 , 72 ] [ http://www.treestoolbox.org ]. Two cylinders (250 μm radius and 250 μm height each) were therefore stacked to form a cylindrical column ( Fig 1B ). Somata of all cells were homogeneously distributed in the lower cylinder for both cell types. Axons were distributed isotropically within planes perpendicular to the cortical depth at random depth values. Pyramidalterm·key cells were connected first to the axons in the upper cylinder and then to the axons of the lower cylinder, this resulted in characteristic apical and basal dendritic trees. Stellate cells were only connected to the axons in the lower cylinder. Using 160 axons in each layer and a maximal reach distance of 150 μm for any dendrite to an input axon, resulted in realistic membrane surfaces, cable lengths and branch point number distributions (see S1 Fig ). Diameter taper was selected to equalize synaptic democracy [ 73 ] and yielded good fits to the real counterparts with similar parameters for interneurons and pyramidalterm·key cells. The resulting pyramidalterm·key cell somatic input resistance was about 200 MΩ with specific membrane resistances R m = 20000 Ωcm 2 and axial resistances R a = 150 Ωcm. The stellate cell input resistance was 175 MΩ with specific membrane resistances R m = 10000 Ωcm 2 and axial resistances R a = 150 Ωcm. In order to spatially embed the simplified LIFterm·mth network model, 4000 such pyramidalterm·key cells and 1000 interneurons were generated to populate the simplified columnar architecture. The resulting morphologies were then exported to NEURON [ 74 , 75 ] using the TREES toolbox functions.

In order to continuously alter the “pyramidalness” of cortical neurons as in Fig 8 we simply modulated the distance between the two cylinders corresponding to the two layers. With a distance of 0 μm, a perfect overlap of both cylinders, the resulting shape was symmetric as for the stellate cell. As the distance was increased between the two cylinders, the shape of the cortical cell traversed the shape of layer 2/3 pyramidalterm·key cells (distance of 250 μm), layer 4 pyramidalterm·key cells (distance of 350 μm) and layer 5 pyramidalterm·key cells (distance larger than 500 μm). The corresponding validation of morphological features compared with real dendrite reconstructions as can be observed in S1 Fig .

As a control for use of algorithmically constructed morphologies we derived an alternative model using multiple copies of real reconstructions distributed within the columnar arrangement. We used reconstructions from NeuroMorpho.org [ 76 ], made available by the group of Markram [ 76 ], of both stellate cells and layer 2/3 pyramidalterm·key cells in young rat somatosensory cortex. Since only 4 stellate cells and 36 layer 2/3 pyramidalterm·key cell morphologies were available, we reached the number of 1000 interneurons and 4000 pyramidalterm·key neurons by randomly selecting copies of the smaller sample and distributing them within the simplified columnar geometry. Cell body locations were chosen to preserve a fairly homogeneous distribution of membrane throughout the cylinders. This alternative model was then injected with the same synaptic currentterm·dom stimuli as the original model based on algorithmically developed morphologies, and yielded similar results (compare Fig 6 and S4 Fig ).
