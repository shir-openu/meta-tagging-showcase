senseMicrobiome versus microbiota - the review uses microbiome for the gene and metabolite capacity and microbiota for the organisms and their composition — “The human gastrointestinal (GI) tract contains an abundant and diverse microbial community that gathers more than 100 trillion microorganisms \ [1\]. The density of bacterial cells in the colon has been estimated at 1011 to 1012 per milliliter which makes the colon one of the most densely populated microbial habitats known on earth \ [2\]. The gut microbiome encodes over 3 million genes producing thousands of metabolites, whereas the human genome consists of approximately 23,000 genes \ [3\]. For years, scientists have been interested in gut microbiota, but one of the major difficulties in the relevant research has been the ability to culture these microorganisms \ [4\]. During the last years, new technologies have allowed researchers to phylogenetically identify and/or quantify the components of the gut microbiota by analyzing nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) directly extracted from stools. The majority of these techniques are based on the extraction of DNA and the amplification of the 16S ribosomal RNA gene (rRNA) \ [5, 6\]. 16S rRNA sequencing has become the most useful technique to highlight diversity and abundance of the microbiome. The 16S rRNA gene sequences can be exploited with polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and metagenomics sequencing to characterize the microbial strains”
senseSuperorganism - the host and resident microorganisms form a symbiotic functional unit while individual species composition remains personal — “Considering the characteristics of gut microbiota such as the large diversity, the stability and resilience, and the symbiotic interaction with the host, we can define the host and the microorganisms inhabiting it as a “superorganism” \ [8, 9\] which performs immune and metabolic functions \ [1\]. Gut bacteria are key regulators of digestion along the gastrointestinal tract; commensal bacteria play an important role in the extraction, synthesis, and absorption of many nutrients and metabolites, including bile acids, lipids, amino acids, vitamins, and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). Gut microbiota have a crucial immune function against pathogenic bacteria colonization inhibiting their growth, consuming available nutrients and/or producing bacteriocins. Gut microbiota also prevent bacteria invasion by maintaining the intestinal epithelium integrity \ [10\]. Microorganisms prevent pathogenic colonization by many competition processes: nutrient metabolism, pH modification, antimicrobial peptide secretions, and effects on cell signaling pathways. Moreover, recent studies have identified a critical role for commensal bacteria and their products in regulating the development, homeostasis, and function of innate and adaptive immune cells \ [11\]. It is paradoxical to note that the gut microbiota functions are highly preserved between individuals, whereas each individual’s gut microbiota are characterized by a specific combination of bacterial species due to inter-individual and intra-individual variations throughout human life”
senseAnatomical gradient - microbial quantity increases toward the large intestine as aerobes give way to strict anaerobes — “Gut microbiota vary according to the intestine anatomical regions, which vary in terms of physiology, pH and O2 tension, digesta flow rates (rapid in the mouth to the caecum, slower afterward), substrate availability, and host secretions \ [14\]. The small intestine provides a more challenging environment for microbial colonizers given the fairly short transit times (3–5 h) and the high bile concentrations. The large intestine, which is characterized by slow flow rates and neutral to mildly acidic pH, harbors by far the largest microbial community (dominated by obligate anaerobic bacteria) \ [14\]. We can observe a microbiota quantitative increasing gradient and a microbiota qualitative decreasing gradient with a progressive aerobic bacteria decrease for the benefit of strictly anaerobic bacteria”
senseEarly-life provenance - gestational age, delivery route, milk exposure, and weaning shape a native core before adult stability — “Each individual is provided with a unique gut microbiota profile that plays many specific functions in host nutrient metabolism, maintenance of structural integrity of the gut mucosal barrier, immunomodulation, and protection against pathogens. Gut microbiota are composed of different bacteria species taxonomically classified by genus, family, order, and phyla. Each human’s gut microbiota are shaped in early life as their composition depends on infant transitions (birth gestational date, type of delivery, methods of milk feeding, weaning period) and external factors such as antibiotic use. These personal and healthy core native microbiota remain relatively stable in adulthood but differ between individuals due to enterotypes, body mass index (BMI) level, exercise frequency, lifestyle, and cultural and dietary habits. Accordingly, there is not a unique optimal gut microbiota composition since it is different for each individual. However, a healthy host–microorganism balance must be respected in order to optimally perform metabolic and immune functions and prevent disease development. This review will provide an overview of the studies that focus on gut microbiota balances in the same individual and between individuals and highlight the close mutualistic relationship between gut microbiota variations and diseases. Indeed, dysbiosis of gut microbiota is associated not only with intestinal disorders but also with numerous extra-intestinal diseases such as metabolic and neurological disorders. Understanding the cause or consequence of these gut microbiota balances in health and disease and how to maintain or restore a healthy gut microbiota composition should be useful in developing promising therapeutic interventions”
senseAntibiotic resilience - community recovery is substantial but repeated exposure can leave memory and a persistent alternative regime — “A cultivation-independent survey of gut microbiota composition of three persons before, during, and after two exposures to the same antibiotic (ciprofloxacin) \ [48\] has been conducted. This study revealed a considerable resilience to antibiotic administration but also suggested that, in some cases, the system retains a memory of past disturbance and that, in all cases, repeated disturbance leads to a persistent regime shift \ [48\]. The impact of antibiotic disturbance on the resilience of microbiota during future antibiotic treatments can thus also vary considerably across individuals”
senseEnterotype function - clusters are organized by shared metabolic functions and substrate use, not merely enumerated bacterial membership — “Gut microbiota of each individual are specifically characterized by clusters of bacteria named enterotypes \ [13\]. Three enterotypes are characterized by three dominant bacteria clusters ( Table 2): _Bacteroides_ (enterotype I), _Prevotella_ (enterotype II), or _Ruminococcus_ (enterotype III). Each enterotype harbors different bacteria genera ( Table 1). These three enterotypes are not only enumerations of bacteria; they are also specifically regrouped by functions. Indeed, an enterotype is a functional harmonious association of several bacteria species rather than a systematic addition of bacteria species. Every enterotype is not a clear-cut identity such as blood groups; however, enterotypes characterize individuals, remain stable from adulthood, and can be restored if they are modified. Each enterotype with its distinctive clusters of bacteria and respective functional characteristics defines a distinctive way of generating energy from fermentable substrates available in the colon. Indeed, bacteria clusters of enterotype I derive energy primarily from carbohydrates using principally glycolysis and pentose phosphate pathways, whereas bacteria clusters of enterotypes II and III are able to degrade mucin glycoproteins of the gut mucosal layer. Enterotypes seem to be principally defined according to dietary habits. Understanding the origins and functions of enterotypes may improve the knowledge of the relationships between gut microbiota and human health”
senseDiet-response timescale - changing to an animal-based diet alters taxa and fermentation activity within about one day — “While the microbiome of a healthy individual is relatively stable, gut microbial dynamics can certainly be influenced by lifestyle and dietary cultural choices \ [56\]. A study of European children (fed with the Western diet) and Burkina Faso children (assuming a diet rich in millet/sorghum + local vegetables containing very few lipids and animal proteins) \ [57\] revealed that African children’s microbiota have a remarkable abundance of _Prevotella_ and _Xylanibacter_. Furthermore, _Shigella_ and _Escherichia_ are widely under-represented. Another study \ [58\] compared Hadza hunter-gatherers and Italian gut microbiota. At the phylum level, Hadza gut microbiota are largely enriched in Proteobacteria and Spirochaetes, which are extremely rare in the Italian gut microbiota, whereas Actinobacteria, an important subdominant component of the Italian gut microbiota, are almost absent. At the genus level, Hadza gut microbiota are comparatively enriched in _Prevotella_, _Eubacterium_, _Oscillibacter_, _Butyricicoccus_, _Sporobacter_, _Succinivibrio_, and _Treponema_ and correspondingly depleted in _Bifidobacterium_, _Bacteroides_, _Blautia_, _Dorea_, unclassified _Lachnospiraceae_, _Roseburia_, _Faecalibacterium_, _Ruminococcus,_ and unclassified _Erysipelotrichaceae_. These two studies showed that African gut microbiota have clearly an enterotype _Prevotella_ (enterotype II); the African diet rich in millet/sorghum + local vegetables containing very few lipids and animal proteins allows a mucine degradation in synergy with _Desulfovibrionaceae_. European gut microbiota (Western diet) have principally an enterotype _Bacteroides_ (enterotype I): the European diet rich in lipids and animal proteins allows energy production from carbohydrates and proteins through fermentation \ [13\]. David et al. \ [59\] compared plant-based diet microbiome (high fibers and low fats and proteins) with animal-based diet microbiome (low fibers and high fats and proteins) and respective changes in microbiome on either diet and demonstrated that a shift toward an animal-based diet increases the abundance of bile-tolerant microorganisms ( _Alistipes_, _Bilophila_, and _Bacteroides_) and decreases the levels of Firmicutes that metabolize dietary plant polysaccharides ( _Roseburia_, _Eubacterium rectale_, and _Ruminococcus bromii_). Moreover, microbial activity trades off between carbohydrate and protein fermentation when the type of diet respectively changes. This change occurred only one day after the diet reached the distal gut microbiota. David et al. \ [59\] showed that diet alters human microbiota rapidly and reproducibly”
senseDysbiosis causal boundary - compositional alteration may be either cause or consequence, and the review repeatedly distinguishes association from demonstrated causation — “Gut microbiota composition is highly variable. The variation itself is considered as physiological in the context of healthy gut microbiota, according to age, ethnicity, lifestyle, and dietary habits. However, these physiological gut microbiota variations have huge implications in intestinal and extra-intestinal disorders. Indeed, dysbiosis is often defined as an alteration of gut microbiota composition and a cause or a consequence of disorders. It is often difficult to ascertain whether the change is beneficial or detrimental. As for the human conditions, changes represent a challenge for a better adaptation in a new context and may potentiate resilience. However, a chronic external stimulation may be stressful and disruptive for an unstructured ecosystem. We will discuss the correlations between several diseases and alterations of gut microbiota composition”
senseIBD causal boundary - microbial imbalance is likely to contribute to severity, but the cited results do not demonstrate that it causes IBD pathophysiology — “Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) gather idiopathic, chronic, and relapsing inflammatory conditions of the gastrointestinal tract including ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CRD). The findings of Frank et al. \ [66\] indicated a diminution of _Lachnospiraceae_ and Bacteroidetes microbial populations and an increase in Proteobacteria families in IBD patients compared to control gut microbiota. Gut microbiota variations appear to be different between UC and CRD. Indeed, a decreased abundance of the butyrate-producing bacteria _Roseburia hominis_ and _Faecalibacterium prausnitzii_ has been observed in UC patients relative to controls \ [67\], whereas the opposite has been observed in CRD patients, who are provided with increased _Faecalibacterium prausnitzii_ levels in addition to a reduced overall diversity \ [68\]. In the case of CRD patients, another study \ [69\] demonstrated a decrease in _Dialister invisus_, an uncharacterized species of _Clostridium_ spp., in _Faecalibacterium prausnitzii_, and in _Bifidobacterium adolescentis_ and an increase in _Ruminococcus gnavus_. Sokol et al. \ [70\] confirmed the reduction of _F. prausnitzii_ in CRD patients and also demonstrated that an oral administration of live _Faecalibacterium prausnitzii_ reduced the severity of colitis and tended to correct the dysbiosis. These results suggest that counterbalancing dysbiosis using _Faecalibacterium prausnitzii_ as a probiotic is a promising strategy in CRD treatment \ [70\]. These results do not demonstrate a causal relationship between microbial dysbiosis and IBD pathophysiology, but they rather suggest that gut microbial unbalances are likely to contribute to disease severity”
senseObesity evidence boundary - animal studies provide most causal evidence while human microbial-composition data are more variable — “Animal studies constitute the majority of causative evidence linking changes in microbial composition to obesity while, in humans, data are more variable. In addition, although the majority of mouse gut species are unique, the mouse and human microbiota are similar at the division level, with Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes dominating \ [83\]. The analysis \ [83\] of gut microbiota of genetically obese ob/ob mice and lean mice all fed the same polysaccharide-rich diet revealed a lower relative abundance (50%) of Bacteroidetes in obese ob/ob mice, whereas the Firmicutes are correspondingly higher. Indeed, higher numbers of _Ruminococcaceae_ and _Rikenellaceae_ have been observed in leptin-resistant obese (leptin-promoting satiety) and diabetic mice (db/db) compared with their lean littermates \ [84\]. In addition, the higher abundance of _Ruminococcaceae_ and _Rikenellaceae_ in mice fed a high-fat diet is not only dependent on the ingested diet but is also closely linked with obesity and type 2 diabetes \ [85\]. Furthermore, the association between _Desulfovibrionaceae_ abundance and obesity and type 2 diabetes has been demonstrated \ [86, 87\]. Other studies \ [50, 88\] confirmed the role of Proteobacteria in obesity by producing pro-inflammatory molecules such as lipopolysaccharides and helping to harvest energy and increase host fat storage \ [88\]. On the contrary, researchers \ [86, 89\] have recently demonstrated that _Akkermansia muciniphila_, a mucin-degrading bacteria living in the mucus layer \ [90\], decreases in both genetically and diet-induced obese mice. All these studies highlight the finding that obesity is associated with changes in the composition of gut microbiota including lower species diversity and shifts in the abundance of genes involved in metabolism. These gut microbiota variations affect the microbiome which has an increased capacity to harvest energy from the diet and these changes are transmissible \ [91\]. Gut microbiota can be considered as a contributing factor to the pathophysiology of obesity and may have potential therapeutic implications”

relationenterotype → distinctive way of generating energy from fermentable substrates — Gut microbiota of each individual are specifically characterized by clusters of bacteria named enterotypes \ [13\]. Three enterotypes are characterized by three dominant bacteria clusters ( Table 2): _Bacteroides_ (enterotype I), _Prevotella_ (enterotype II), or _Ruminococcus_ (enterotype III). Each enterotype harbors different bacteria genera ( Table 1). These three enterotypes are not only enumerations of bacteria; they are also specifically regrouped by functions. Indeed, an enterotype is a functional harmonious association of several bacteria species rather than a systematic addition of bacteria species. Every enterotype is not a clear-cut identity such as blood groups; however, enterotypes characterize individuals, remain stable from adulthood, and can be restored if they are modified. Each enterotype with its distinctive clusters of bacteria and respective functional characteristics defines a distinctive way of generating energy from fermentable substrates available in the colon. Indeed, bacteria clusters of enterotype I derive energy primarily from carbohydrates using principally glycolysis and pentose phosphate pathways, whereas bacteria clusters of enterotypes II and III are able to degrade mucin glycoproteins of the gut mucosal layer. Enterotypes seem to be principally defined according to dietary habits. conceptenterotype I — Bacteroides-dominated enterotype — “Gut microbiota of each individual are specifically characterized by clusters of bacteria named enterotypes \ [13\]. Each enterotype harbors different bacteria genera ( Table 1). These three enterotypes are not only enumerations of bacteria; they are also specifically regrouped by functions. Indeed, an enterotype is a functional harmonious association of several bacteria species rather than a systematic addition of bacteria species. Every enterotype is not a clear-cut identity such as blood groups; however, enterotypes characterize individuals, remain stable from adulthood, and can be restored if they are modified. Enterotypes seem to be principally defined according to dietary habits. # Abstract

Each individual is provided with a unique gut microbiota profileterm that plays many specific functions in host nutrient metabolismterm, maintenance of structural integrity of the gut mucosal barrierterm, immunomodulationterm, and protection against pathogensterm. Gut microbiotaterm are composed of different bacteria speciesterm taxonomically classified by genus, family, order, and phyla. ## 1\. Introduction

The human gastrointestinal (GI) tractterm contains an abundant and diverse microbial community that gathers more than 100 trillion microorganisms \ [1\]. The density of bacterial cells in the colon has been estimated at 1011 to 1012 per milliliter which makes the colon one of the most densely populated microbial habitats known on earth \ [2\]. The gut microbiometerm encodes over 3 million genes producing thousands of metabolitesterm, whereas the human genometerm consists of approximately 23,000 genes \ [3\]. For years, scientists have been interested in gut microbiotaterm, but one of the major difficulties in the relevant research has been the ability to culture these microorganisms \ [4\]. During the last years, new technologies have allowed researchers to phylogenetically identify and/or quantify the components of the gut microbiotaterm by analyzing nucleic acids (DNA and RNAterm) directly extracted from stools. The majority of these techniques are based on the extraction of DNA and the amplification of the 16S ribosomal RNA gene (rRNA)term \ [5, 6\]. 16S rRNA sequencingterm has become the most useful technique to highlight diversity and abundance of the microbiometerm. The 16S rRNA gene sequences can be exploited with polymerase chain reaction (PCR)term and metagenomics sequencingterm to characterize the microbial strains \ [7\].

Considering the characteristics of gut microbiotaterm such as the large diversityterm, the stability and resilienceterm, and the symbiotic interaction with the hostterm, we can define the host and the microorganisms inhabiting itterm as a “superorganismterm” \ [8, 9\] which performs immune and metabolic functions \ [1\]. Gutterm bacteria are key regulators of digestion along the gastrointestinal tract; commensal bacteriaterm play an important role in the extraction, synthesis, and absorption of many nutrients and metabolitesterm, including bile acids, lipids, amino acids, vitamins, and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)term. Gut microbiotaterm have a crucial immune function against pathogenic bacteria colonizationterm inhibiting their growth, consuming available nutrients and/or producing bacteriocins. Gut microbiotaterm also prevent bacteria invasion by maintaining the intestinal epithelium integrityterm \ [10\]. Microorganisms prevent pathogenic colonization by many competition processes: nutrient metabolismterm, pH modificationterm, antimicrobial peptide secretionsterm, and effects on cell signaling pathwaysterm. Moreover, recent studies have identified a critical role for commensal bacteriaterm and their products in regulating the development, homeostasis, and function of innate and adaptive immune cellsterm \ [11\]. It is paradoxical to note that the gut microbiotaterm functions are highly preserved between individuals, whereas each individual’s gut microbiotaterm are characterized by a specific combination of bacterial species due to inter-individual and intra-individual variationsterm throughout human life.

This review aims to define what would be the optimal gut microbiotaterm composition in order to maintain these optimal microbiota immune and metabolic functions. After defining microbiota variations within and between individuals, we highlight the involvement of gut microbiota dysbiosisterm in various intestinal and extra-intestinal disorder development and we describe the strong relationship between gut microbiotaterm diversityterm and health. We will see how these gut microbiotaterm variations may have huge implications for intestinal and extra-intestinal disordersterm and, consequently, may influence health.

#### 2.1.1. Intestine Anatomical Regions

Gut microbiotaterm vary according to the intestine anatomical regions, which vary in terms of physiology, pH and O2 tension, digesta flow rates (rapid in the mouth to the caecum, slower afterward), substrate availability, and host secretions \ [14\]. The small intestineterm provides a more challenging environment for microbial colonizers given the fairly short transit timesterm (3–5 h) and the high bile concentrationsterm. The large intestineterm, which is characterized by slow flow rates and neutral to mildly acidic pH, harbors by far the largest microbial community (dominated by obligate anaerobic bacteriaterm) \ [14\]. We can observe a microbiota quantitative increasing gradientterm and a microbiota qualitative decreasing gradientterm with a progressive aerobic bacteria decrease for the benefit of strictly anaerobic bacteria ( Table 1).

#### 2.1.2. Infant Transitionsterm

##### Birth Gestational Ageterm

Birth gestational ageterm is a major determinant of gut microbiotaterm colonization. The microbiota composition of preterm infantsterm (<37 weeks of gestation) is different from term counterparts ( Table 1). In preterm infantsterm, after birth, the microbiota colonization is challenged by organ immaturityterm and environmental factors such as antibiotic use, hospital stay \ [15\], and enteral feeding \ [16\]. For these reasons, the postnatal maturation of gut and systemic immunityterm may be highly affected by preterm birth \ [16\].

In a new-born pig model, an analysis of the gut microbiotaterm composition of preterm and term pigs showed that _Ruminococcus_ spp., some _Enterobacterium_ spp., _Lachnospiraceae_, _Peptostreptococcaceae_, and _Clostridiaceae_ were dominant genera in both preterm and term pigs. However, a higher _Enterococcus_ spp. abundance in preterm than term pigs was found \ [16\].

Preterm infantsterm show low diversityterm with an increased colonization of potentially pathogenic bacteria from the _Enterobacteriaceaeterm_ family of the Proteobacteriaterm phylum \ [15\] and reduced levels of strict anaerobesterm such as _Bifidobacteriumterm_ \ [17\], _Bacteroidesterm_, and _Atopobium_ \ [15\].

Human milk composition depends on genetic factors and the mother’s secretor and Lewis blood groupsterm defining four phenotypes characterized by different levels of oligosaccharide amounts \ [18\]. Higher levels of Proteobacteriaterm and lower levels of Firmicutesterm are noted in premature infants of non-secretor mothers \ [19\]. A recent study by Praticò et al. \ [20\] characterizing human breast milk composition demonstrated that human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs)term related to different mother phenotypes modulate gut microbiotaterm composition in infants. Specifically, HMOs associated with secretor mothersterm may have a protective prebiotic effectterm by decreasing pathogens associated with sepsisterm and necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC)term \ [19\]. This indicates that HMOs may influence the intestinal microbiota and prevent gutterm dysfunction and NEC in preterm infantsterm \ [21\].

Lactoferrin (LF)term is another well-known component of human milk favoring gutterm infant colonization with beneficial bacteriaterm and consequently representing a crucial role played in preterm infantsterm’ microbiota \ [22\]. Indeed, correlations between LF and beneficial microbiota in breast milk and infant’s feces have been demonstrated \ [22\]. High levels of fecal LF, particularly in preterm infantsterm, may represent an important factor in the initiation, development, and/or composition of the neonatal gut microbiotaterm contributing to the immunologic maturation and well-being of the newborn, especially in preterm infantsterm \ [22\].

Therefore, a mother’s own milkterm helps to shape developing core gut microbiotaterm that can improve growth and neurodevelopment and decrease the risk of NEC and late-onset sepsisterm \ [19, 21\]. It is for all these reasons that human milk should be the primary enteral diet of premature infants.

##### Type of Deliveryterm·var

At birth, the intestine is sterile and devoid of bacteria. After birth, a rich and dynamic ecosystem develops from mother’s skin, vaginal and fecal microbiota, and environment microbiota contacts. Microbiota colonization varies according to the type of deliveryterm·var \ [23, 24, 25\] ( Table 1).

With regard to vaginal deliveryterm, newborns acquire a microbiota composition resembling their mother’s vaginal microbiotaterm. Indeed, an analysis of the meconium of newborn infants \ [24\] revealed a strong correlation between the microbiota of the newborn digestive tract and the microbial communities of the mother’s vagina: _Lactobacillus_, _Prevotellaterm,_ and _Sneathia_. Biasucci et al. \ [25\] showed that the microbiota of vaginally delivered infants are also characterized by predominant groups such as _Bifidobacteriumterm longum_ and _Bifidobacteriumterm catenulatum_. Other facultative anaerobic species such as _Escherichia coli_, _Staphylococcus_, _Bacteroidesterm fragilis_, and _Streptococcus_ colonize the infant gutterm \ [26, 27, 28\].

On the contrary, infants born by cesarean section (C-section)term acquire bacteria derived from hospital environmentterm and mother’s skin: _Staphylococcus, Corynebacterium, Propionibacterium_ spp. \ [24, 29\]. _Escherichia, Shigella,_ and _Bacteroidesterm_ species are underrepresented in infants born by cesarean delivery \ [29\]. The intestinal microbiota of neonates delivered by cesarean delivery are less diverse in terms of bacteria speciesterm than the microbiota of vaginally delivered infants \ [25\].

Differences between the microbiota of C-section and vaginally born infants have been also detected in analyses performed at 7 years of age \ [23\]. Cesarean birth has been associated with an increased risk of chronic immune disorders such as asthma, systemic connective tissue disorders, juvenile arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease \ [30\], and obesity \ [31\].

##### Methods of Milk Feeding

Studies have demonstrated that formula-fed infantsterm are more often colonized with _Escherichia coli_, _Bacteroidesterm_, and _Clostridiumterm difficile_ compared with breastfed infantsterm \ [29, 32\]. Regarding Actinobacteriaterm abundance, _Bifidobacteriumterm_ spp. have been associated with breastfeeding and formula milk \ [33, 34\]. However, breastfed infantsterm generally harbor a more complex and diverse _Bifidobacteriumterm_ microbiota than formula-fed infantsterm \ [34\]. Breastfed infantsterm are provided with microbiota having more than a two-fold increase in the number of _Bifidobacteriumterm_ cells compared to formula-fed infantsterm \ [33\]. Breastfed infantsterm have more beneficial gut microbiotaterm, with a higher richness and diversityterm of _Bifidobacteriumterm_ spp. and a lower number of _Clostridiumterm difficile_ and _Escherichia coli_ than formula-fed infantsterm \ [32\] ( Table 1).

_Bifidobacteriumterm_ spp. are responsible for the fermentation of galactooligosaccharide (GOS)term, one of the main components of breast milk, to produce SCFAsterm \ [35\]. Indeed, _Bifidobacteriumterm_ spp. have an enzyme named lacto-N-biosidaseterm, which facilitates the assimilation of GOS \ [36\]. If _Bifidobacteriumterm_ quantity increases, the quantity of HMOs in feces decreases causing elevated acetate and lactate concentrations and decreasing pH \ [37\]; an HMO consumptionterm by Bifidobacteria in the infant’s gutterm may suggest a prebiotic effect of HMOs on gutterm infant microbiota by selectively stimulating _Bifidobacteriumterm_ spp. \ [37\]. Another prebiotic effect of β-palmitateterm, a natural human milk fatty acid and a component of some infant formulas, on gutterm infant microbiota was demonstrated by positively influencing _Bifidobacteriumterm_ spp. (and _Lactobacillus_ spp.) abundance \ [38\].

Furthermore, the maintenance of a healthy and balanced mother’s gut microbiotaterm during pregnancy is also considered as an important factor in positively influencing the milk microbiota composition \ [39\]. Additionally, oral probiotic supplementation in mothers with vaginal deliveryterm may increase breast milk _Bifidobacteriumterm_ spp. and _Lactobacilli_ spp. abundance \ [39\].

These studies have shown that native core microbiotaterm·def are shaped during early life: the profile of intestinal microbiota in the full-term, vaginally delivered, and breastfed infant with healthy and balanced mother’s milk microbiota is considered healthy. Researches should seek to demonstrate the role of this native core microbiotaterm·def composition on gutterm health and subsequent diseasesterm.

##### Weaning Period

The introduction of solid foodsterm and the termination of milk-feeding/weaning coincide with major gut microbiotaterm changes. The abundance of genera _Bifidobacteriumterm_, _Clostridiumterm coccoides_, and _Bacteroidesterm_ are predominant after weaning \ [40\]. The microbiota of breastfed children and the microbiota of children previously fed with formula milk become closer to each other ( Table 1).

Dietary habitsterm, infant weaning, and feeding practices become determinants and play a crucial role in gut microbiotaterm variations. Furthermore, Fallani et al. \ [40\] demonstrated that northern European countries are associated with a higher proportion of Bifidobacteria in the infant gut microbiotaterm, whereas higher levels of _Bacteroidesterm_ and _Lactobacilli_ characterize southern European countries.

#### 2.1.3. Age

At one year old, a child’s microbiota composition has a characteristic abundance of _Akkermansia muciniphilaterm_, Bacteroidesterm, _Veillonella_, _Clostridiumterm coccoides_ spp., and _Clostridiumterm botulinum_ spp. \ [42\].

At approximately three years of age, a child’s gut microbiotaterm composition and diversityterm are most like those of adults \ [43\].

With regard to older people over the age of 70term, gut microbiotaterm composition can be affected by digestion and nutrient absorption changes and immune activity weakness. Dietary habit changes (more monotonous) may also weaken gut microbiotaterm diversityterm. A decrease in anaerobic bacteria such as _Bifidobacteriumterm_ spp. and an increase in _Clostridiumterm_ and Proteobacteriaterm have been observed \ [44\]. Given the role of _Bifidobacteriumterm_ spp. in the stimulation of the immune system and metabolic processes, a Bifidobacteria decrease may partially explain low systemic inflammatory status and malnutrition in older adults \ [45\].

#### 2.1.4. Antibiotics

Gut microbiotaterm composition can be more or less affected by antibiotic use. A follow-up study explored the effect of antibiotics with different modes of action on human gut microbiotaterm composition \ [46\] and demonstrated that antibiotic treatmentsterm modify the gut microbiotaterm composition with an abundance/appearance of certain species and a decrease/disappearance of other species ( Table 1). Broad-spectrum antibioticsterm lead to an imbalance between Firmicutes and Bacteroidetesterm. The bacterial diversityterm decreases and so does the abundance of these bacteria during the treatments. The alteration of microbiome composition depends on the antibiotic class, dose, period of exposure, pharmacological action, and target bacteriaterm \ [47\] ( Table 1). Specific properties of antibiotics such as antimicrobial effects or mode of action are powerful forces for the selection of intestinal microbiota and are partially responsible for the shifts in bacterial composition during antibiotic therapy \ [46\].

Each class of antibiotics has different properties and excretion systems, resulting in different patterns of alteration in the microbiome composition \ [47\].

A cultivation-independent survey of gut microbiotaterm composition of three persons before, during, and after two exposures to the same antibiotic (ciprofloxacin) \ [48\] has been conducted. This study revealed a considerable resilience to antibiotic administration but also suggested that, in some cases, the system retains a memory of past disturbanceterm and that, in all cases, repeated disturbanceterm leads to a persistent regime shiftterm \ [48\]. The impact of antibiotic disturbance on the resilience of microbiota during future antibiotic treatmentsterm can thus also vary considerably across individuals \ [49\].

#### 2.2.1. Enterotypes

Gut microbiotaterm of each individual are specifically characterized by clusters of bacteria named enterotypes \ [13\]. Three enterotypes are characterized by three dominant bacteria clusters ( Table 2): _Bacteroidesterm_ (enterotype Iterm), _Prevotellaterm_ (enterotype IIterm), or _Ruminococcus_ (enterotype IIIterm). Each enterotypeterm harbors different bacteria genera ( Table 1). These three enterotypes are not only enumerations of bacteria; they are also specifically regrouped by functions. Indeed, an enterotypeterm is a functional harmonious association of several bacteria speciesterm rather than a systematic addition of bacteria speciesterm. Every enterotypeterm is not a clear-cut identity such as blood groupsterm; however, enterotypes characterize individuals, remain stable from adulthood, and can be restored if they are modified. Each enterotypeterm with its distinctive clusters of bacteria and respective functional characteristics defines a distinctive way of generating energy from fermentable substratesterm available in the colon. Indeed, bacteria clusters of enterotype Iterm derive energy primarily from carbohydrates using principally glycolysis and pentose phosphate pathwaysterm, whereas bacteria clusters of enterotypes II and III are able to degrade mucin glycoproteinsterm of the gutterm mucosal layer. Enterotypes seem to be principally defined according to dietary habitsterm. Understanding the origins and functions of enterotypes may improve the knowledge of the relationships between gut microbiotaterm and human health.

#### 2.2.2. Body Mass Index (BMI) Classes

Several studies \ [50, 51\] have examined the impact of childhood BMI on gut microbiotaterm composition and demonstrated that children with overweight or normal BMI are provided with a higher microbial diversityterm than underweight children. Gut microbiotaterm diversityterm descends stepwise based on BMI class \ [50, 52\].

Bervoets et al. \ [53\] demonstrated that the microbiota of obese children are provided with an elevated Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratioterm compared to the microbiota of lean children \ [53\]. Furthermore, low relative proportions of _Bifidobacteriumterm vulgatus_ and high concentrations of _Lactobacillus_ spp. are observed in the obese microbiota \ [53\]. Riva et al. \ [54\] confirmed that obesity is associated with elevated levels of Firmicutesterm such as _Ruminococcaceae_ and depleted levels of Bacteroidetesterm such as _Bacteroidaceae_ and _Bacteroidesterm_. SCFAsterm are higher in obese children suggesting elevated substrate utilization. These findings suggest that the dysbiosisterm of gut microbiotaterm may contribute to the pathophysiology of obesity and the increased ratio of Firmicutesterm to Bacteroidetesterm is associated with the increased production of SCFAsterm and energy harvest from colonic fermentationterm \ [54\].

The underweight BMI level also revealed profound gut microbiotaterm variations. Borgo et al. \ [55\] performed a comprehensive data analysis comparing gut microbiotaterm and anthropometric traits of 15 anorexia nervosa (AN)term women and healthy controls. Results showed that AN intestinal microbiota showed a significant increase in _Enterobacteriaceaeterm_ and _Methanobrevibacter smithii_ compared with healthy controls \ [55\]. On the contrary, the genera _Roseburia_, _Ruminococcus_, and _Clostridiumterm_ were depleted in line with the observed reduction in AN of the total SCFAsterm \ [55\].

BMI levelsterm represent a valid predictive value for gut microbiota dysbiosisterm. Studies have demonstrated that gut microbiotaterm variations are correlated with increased or depleted production of SCFAsterm that may respectively contribute to the pathophysiology of obesity or AN. Interventions such as prebiotics and probioticsterm may be possible solutions to manage both pediatric obesity \ [50\] and AN patients.

#### 2.2.3. Ethnicity, Dietary Habitsterm, and Cultural Habits

While the microbiome of a healthy individual is relatively stable, gutterm microbial dynamics can certainly be influenced by lifestyle and dietary cultural choices \ [56\]. A study of European children (fed with the Western dietterm) and Burkina Faso children (assuming a diet rich in millet/sorghum + local vegetables containing very few lipids and animal proteins) \ [57\] revealed that African children’s microbiota have a remarkable abundance of _Prevotellaterm_ and _Xylanibacter_. Furthermore, _Shigella_ and _Escherichia_ are widely under-represented. Another study \ [58\] compared Hadza hunter-gatherers and Italian gut microbiotaterm. At the phylum level, Hadza gut microbiotaterm are largely enriched in Proteobacteriaterm and Spirochaetes, which are extremely rare in the Italian gut microbiotaterm, whereas Actinobacteriaterm, an important subdominant component of the Italian gut microbiotaterm, are almost absent. At the genus level, Hadza gut microbiotaterm are comparatively enriched in _Prevotellaterm_, _Eubacterium_, _Oscillibacter_, _Butyricicoccus_, _Sporobacter_, _Succinivibrio_, and _Treponema_ and correspondingly depleted in _Bifidobacteriumterm_, _Bacteroidesterm_, _Blautia_, _Dorea_, unclassified _Lachnospiraceae_, _Roseburia_, _Faecalibacterium_, _Ruminococcus,_ and unclassified _Erysipelotrichaceae_. These two studies showed that African gut microbiotaterm have clearly an enterotypeterm _Prevotellaterm_ (enterotype IIterm); the African dietterm rich in millet/sorghum + local vegetables containing very few lipids and animal proteins allows a mucine degradation in synergy with _Desulfovibrionaceae_. European gut microbiotaterm (Western dietterm) have principally an enterotypeterm _Bacteroidesterm_ (enterotype Iterm): the European diet rich in lipids and animal proteins allows energy production from carbohydrates and proteins through fermentation \ [13\]. David et al. \ [59\] compared plant-based diet microbiometerm (high fibers and low fats and proteins) with animal-based diet microbiometerm (low fibers and high fats and proteins) and respective changes in microbiome on either diet and demonstrated that a shift toward an animal-based diet increases the abundance of bile-tolerant microorganismsterm ( _Alistipes_, _Bilophila_, and _Bacteroidesterm_) and decreases the levels of Firmicutesterm that metabolize dietary plant polysaccharides ( _Roseburia_, _Eubacterium rectale_, and _Ruminococcus bromii_). Moreover, microbial activity trades off between carbohydrate and protein fermentationterm when the type of diet respectively changes. This change occurred only one day after the diet reached the distal gut microbiotaterm. David et al. \ [59\] showed that diet alters human microbiota rapidly and reproducibly.

#### 2.2.4. Exercise Frequency

Bai et al. \ [50\] suggested associations of exercise frequency with gut microbiotaterm composition of young children and adolescents. Daily exerciseterm increases gut microbial diversityterm ( Table 2) with a Firmicutesterm enrichment microbiota: _Clostridiales, Roseburia, Lachnospiraceae_, and _Erysipelotrichaceae_ by producing more SCFAsterm which may increase the expression of tight junction proteinsterm in colon epithelia to heighten the resistance of the intestinal barrier, reduce mucosal permeabilityterm, and inhibit inflammatory cytokinesterm \ [50, 60\].

Clarke et al. \ [61\] explored exercise and protein consumption for their impact on the gut microbiotaterm of professional athletes from an international rugby union squad. A higher alpha diversityterm of gutterm microorganisms representing more than 20 distinct phyla has been demonstrated in athletes compared with high and low BMI controls \ [61\]. Indeed, this study showed that exercise increases gut microbial diversityterm ( Table 2) and protein consumption positively correlates with microbial diversityterm. These results provide evidence for a beneficial impact of exercise on gut microbiotaterm diversityterm, but also indicate that the relationship exercise–microbiota diversityterm is probably related to accompanying dietary extremes \ [61\].

## 3\. Gut Microbiotaterm Variations in Health and Disease

Gut microbiotaterm composition is highly variable. The variation itself is considered as physiological in the context of healthy gut microbiotaterm, according to age, ethnicity, lifestyle, and dietary habitsterm. However, these physiological gut microbiota variationsterm have huge implications in intestinal and extra-intestinal disordersterm. Indeed, dysbiosisterm is often defined as an alteration of gut microbiota compositionterm and a cause or a consequence of disorders. It is often difficult to ascertain whether the change is beneficial or detrimental. As for the human conditions, changes represent a challenge for a better adaptation in a new context and may potentiate resilience. However, a chronic external stimulationterm may be stressful and disruptive for an unstructured ecosystemterm. We will discuss the correlations between several diseasesterm and alterations of gut microbiotaterm composition.

### 3.1. Intestinal Disorders

#### 3.1.1. Irritable Bowel Syndrome

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)term is one of the most common gastrointestinal (GI) disorders and the bacterial role has been largely investigated \ [62\].

The study \ [63\] of fecal and colonic mucosal biopsy samples from IBS patients and healthy controls demonstrated a significant reduction in the concentration of aerobic bacteria such as the _Lactobacillus_ species in fecal samples from IBS patients compared to healthy controls. Another study \ [64\] demonstrated that the microbial communities of IBS patients are enriched in Proteobacteriaterm and Firmicutesterm but reduced in Actinobacteriaterm and Bacteroidetesterm compared to controls. In particular, 16S rDNA sequences belonging to the family _Lachnospiraceae_ within the phylum Firmicutesterm are in greater abundance in the IBS clone library \ [64\].

Other studies \ [62, 65\] confirmed and revealed some phyla and genera variations in IBS patients compared to healthy controls: an increase in the Firmicutes-to Bacteroidetes-ratio, a decrease in some Firmicutesterm families ( _Lactobacilli_, _Faecalibacterium_) and the Actinobacteriaterm population (Bifidobacteria, _Collinsella_), and an increase in some Firmicutesterm families ( _Veillonella, Streptococci_, _and Ruminococcus_ spp.) and in Proteobacteriaterm ( _Enterobacteriaceaeterm_ spp.). Therefore, these findings demonstrated a loss of microbial richnessterm that may be involved in amino acid synthesis, in the integrity of cellular junctions, and in inflammatory response, suggesting a weakness of the epithelial barrier functionsterm partially explaining IBS symptoms.

#### 3.1.2. Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)

Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD)term gather idiopathic, chronic, and relapsing inflammatory conditions of the gastrointestinal tract including ulcerative colitis (UC)term and Crohn’s disease (CRD)term.

The findings of Frank et al. \ [66\] indicated a diminution of _Lachnospiraceae_ and Bacteroidetesterm microbial populations and an increase in Proteobacteriaterm families in IBD patients compared to control gut microbiotaterm.

Gut microbiotaterm variations appear to be different between UC and CRD. Indeed, a decreased abundance of the butyrate-producing bacteriaterm _Roseburia hominis_ and _Faecalibacterium prausnitziiterm_ has been observed in UC patients relative to controls \ [67\], whereas the opposite has been observed in CRD patients, who are provided with increased _Faecalibacterium prausnitziiterm_ levels in addition to a reduced overall diversityterm \ [68\].

In the case of CRD patients, another study \ [69\] demonstrated a decrease in _Dialister invisus_, an uncharacterized species of _Clostridiumterm_ spp., in _Faecalibacterium prausnitziiterm_, and in _Bifidobacteriumterm adolescentis_ and an increase in _Ruminococcus gnavus_.

Sokol et al. \ [70\] confirmed the reduction of _F. prausnitzii_ in CRD patients and also demonstrated that an oral administrationterm of live _Faecalibacterium prausnitziiterm_ reduced the severity of colitisterm and tended to correct the dysbiosisterm. These results suggest that counterbalancing dysbiosisterm using _Faecalibacterium prausnitziiterm_ as a probiotic is a promising strategy in CRD treatment \ [70\].

These results do not demonstrate a causal relationship between microbial dysbiosisterm and IBD pathophysiology, but they rather suggest that gutterm microbial unbalances are likely to contribute to disease severity.

#### 3.1.3. Celiac Diseaseterm

Celiac disease (CD)term is a chronic intestinal inflammatory disorder due to an aberrant immune response to dietary gluten proteins in genetically predisposed individuals.

Gut microbiotaterm variations may play a significant role in the pathogenesis of CD; indeed, dysbiosisterm is linked with an inflammatory milieu in celiac patients \ [71\]. Celiac diseaseterm is caused by the interplay between gluten, genetic factors, and environmental factorsterm such as gut microbiotaterm \ [72\].

Studies \ [73, 74, 75, 76\] have demonstrated that _Bifidobacteriumterm_ spp., _Bifidobacteriumterm longum_, _Clostridiumterm histolyticum_, _C. lituseburense,_ and _Faecalibacterium prausnitziiterm_ group proportions are less abundant in untreated CD patients than in healthy controls. Indeed, patients with CD show a reduction in beneficial species ( _Lactobacillus_ and _Bifidobacteriumterm_) and an increase in those potentially pathogenic ( _Bacteroidesterm_ and _E. coli_) as compared to healthy subjects \ [74\]. The mucosal layer of CD patients fails to stabilize the gut microbiotaterm and fails to prevent the host from the invasion of harmful antigens and pathogens \ [71\]. This dysbiosisterm is reduced, but may still remain, after a gluten-free dietterm \ [71, 73\].

#### 3.1.4. Colorectal Cancerterm (CRC)

Colorectal cancerterm is the third most common cause of cancer mortality in the world \ [77\]. Structural fecal bacterial segregations between CRC patients and healthy volunteers have been demonstrated \ [78\]. The gut microbiotaterm of the CRC patients were enriched in _Bacteroidesterm fragilis_, _Enterococcus_, _Escherichia/Shigella_, _Klebsiella_, _Streptococcus_, and _Peptostreptococcus_ and were impoverished in _Roseburia_ and other butyrate-producing bacteriaterm of the family _Lachnospiraceae_ \ [78\]. The gut microbiotaterm of the healthy volunteers were enriched in _Bacteroidesterm vulgatus_ and _Bacteroidesterm uniformis_. Therefore, gut microbiotaterm variations, such as a reduction of butyrate producers and an increase in opportunistic pathogensterm, constitute a major structural imbalance of gut microbiotaterm in CRC patients.

Shen et al. \ [79\] demonstrated a higher abundance of Proteobacteriaterm and a lower abundance of Bacteroidetesterm in CRC cases compared to controls. At the genus level, CRC cases showed an increased abundance of _Dorea_ spp. and _Faecalibacterium_ spp. and lower proportions of _Bacteroidesterm_ spp. and _Coprococcus_ spp. than controls.

Genomic analysis identified an association of _Fusobacteriumterm_ spp. with colorectal cancerterm \ [80\]; the microbiota of CRC patients were enriched in _Fusobacteriumterm_ spp., whereas the Bacteroidetesterm and Firmicutesterm phyla were depleted. Furthermore, _Fusobacteriumterm_ spp. may contribute to tumorigenesis by an inflammatory-mediated mechanismterm \ [80\], but the precise role of Fusobacteriaterm in colorectal carcinoma pathogenesis requires further investigation.

All these findings reveal alterations in CRC microbiota that may contribute to the etiology of colorectal cancerterm. The extension of these findings may lead to strategies able to manipulate microbiota to prevent colorectal cancerterm as well as to identify individuals at high risk \ [79\].

### 3.2. Extra-Intestinal Disorders

We highlighted that the pivotal relationship between gut microbiotaterm and the host is involved in many intestinal disorders. Recent advances in research have demonstrated that this mutualistic gut microbiotaterm–host relationship appears to play a critical role for many extra-intestinal diseasesterm such as metabolic diseasesterm and neurological disorders with “gut–brain axisterm” interactions \ [81\], but also for complex multifactorial diseasesterm such as age-related macular degeneration with “gut–retina axisterm” interactions \ [82\].

#### 3.2.1. Metabolic Disorders

##### Obesity

Animal studies constitute the majority of causative evidence linking changes in microbial composition to obesity while, in humans, data are more variable. In addition, although the majority of mouse gutterm species are unique, the mouse and human microbiota are similar at the division level, with Firmicutesterm and Bacteroidetesterm dominating \ [83\].

The analysis \ [83\] of gut microbiotaterm of genetically obese ob/ob mice and lean mice all fed the same polysaccharide-rich diet revealed a lower relative abundance (50%) of Bacteroidetesterm in obese ob/ob mice, whereas the Firmicutesterm are correspondingly higher. Indeed, higher numbers of _Ruminococcaceae_ and _Rikenellaceae_ have been observed in leptin-resistant obese (leptin-promoting satiety) and diabetic mice (db/db) compared with their lean littermates \ [84\]. In addition, the higher abundance of _Ruminococcaceae_ and _Rikenellaceae_ in mice fed a high-fat diet is not only dependent on the ingested diet but is also closely linked with obesity and type 2 diabetesterm \ [85\]. Furthermore, the association between _Desulfovibrionaceae_ abundance and obesity and type 2 diabetesterm has been demonstrated \ [86, 87\]. Other studies \ [50, 88\] confirmed the role of Proteobacteriaterm in obesity by producing pro-inflammatory molecules such as lipopolysaccharidesterm and helping to harvest energy and increase host fat storageterm \ [88\].

On the contrary, researchers \ [86, 89\] have recently demonstrated that _Akkermansia muciniphilaterm_, a mucin-degrading bacteria living in the mucus layer \ [90\], decreases in both genetically and diet-induced obese mice.

All these studies highlight the finding that obesity is associated with changes in the composition of gut microbiotaterm including lower species diversityterm and shifts in the abundance of genes involved in metabolism. These gut microbiotaterm variations affect the microbiome which has an increased capacity to harvest energy from the dietterm and these changes are transmissible \ [91\]. Gut microbiotaterm can be considered as a contributing factor to the pathophysiology of obesity and may have potential therapeutic implications.

##### Type 2 Diabetesterm (T2D)

Several studies \ [92, 93\] demonstrated that gut microbiotaterm composition is altered in patients suffering from T2D, but it is not clear whether these changes are a cause or simply a consequence of the disorder.

Larsen et al. \ [92\] showed that the proportions of phyla Firmicutesterm and _Clostridia_ are significantly reduced in the T2D sufferers compared to the healthy group. Furthermore, the ratios of Bacteroidetesterm to Firmicutesterm, the ratios of _Bacteroides-Prevotella_, and Betaproteobacteria are highly increased in T2D sufferers compared to healthy patients and positively correlated with plasma glucose.

A metagenome-wide association study of gut microbiotaterm of T2D Chinese sufferers \ [94\] demonstrated that patients with type 2 diabetesterm were characterized by a decrease in butyrate-producing bacteriaterm such as _Roseburia_ spp. and an increase in various opportunistic pathogensterm ( _Clostridiumterm_ spp., _Bacteroidesterm caccae_) as well as an enrichment of other microbial functions conferring sulfate reduction ( _Desulfovibrionaceae_ spp.).

Gut microbiotaterm interact with various host sensing and signaling pathwaysterm, leading to a modulation of the endocrine systemterm, immune responsesterm, nervous system activityterm, and hence, the predisposition to metabolic diseasesterm. Gut microbiota dysbiosisterm drives and implies new therapeutic strategies for diabetes and related metabolic diseasesterm \ [95\].

#### 3.2.2. Central Nervous Systemterm (CNS)-Related Disorders

The brainterm and the gutterm are connected via the gut–brain axisterm with bidirectional interactionsterm between the central nervous systemterm, the enteric nervous systemterm, and the gastrointestinal tract. These bidirectional interactionsterm enable the brainterm to influence gastrointestinal functions as well as immune functions.

##### Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s Diseasesterm

Today, over 46 million people live with dementia worldwide \ [96\]. Alzheimer’s disease (AD)term and Parkinson’s disease (PD)term are considered the most common forms of dementia in the elderly.

Alzheimer’s disease (AD)term is a chronic, rapidly progressive neurodegenerative disorder associated with impaired cognition and cerebral accumulation of amyloid-beta peptidesterm. Cattaneo et al. \ [97\] demonstrated that an increase in _Escherichia/Shigella_ abundance and a reduction of _E. rectale_ abundance are possibly associated with a peripheral inflammatory state in patients with cognitive impairmentterm and brainterm amyloidosis. Vogt et al. \ [98\] compared gut microbiotaterm composition from patients with and without a diagnosis of AD and revealed a lower microbial diversityterm compositionally distinct from control-age and sex-matched individuals, with a decrease in Firmicutesterm and _Bifidobacteriumterm_ abundance and an increase in Bacteroidetesterm abundance in the microbiome of AD participants.

The gut microbiotaterm variations are also involved in the pathogenesis of PD. Hopfner et al. \ [99\] suggested, in agreement with most other studies \ [100, 101, 102\], an increase in _Lactobacillaceae_ abundance in Parkinson patients compared to healthy controls. Moreover, none of the differences in alpha diversityterm has been observed between PD cases and controls, whereas beta diversityterm has been revealed for three bacterial families ( _Lactobacillaceae_, _Barnesiellaceae_, and _Enterococcaceae_) \ [99\]. PD is associated with changes in gut microbiotaterm and this gut microbiota dysbiosisterm may be the mechanism of neuroinflammationterm that leads to PD pathologyterm \ [99\].

##### Hepatic Encephalopathy

Hepatic encephalopathy (HE)term is a complication of cirrhosis and is defined as a spectrum of neuropsychiatric abnormalities in patients with liver dysfunctions. Bajaj et al. \ [103\] linked the gut microbiotaterm composition with cognition and inflammation in HE. Fecal microbiota of cirrhotics were significantly higher in _Enterobacteriaceaeterm_, _Alcaligenaceae_, and _Fusobacteriaceae_ and lower in _Ruminococcaceae_ and _Lachnospiraceae_ compared with controls \ [103\]; moreover, altered higher levels of _Veillonellaceae_ were found in HE patients compared with cirrhotics without HE. In the cirrhosis group, _Alcaligenaceae_ and _Porphyromonadaceae_ were positively correlated with cognitive impairmentterm. _Fusobacteriaceae_, _Veillonellaceae_, and _Enterobacteriaceaeterm_ were positively and _Ruminococcaceae_ was negatively related to inflammation. Thus, cirrhosis, especially when complicated with HE, is associated with significant gut microbiotaterm alterations compared with healthy individuals. Specific bacterial families ( _Alcaligenaceae_, _Porphyromonadaceae_, and _Enterobacteriaceaeterm_) are strongly associated with cognition and inflammation in HE \ [103\].

##### Autism Spectrum Disordersterm

Compared with the gut microbiotaterm of children without ASD, the gut microbiotaterm of children with ASD is less diverse and exhibits lower levels of _Bifidobacteriumterm_ and Firmicutesterm and higher levels of _Lactobacillus_, _Clostridiumterm_, _Bacteroidetesterm_, _Desulfovibrio_, _Caloramator_, and _Sarcina_ \ [104, 105, 106, 107\]. A study by Wang et al. \ [108\] showed a decrease in _Bifidobacteriumterm_ spp. and mucolytic bacteria _Akkermansia muciniphilaterm_ in the gut microbiotaterm of autism subjects compared to control subjects.

Williams et al. \ [109\] found an increase in the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratioterm and a significant increase in _Sutterella_ compared to their abundance in controls. These findings demonstrated that gut microbiota dysbiosisterm has close relationships with gastrointestinal and behavioral manifestations of autismterm.

##### Stress

The bidirectional interactionsterm of gutterm–brainterm mean that the brainterm can also alter gut microbiotaterm composition \ [110\]. A model of social disruption among adult mice \ [111\] demonstrated that exposure to stressterm results in substantial changes in gut microbiota compositionterm: a decrease in _Bacteroidesterm_ spp. abundance and an increase in _Clostridiumterm_ spp. abundance have been observed in stress-induced mice relative to controls. The mechanisms by which stress influences gut microbiotaterm composition are unclear but include microbial habitat alterations following stress-induced changes in intestinal mobilityterm and mucin secretionterm \ [110, 112\].

Other studies \ [62\] revealed that chronic psychological stress is reflected in the microbiota composition. Indeed, depression due to chronic stressful life events is associated with an increase in _Enterobacteriaceaeterm_, whereas psychological stress is associated with a reduction in _Lactobacilli_ spp. and an increase in _Escherichia coli_ and _Pseudomonas_ spp.

Gut microbiotaterm variations are closely associated with the onset of these neurological disorders, and the gut bacterial communitiesterm may be a target for therapeutic interventionsterm.

## 4\. Conclusions

Each healthy human is provided with unique gut microbiotaterm. Core native microbiota are shaped in early life (4–36 months) by gutterm maturation developing from enterotypeterm, birth gestational ageterm, type of deliveryterm·var, methods of milk feeding, weaning period, lifestyle, and dietary and cultural habits. After a child reaches 2–3 years old, a relative stability in gut microbiotaterm composition has been demonstrated. Richness and diversityterm of gut microbiotaterm shaped in early life characterize a healthy gut microbiotaterm composition. However, this optimal healthy gut microbiotaterm composition is different for each individual.

Throughout life, the richer and more diverse the microbiota, the better they will withstand external threats. Indeed, gut microbiotaterm represent a changing ecosystem that is severely tested by many factors such as unbalanced diet, stress, antibiotic use, or diseasesterm. A healthy host–microorganism balanceterm must be respected in order to optimally perform metabolic and immune functionsterm and prevent disease development. Indeed, disturbances to the delicate host–microbe relationshipterm may disrupt the development of the immune system, which may in turn result in diseasesterm \ [113\]. Although the role of gut microbiotaterm is still poorly understood, the close association between gut microbiota dysbiosisterm and intestinal and extra-intestinal disordersterm has been demonstrated. This is the reason why dysbiosisterm can be considered as a biomarkerterm of such disorders and that the study of gut microbiotaterm balances shall be one of the priorities for future therapiesterm to prevent and treat diseasesterm.
