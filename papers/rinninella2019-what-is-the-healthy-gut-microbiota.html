senseMicrobiome versus microbiota - the review uses microbiome for the gene and metabolite capacity and microbiota for the organisms and their composition — “The human gastrointestinal (GI) tract contains an abundant and diverse microbial community that gathers more than 100 trillion microorganisms [1]. The density of bacterial cells in the colon has been estimated at 1011 to 1012 per milliliter which makes the colon one of the most densely populated microbial habitats known on earth [2]. The gut microbiome encodes over 3 million genes producing thousands of metabolites, whereas the human genome consists of approximately 23,000 genes [3]. For years, scientists have been interested in gut microbiota, but one of the major difficulties in the relevant research has been the ability to culture these microorganisms [4]. During the last years, new technologies have allowed researchers to phylogenetically identify and/or quantify the components of the gut microbiota by analyzing nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) directly extracted from stools. The majority of these techniques are based on the extraction of DNA and the amplification of the 16S ribosomal RNA gene (rRNA) [5, 6]. 16S rRNA sequencing has become the most useful technique to highlight diversity and abundance of the microbiome. The 16S rRNA gene sequences can be exploited with polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and metagenomics sequencing to characterize the microbial strains”senses[3]
senseSuperorganism - the host and resident microorganisms form a symbiotic functional unit while individual species composition remains personal — “Considering the characteristics of gut microbiota such as the large diversity, the stability and resilience, and the symbiotic interaction with the host, we can define the host and the microorganisms inhabiting it as a “superorganism” [8, 9] which performs immune and metabolic functions [1]. Gut bacteria are key regulators of digestion along the gastrointestinal tract; commensal bacteria play an important role in the extraction, synthesis, and absorption of many nutrients and metabolites, including bile acids, lipids, amino acids, vitamins, and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). Gut microbiota have a crucial immune function against pathogenic bacteria colonization inhibiting their growth, consuming available nutrients and/or producing bacteriocins. Gut microbiota also prevent bacteria invasion by maintaining the intestinal epithelium integrity [10]. Microorganisms prevent pathogenic colonization by many competition processes: nutrient metabolism, pH modification, antimicrobial peptide secretions, and effects on cell signaling pathways. Moreover, recent studies have identified a critical role for commensal bacteria and their products in regulating the development, homeostasis, and function of innate and adaptive immune cells [11]. It is paradoxical to note that the gut microbiota functions are highly preserved between individuals, whereas each individual’s gut microbiota are characterized by a specific combination of bacterial species due to inter-individual and intra-individual variations throughout human life”senses[4]
senseAnatomical gradient - microbial quantity increases toward the large intestine as aerobes give way to strict anaerobes — “Gut microbiota vary according to the intestine anatomical regions, which vary in terms of physiology, pH and O2 tension, digesta flow rates (rapid in the mouth to the caecum, slower afterward), substrate availability, and host secretions [14]. The small intestine provides a more challenging environment for microbial colonizers given the fairly short transit times (3–5 h) and the high bile concentrations. The large intestine, which is characterized by slow flow rates and neutral to mildly acidic pH, harbors by far the largest microbial community (dominated by obligate anaerobic bacteria) [14]. We can observe a microbiota quantitative increasing gradient and a microbiota qualitative decreasing gradient with a progressive aerobic bacteria decrease for the benefit of strictly anaerobic bacteria”senses[5]
senseEarly-life provenance - gestational age, delivery route, milk exposure, and weaning shape a native core before adult stability — “Each individual is provided with a unique gut microbiota profile that plays many specific functions in host nutrient metabolism, maintenance of structural integrity of the gut mucosal barrier, immunomodulation, and protection against pathogens. Gut microbiota are composed of different bacteria species taxonomically classified by genus, family, order, and phyla. Each human’s gut microbiota are shaped in early life as their composition depends on infant transitions (birth gestational date, type of delivery, methods of milk feeding, weaning period) and external factors such as antibiotic use. These personal and healthy core native microbiota remain relatively stable in adulthood but differ between individuals due to enterotypes, body mass index (BMI) level, exercise frequency, lifestyle, and cultural and dietary habits. Accordingly, there is not a unique optimal gut microbiota composition since it is different for each individual. However, a healthy host–microorganism balance must be respected in order to optimally perform metabolic and immune functions and prevent disease development. This review will provide an overview of the studies that focus on gut microbiota balances in the same individual and between individuals and highlight the close mutualistic relationship between gut microbiota variations and diseases. Indeed, dysbiosis of gut microbiota is associated not only with intestinal disorders but also with numerous extra-intestinal diseases such as metabolic and neurological disorders. Understanding the cause or consequence of these gut microbiota balances in health and disease and how to maintain or restore a healthy gut microbiota composition should be useful in developing promising therapeutic interventions”senses[6]

senseEnterotype function - clusters are organized by shared metabolic functions and substrate use, not merely enumerated bacterial membership — “Gut microbiota of each individual are specifically characterized by clusters of bacteria named enterotypes [13]. Three enterotypes are characterized by three dominant bacteria clusters ( Table 2): Bacteroides (enterotype I), Prevotella (enterotype II), or Ruminococcus (enterotype III). Each enterotype harbors different bacteria genera ( Table 1). These three enterotypes are not only enumerations of bacteria; they are also specifically regrouped by functions. Indeed, an enterotype is a functional harmonious association of several bacteria species rather than a systematic addition of bacteria species. Every enterotype is not a clear-cut identity such as blood groups; however, enterotypes characterize individuals, remain stable from adulthood, and can be restored if they are modified. Each enterotype with its distinctive clusters of bacteria and respective functional characteristics defines a distinctive way of generating energy from fermentable substrates available in the colon. Indeed, bacteria clusters of enterotype I derive energy primarily from carbohydrates using principally glycolysis and pentose phosphate pathways, whereas bacteria clusters of enterotypes II and III are able to degrade mucin glycoproteins of the gut mucosal layer. Enterotypes seem to be principally defined according to dietary habits. Understanding the origins and functions of enterotypes may improve the knowledge of the relationships between gut microbiota and human health”senses[8]
senseDiet-response timescale - changing to an animal-based diet alters taxa and fermentation activity within about one day — “While the microbiome of a healthy individual is relatively stable, gut microbial dynamics can certainly be influenced by lifestyle and dietary cultural choices [56]. A study of European children (fed with the Western diet) and Burkina Faso children (assuming a diet rich in millet/sorghum + local vegetables containing very few lipids and animal proteins) [57] revealed that African children’s microbiota have a remarkable abundance of Prevotella and Xylanibacter. Furthermore, Shigella and Escherichia are widely under-represented. Another study [58] compared Hadza hunter-gatherers and Italian gut microbiota. At the phylum level, Hadza gut microbiota are largely enriched in Proteobacteria and Spirochaetes, which are extremely rare in the Italian gut microbiota, whereas Actinobacteria, an important subdominant component of the Italian gut microbiota, are almost absent. At the genus level, Hadza gut microbiota are comparatively enriched in Prevotella, Eubacterium, Oscillibacter, Butyricicoccus, Sporobacter, Succinivibrio, and Treponema and correspondingly depleted in Bifidobacterium, Bacteroides, Blautia, Dorea, unclassified Lachnospiraceae, Roseburia, Faecalibacterium, Ruminococcus, and unclassified Erysipelotrichaceae. These two studies showed that African gut microbiota have clearly an enterotype Prevotella (enterotype II); the African diet rich in millet/sorghum + local vegetables containing very few lipids and animal proteins allows a mucine degradation in synergy with Desulfovibrionaceae. European gut microbiota (Western diet) have principally an enterotype Bacteroides (enterotype I): the European diet rich in lipids and animal proteins allows energy production from carbohydrates and proteins through fermentation [13]. David et al. [59] compared plant-based diet microbiome (high fibers and low fats and proteins) with animal-based diet microbiome (low fibers and high fats and proteins) and respective changes in microbiome on either diet and demonstrated that a shift toward an animal-based diet increases the abundance of bile-tolerant microorganisms ( Alistipes, Bilophila, and Bacteroides) and decreases the levels of Firmicutes that metabolize dietary plant polysaccharides ( Roseburia, Eubacterium rectale, and Ruminococcus bromii). Moreover, microbial activity trades off between carbohydrate and protein fermentation when the type of diet respectively changes. This change occurred only one day after the diet reached the distal gut microbiota. David et al. [59] showed that diet alters human microbiota rapidly and reproducibly”senses[9]
senseDysbiosis causal boundary - compositional alteration may be either cause or consequence, and the review repeatedly distinguishes association from demonstrated causation — “Gut microbiota composition is highly variable. The variation itself is considered as physiological in the context of healthy gut microbiota, according to age, ethnicity, lifestyle, and dietary habits. However, these physiological gut microbiota variations have huge implications in intestinal and extra-intestinal disorders. Indeed, dysbiosis is often defined as an alteration of gut microbiota composition and a cause or a consequence of disorders. It is often difficult to ascertain whether the change is beneficial or detrimental. As for the human conditions, changes represent a challenge for a better adaptation in a new context and may potentiate resilience. However, a chronic external stimulation may be stressful and disruptive for an unstructured ecosystem. We will discuss the correlations between several diseases and alterations of gut microbiota composition”senses[10]
senseIBD causal boundary - microbial imbalance is likely to contribute to severity, but the cited results do not demonstrate that it causes IBD pathophysiology — “Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) gather idiopathic, chronic, and relapsing inflammatory conditions of the gastrointestinal tract including ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CRD). The findings of Frank et al. [66] indicated a diminution of Lachnospiraceae and Bacteroidetes microbial populations and an increase in Proteobacteria families in IBD patients compared to control gut microbiota. Gut microbiota variations appear to be different between UC and CRD. Indeed, a decreased abundance of the butyrate-producing bacteria Roseburia hominis and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii has been observed in UC patients relative to controls [67], whereas the opposite has been observed in CRD patients, who are provided with increased Faecalibacterium prausnitzii levels in addition to a reduced overall diversity [68]. In the case of CRD patients, another study [69] demonstrated a decrease in Dialister invisus, an uncharacterized species of Clostridium spp., in Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, and in Bifidobacterium adolescentis and an increase in Ruminococcus gnavus. Sokol et al. [70] confirmed the reduction of F. prausnitzii in CRD patients and also demonstrated that an oral administration of live Faecalibacterium prausnitzii reduced the severity of colitis and tended to correct the dysbiosis. These results suggest that counterbalancing dysbiosis using Faecalibacterium prausnitzii as a probiotic is a promising strategy in CRD treatment [70]. These results do not demonstrate a causal relationship between microbial dysbiosis and IBD pathophysiology, but they rather suggest that gut microbial unbalances are likely to contribute to disease severity”senses[11]
senseObesity evidence boundary - animal studies provide most causal evidence while human microbial-composition data are more variable — “Animal studies constitute the majority of causative evidence linking changes in microbial composition to obesity while, in humans, data are more variable. In addition, although the majority of mouse gut species are unique, the mouse and human microbiota are similar at the division level, with Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes dominating [83]. The analysis [83] of gut microbiota of genetically obese ob/ob mice and lean mice all fed the same polysaccharide-rich diet revealed a lower relative abundance (50%) of Bacteroidetes in obese ob/ob mice, whereas the Firmicutes are correspondingly higher. Indeed, higher numbers of Ruminococcaceae and Rikenellaceae have been observed in leptin-resistant obese (leptin-promoting satiety) and diabetic mice (db/db) compared with their lean littermates [84]. In addition, the higher abundance of Ruminococcaceae and Rikenellaceae in mice fed a high-fat diet is not only dependent on the ingested diet but is also closely linked with obesity and type 2 diabetes [85]. Furthermore, the association between Desulfovibrionaceae abundance and obesity and type 2 diabetes has been demonstrated [86, 87]. Other studies [50, 88] confirmed the role of Proteobacteria in obesity by producing pro-inflammatory molecules such as lipopolysaccharides and helping to harvest energy and increase host fat storage [88]. On the contrary, researchers [86, 89] have recently demonstrated that Akkermansia muciniphila, a mucin-degrading bacteria living in the mucus layer [90], decreases in both genetically and diet-induced obese mice. All these studies highlight the finding that obesity is associated with changes in the composition of gut microbiota including lower species diversity and shifts in the abundance of genes involved in metabolism. These gut microbiota variations affect the microbiome which has an increased capacity to harvest energy from the diet and these changes are transmissible [91]. Gut microbiota can be considered as a contributing factor to the pathophysiology of obesity and may have potential therapeutic implications”senses[12]
senseGut-brain directionality - the brain influences gastrointestinal and immune functions, while gut dysbiosis is also linked to neuroinflammatory pathology — “The brain and the gut are connected via the gut–brain axis with bidirectional interactions between the central nervous system, the enteric nervous system, and the gastrointestinal tract. senseHealthy composition as resilience - no universal species profile is asserted; richness, diversity, balance, and resistance to disturbance are the operative properties — “Each healthy human is provided with unique gut microbiota. After a child reaches 2–3 years old, a relative stability in gut microbiota composition has been demonstrated. Richness and diversity of gut microbiota shaped in early life characterize a healthy gut microbiota composition. However, this optimal healthy gut microbiota composition is different for each individual. Throughout life, the richer and more diverse the microbiota, the better they will withstand external threats. Indeed, gut microbiota represent a changing ecosystem that is severely tested by many factors such as unbalanced diet, stress, antibiotic use, or diseases. A healthy host–microorganism balance must be respected in order to optimally perform metabolic and immune functions and prevent disease development. Indeed, disturbances to the delicate host–microbe relationship may disrupt the development of the immune system, which may in turn result in diseases [113]. conceptunique gut microbiota profile — not defined in this paper's own words; individual microbial configuration with host-specific functions — “Each individual is provided with a unique gut microbiota profile that plays many specific functions in host nutrient metabolism, maintenance of structural integrity of the gut mucosal barrier, immunomodulation, and protection against pathogens. Gut microbiota are composed of different bacteria species taxonomically classified by genus, family, order, and phyla. Each human’s gut microbiota are shaped in early life as their composition depends on infant transitions (birth gestational date, type of delivery, methods of milk feeding, weaning period) and external factors such as antibiotic use. These personal and healthy core native microbiota remain relatively stable in adulthood but differ between individuals due to enterotypes, body mass index (BMI) level, exercise frequency, lifestyle, and cultural and dietary habits. Accordingly, there is not a unique optimal gut microbiota composition since it is different for each individual. However, a healthy host–microorganism balance must be respected in order to optimally perform metabolic and immune functions and prevent disease development. This review will provide an overview of the studies that focus on gut microbiota balances in the same individual and between individuals and highlight the close mutualistic relationship between gut microbiota variations and diseases. Indeed, dysbiosis of gut microbiota is associated not only with intestinal disorders but also with numerous extra-intestinal diseases such as metabolic and neurological disorders. Understanding the cause or consequence of these gut microbiota balances in health and disease and how to maintain or restore a healthy gut microbiota composition should be useful in developing promising therapeutic interventions”concepts[7]

The gut microbiome encodes over 3 million genes producing thousands of metabolites, whereas the human genome consists of approximately 23,000 genes [3]. ##### Weaning Period The introduction of solid foodsterm and the termination of milk-feeding/weaning coincide with major gut microbiotaterm changes. The abundance of genera Bifidobacteriumterm, Clostridiumterm coccoides, and Bacteroidesterm are predominant after weaning [40]. The microbiota of breastfed children and the microbiota of children previously fed with formula milk become closer to each other ( Table 1).

Dietary habitsterm, infant weaning, and feeding practices become determinants and play a crucial role in gut microbiotaterm variations. The introduction of high-fiber and carbohydrate foodsterm (traditional foods) causes an increase in Firmicutesterm and Prevotellaterm, whereas the introduction of high-fiber and animal protein foodsterm causes an increase in Bacteroidetesterm [41]. Furthermore, Fallani et al. [40] demonstrated that northern European countries are associated with a higher proportion of Bifidobacteria in the infant gut microbiotaterm, whereas higher levels of Bacteroidesterm and Lactobacilli characterize southern European countries.

2.1.3. Age At one year old, a child’s microbiota composition has a characteristic abundance of Akkermansia muciniphilaterm, Bacteroidesterm, Veillonella, Clostridiumterm coccoides spp., and Clostridiumterm botulinum spp. [42].

Microbiota diversityterm increases with age until it becomes a stable adult microbiota compositionterm dominated by three bacterial phyla ( Table 1): Firmicutesterm ( Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae), Bacteroidetesterm ( Bacteroidaceae, Prevotellaceae, and Rikenellaceae), and Actinobacteriaterm ( Bifidobacteriaceae and Coriobacteriaceae) that are the result of maturation due to the influence of genetics, environment, diet, lifestyle, and gut physiologyterm [42]. At approximately three years of age, a child’s gut microbiotaterm composition and diversityterm are most like those of adults [43].

With regard to older people over the age of 70term, gut microbiotaterm composition can be affected by digestion and nutrient absorption changes and immune activity weakness. Dietary habitterm changes (more monotonous) may also weaken gut microbiotaterm diversityterm. A decrease in anaerobic bacteria such as Bifidobacteriumterm spp. and an increase in Clostridiumterm and Proteobacteriaterm have been observed [44]. Given the role of Bifidobacteriumterm spp. in the stimulation of the immune system and metabolic processes, a Bifidobacteria decrease may partially explain low systemic inflammatory status and malnutrition in older adults [45].

2.1.4. Antibiotics Gut microbiotaterm composition can be more or less affected by antibiotic use. A follow-up study explored the effect of antibiotics with different modes of action on human gut microbiotaterm composition [46] and demonstrated that antibiotic treatmentsterm modify the gut microbiotaterm composition with an abundance/appearance of certain species and a decrease/disappearance of other species ( Table 1). Broad-spectrum antibioticsterm lead to an imbalance between Firmicutes and Bacteroidetesterm. The bacterial diversityterm decreases and so does the abundance of these bacteria during the treatments. The alteration of microbiome composition depends on the antibiotic class, dose, period of exposure, pharmacological action, and target bacteriaterm [47] ( Table 1). Specific properties of antibiotics such as antimicrobial effects or mode of action are powerful forces for the selection of intestinal microbiota and are partially responsible for the shifts in bacterial composition during antibiotic therapy [46].

Each class of antibiotics has different properties and excretion systems, resulting in different patterns of alteration in the microbiome composition [47].

A cultivation-independent survey of gut microbiotaterm composition of three persons before, during, and after two exposures to the same antibiotic (ciprofloxacin) [48] has been conducted. This study revealed a considerable resilience to antibiotic administration but also suggested that, in some cases, the system retains a memory of past disturbanceterm and that, in all cases, repeated disturbanceterm leads to a persistent regime shiftterm [48]. The impact of antibiotic disturbance on the resilience of microbiota during future antibiotic treatmentsterm can thus also vary considerably across individuals [49].

2.2.1. Enterotypesterm Gut microbiotaterm of each individual are specifically characterized by clusters of bacteria named enterotypesterm [13]. Three enterotypesterm are characterized by three dominant bacteria clusters ( Table 2): Bacteroidesterm (enterotype Iterm), Prevotellaterm (enterotype IIterm), or Ruminococcus (enterotype IIIterm). Each enterotypeterm harbors different bacteria genera ( Table 1). These three enterotypesterm are not only enumerations of bacteria; they are also specifically regrouped by functions. Indeed, an enterotype isterm a functional harmonious association of several bacteria speciesterm rather than a systematic addition of bacteria speciesterm. Every enterotype isterm not a clear-cut identity such as blood groupsterm; however, enterotypesterm characterize individuals, remain stable from adulthood, and can be restored if they are modified. Each enterotypeterm with its distinctive clusters of bacteria and respective functional characteristics defines a distinctive way of generating energy from fermentable substratesterm available in the colon. Indeed, bacteria clusters of enterotype Iterm derive energy primarily from carbohydrates using principally glycolysis and pentose phosphate pathwaysterm, whereas bacteria clusters of enterotypesterm II and III are able to degrade mucin glycoproteinsterm of the gutterm mucosal layer. Enterotypesterm seem to be principally defined according to dietary habitsterm. Understanding the origins and functions of enterotypesterm may improve the knowledge of the relationships between gut microbiotaterm and human health.

2.2.2. Body Mass Index (BMI) Classes Several studies [50, 51] have examined the impact of childhood BMI on gut microbiotaterm composition and demonstrated that children with overweight or normal BMI are provided with a higher microbial diversityterm than underweight children. Gut microbiotaterm diversityterm descends stepwise based on BMI class [50, 52].

Bervoets et al. [53] demonstrated that the microbiota of obese children are provided with an elevated Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratioterm compared to the microbiota of lean children [53]. Furthermore, low relative proportions of Bifidobacteriumterm vulgatus and high concentrations of Lactobacillus spp. are observed in the obese microbiota [53]. Riva et al. [54] confirmed that obesity is associated with elevated levels of Firmicutesterm such as Ruminococcaceae and depleted levels of Bacteroidetesterm such as Bacteroidaceae and Bacteroidesterm. SCFAsterm are higher in obese children suggesting elevated substrate utilization. These findings suggest that the dysbiosisterm of gut microbiotaterm may contribute to the pathophysiology of obesity and the increased ratio of Firmicutesterm to Bacteroidetesterm is associated with the increased production of SCFAsterm and energy harvest from colonic fermentationterm [54].

The underweight BMI levelterm also revealed profound gut microbiotaterm variations. Borgo et al. [55] performed a comprehensive data analysis comparing gut microbiotaterm and anthropometric traits of 15 anorexia nervosa (AN)term women and healthy controls. Results showed that AN intestinal microbiota showed a significant increase in Enterobacteriaceaeterm and Methanobrevibacter smithii compared with healthy controls [55]. On the contrary, the genera Roseburia, Ruminococcus, and Clostridiumterm were depleted in line with the observed reduction in AN of the total SCFAsterm [55].

BMI levelsterm represent a valid predictive value for gut microbiota dysbiosisterm. Studies have demonstrated that gut microbiotaterm variations are correlated with increased or depleted production of SCFAsterm that may respectively contribute to the pathophysiology of obesity or AN. Interventions such as prebiotics and probioticsterm may be possible solutions to manage both pediatric obesity [50] and AN patients.

2.2.3. Ethnicity, Dietary Habitsterm, and Cultural Habits While the microbiome of a healthy individual is relatively stable, gutterm microbial dynamics can certainly be influenced by lifestyle and dietary cultural choices [56]. A study of European children (fed with the Western dietterm) and Burkina Faso children (assuming a diet rich in millet/sorghum + local vegetables containing very few lipids and animal proteins) [57] revealed that African children’s microbiota have a remarkable abundance of Prevotellaterm and Xylanibacter. Furthermore, Shigella and Escherichia are widely under-represented. Another study [58] compared Hadza hunter-gatherers and Italian gut microbiotaterm. At the phylum level, Hadza gut microbiotaterm are largely enriched in Proteobacteriaterm and Spirochaetes, which are extremely rare in the Italian gut microbiotaterm, whereas Actinobacteriaterm, an important subdominant component of the Italian gut microbiotaterm, are almost absent. At the genus level, Hadza gut microbiotaterm are comparatively enriched in Prevotellaterm, Eubacterium, Oscillibacter, Butyricicoccus, Sporobacter, Succinivibrio, and Treponema and correspondingly depleted in Bifidobacteriumterm, Bacteroidesterm, Blautia, Dorea, unclassified Lachnospiraceae, Roseburia, Faecalibacterium, Ruminococcus, and unclassified Erysipelotrichaceae. These two studies showed that African gut microbiotaterm have clearly an enterotypeterm Prevotellaterm (enterotype IIterm); the African dietterm rich in millet/sorghum + local vegetables containing very few lipids and animal proteins allows a mucine degradation in synergy with Desulfovibrionaceae. European gut microbiotaterm (Western dietterm) have principally an enterotypeterm Bacteroidesterm (enterotype Iterm): the European diet rich in lipids and animal proteins allows energy production from carbohydrates and proteins through fermentation [13]. David et al. [59] compared plant-based diet microbiometerm (high fibers and low fats and proteins) with animal-based diet microbiometerm (low fibers and high fats and proteins) and respective changes in microbiome on either diet and demonstrated that a shift toward an animal-based diet increases the abundance of bile-tolerant microorganismsterm ( Alistipes, Bilophila, and Bacteroidesterm) and decreases the levels of Firmicutesterm that metabolize dietary plant polysaccharides ( Roseburia, Eubacterium rectale, and Ruminococcus bromii). Moreover, microbial activity trades off between carbohydrate and protein fermentationterm when the type of diet respectively changes. This change occurred only one day after the diet reached the distal gut microbiotaterm. David et al. [59] showed that diet alters human microbiota rapidly and reproducibly.

2.2.4. Exercise Frequency Bai et al. [50] suggested associations of exercise frequency with gut microbiotaterm composition of young children and adolescents. Daily exerciseterm increases gut microbial diversityterm ( Table 2) with a Firmicutesterm enrichment microbiota: Clostridiales, Roseburia, Lachnospiraceae, and Erysipelotrichaceae by producing more SCFAsterm which may increase the expression of tight junction proteinsterm in colon epithelia to heighten the resistance of the intestinal barrier, reduce mucosal permeabilityterm, and inhibit inflammatory cytokinesterm [50, 60].

Clarke et al. [61] explored exercise and protein consumption for their impact on the gut microbiotaterm of professional athletes from an international rugby union squad. A higher alpha diversityterm of gutterm microorganisms representing more than 20 distinct phyla has been demonstrated in athletes compared with high and low BMI controls [61]. Indeed, this study showed that exercise increases gut microbial diversityterm ( Table 2) and protein consumption positively correlates with microbial diversityterm. These results provide evidence for a beneficial impact of exercise on gut microbiotaterm diversityterm, but also indicate that the relationship exercise–microbiota diversityterm is probably related to accompanying dietary extremes [61].

3. Gut Microbiotaterm Variations in Health and Diseaseterm Gut microbiotaterm composition is highly variable. The variation itself is considered as physiological in the context of healthy gut microbiotaterm, according to age, ethnicity, lifestyle, and dietary habitsterm. However, these physiological gut microbiota variationsterm have huge implications in intestinal and extra-intestinal disordersterm. Indeed, dysbiosisterm is often defined as an alteration of gut microbiota compositionterm and a cause or a consequence of disorders. It is often difficult to ascertain whether the change is beneficial or detrimental. As for the human conditions, changes represent a challenge for a better adaptation in a new context and may potentiate resilience. However, a chronic external stimulationterm may be stressful and disruptive for an unstructured ecosystemterm. We will discuss the correlations between several diseasesterm and alterations of gut microbiotaterm composition.

3.1. Intestinal Disorders 3.1.1. Irritable Bowel Syndrome Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)term is one of the most common gastrointestinal (GI) disorders and the bacterial role has been largely investigated [62].

The study [63] of fecal and colonic mucosal biopsy samples from IBS patients and healthy controls demonstrated a significant reduction in the concentration of aerobic bacteria such as the Lactobacillus species in fecal samples from IBS patients compared to healthy controls. Another study [64] demonstrated that the microbial communities of IBS patients are enriched in Proteobacteriaterm and Firmicutesterm but reduced in Actinobacteriaterm and Bacteroidetesterm compared to controls. In particular, 16S rDNA sequences belonging to the family Lachnospiraceae within the phylum Firmicutesterm are in greater abundance in the IBS clone library [64].

Other studies [62, 65] confirmed and revealed some phyla and genera variations in IBS patients compared to healthy controls: an increase in the Firmicutes-to Bacteroidetes-ratio, a decrease in some Firmicutesterm families ( Lactobacilli, Faecalibacterium) and the Actinobacteriaterm population (Bifidobacteria, Collinsella), and an increase in some Firmicutesterm families ( Veillonella, Streptococci, and Ruminococcus spp.) and in Proteobacteriaterm ( Enterobacteriaceaeterm spp.). Therefore, these findings demonstrated a loss of microbial richnessterm that may be involved in amino acid synthesis, in the integrity of cellular junctions, and in inflammatory response, suggesting a weakness of the epithelial barrier functionsterm partially explaining IBS symptoms.

3.1.2. Inflammatory Bowel Diseaseterm (IBD) Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD)term gather idiopathic, chronic, and relapsing inflammatory conditions of the gastrointestinal tract including ulcerative colitis (UC)term and Crohn’s disease (CRD)term.

The findings of Frank et al. [66] indicated a diminution of Lachnospiraceae and Bacteroidetesterm microbial populations and an increase in Proteobacteriaterm families in IBD patients compared to control gut microbiotaterm.

Gut microbiotaterm variations appear to be different between UC and CRD. Indeed, a decreased abundance of the butyrate-producing bacteriaterm Roseburia hominis and Faecalibacterium prausnitziiterm has been observed in UC patients relative to controls [67], whereas the opposite has been observed in CRD patients, who are provided with increased Faecalibacterium prausnitziiterm levels in addition to a reduced overall diversityterm [68].

In the case of CRD patients, another study [69] demonstrated a decrease in Dialister invisus, an uncharacterized species of Clostridiumterm spp., in Faecalibacterium prausnitziiterm, and in Bifidobacteriumterm adolescentis and an increase in Ruminococcus gnavus.

Sokol et al. [70] confirmed the reduction of F. prausnitzii in CRD patients and also demonstrated that an oral administrationterm of live Faecalibacterium prausnitziiterm reduced the severity of colitisterm and tended to correct the dysbiosisterm. These results suggest that counterbalancing dysbiosisterm using Faecalibacterium prausnitziiterm as a probiotic is a promising strategy in CRD treatment [70].

These results do not demonstrate a causal relationship between microbial dysbiosisterm and IBD pathophysiology, but they rather suggest that gutterm microbial unbalances are likely to contribute to diseaseterm severity.

3.1.3. Celiac Diseaseterm Celiac disease (CD)term is a chronic intestinal inflammatory disorder due to an aberrant immune responseterm to dietary gluten proteins in genetically predisposed individuals.

Gut microbiotaterm variations may play a significant role in the pathogenesis of CD; indeed, dysbiosisterm is linked with an inflammatory milieu in celiac patients [71]. Celiac diseaseterm is caused by the interplay between gluten, genetic factors, and environmental factorsterm such as gut microbiotaterm [72].

Studies [73, 74, 75, 76] have demonstrated that Bifidobacteriumterm spp., Bifidobacteriumterm longum, Clostridiumterm histolyticum, C. lituseburense, and Faecalibacterium prausnitziiterm group proportions are less abundant in untreated CD patients than in healthy controls. Indeed, patients with CD show a reduction in beneficial species ( Lactobacillus and Bifidobacteriumterm) and an increase in those potentially pathogenic ( Bacteroidesterm and E. coli) as compared to healthy subjects [74]. The mucosal layer of CD patients fails to stabilize the gut microbiotaterm and fails to prevent the host from the invasion of harmful antigens and pathogens [71]. This dysbiosisterm is reduced, but may still remain, after a gluten-free dietterm [71, 73].

3.1.4. Colorectal Cancerterm (CRC) Colorectal cancerterm is the third most common cause of cancer mortality in the world [77]. Structural fecal bacterial segregations between CRC patients and healthy volunteers have been demonstrated [78]. The gut microbiotaterm of the CRC patients were enriched in Bacteroidesterm fragilis, Enterococcus, Escherichia/Shigella, Klebsiella, Streptococcus, and Peptostreptococcus and were impoverished in Roseburia and other butyrate-producing bacteriaterm of the family Lachnospiraceae [78]. The gut microbiotaterm of the healthy volunteers were enriched in Bacteroidesterm vulgatus and Bacteroidesterm uniformis. Therefore, gut microbiotaterm variations, such as a reduction of butyrate producers and an increase in opportunistic pathogensterm, constitute a major structural imbalance of gut microbiotaterm in CRC patients.

Shen et al. [79] demonstrated a higher abundance of Proteobacteriaterm and a lower abundance of Bacteroidetesterm in CRC cases compared to controls. At the genus level, CRC cases showed an increased abundance of Dorea spp. and Faecalibacterium spp. and lower proportions of Bacteroidesterm spp. and Coprococcus spp. than controls.

Genomic analysis identified an association of Fusobacteriumterm spp. with colorectal cancerterm [80]; the microbiota of CRC patients were enriched in Fusobacteriumterm spp., whereas the Bacteroidetesterm and Firmicutesterm phyla were depleted. Furthermore, Fusobacteriumterm spp. may contribute to tumorigenesis by an inflammatory-mediated mechanismterm [80], but the precise role of Fusobacteriaterm in colorectal carcinoma pathogenesis requires further investigation.

All these findings reveal alterations in CRC microbiota that may contribute to the etiology of colorectal cancerterm. The extension of these findings may lead to strategies able to manipulate microbiota to prevent colorectal cancerterm as well as to identify individuals at high risk [79].

3.2. Extra-Intestinal Disorders We highlighted that the pivotal relationship between gut microbiotaterm and the host is involved in many intestinal disorders. Recent advances in research have demonstrated that this mutualistic gut microbiotaterm–host relationship appears to play a critical role for many extra-intestinal diseasesterm such as metabolic diseasesterm and neurological disorders with “gut–brain axisterm” interactions [81], but also for complex multifactorial diseasesterm such as age-related macular degeneration with “gut–retina axisterm” interactions [82].

3.2.1. Metabolic Disorders ##### Obesity Animal studies constitute the majority of causative evidence linking changes in microbial composition to obesity while, in humans, data are more variable. In addition, although the majority of mouse gutterm species are unique, the mouse and human microbiota are similar at the division level, with Firmicutesterm and Bacteroidetesterm dominating [83].

The analysis [83] of gut microbiotaterm of genetically obese ob/ob mice and lean mice all fed the same polysaccharide-rich diet revealed a lower relative abundance (50%) of Bacteroidetesterm in obese ob/ob mice, whereas the Firmicutesterm are correspondingly higher. Indeed, higher numbers of Ruminococcaceae and Rikenellaceae have been observed in leptin-resistant obese (leptin-promoting satiety) and diabetic mice (db/db) compared with their lean littermates [84]. In addition, the higher abundance of Ruminococcaceae and Rikenellaceae in mice fed a high-fat diet is not only dependent on the ingested diet but is also closely linked with obesity and type 2 diabetesterm [85]. Furthermore, the association between Desulfovibrionaceae abundance and obesity and type 2 diabetesterm has been demonstrated [86, 87]. Other studies [50, 88] confirmed the role of Proteobacteriaterm in obesity by producing pro-inflammatory molecules such as lipopolysaccharidesterm and helping to harvest energy and increase host fat storageterm [88].

On the contrary, researchers [86, 89] have recently demonstrated that Akkermansia muciniphilaterm, a mucin-degrading bacteria living in the mucus layer [90], decreases in both genetically and diet-induced obese mice.

All these studies highlight the finding that obesity is associated with changes in the composition of gut microbiotaterm including lower species diversityterm and shifts in the abundance of genes involved in metabolism. These gut microbiotaterm variations affect the microbiome which has an increased capacity to harvest energy from the dietterm and these changes are transmissible [91]. Gut microbiotaterm can be considered as a contributing factor to the pathophysiology of obesity and may have potential therapeutic implications.

##### Type 2 Diabetesterm (T2D) Several studies [92, 93] demonstrated that gut microbiotaterm composition is altered in patients suffering from T2D, but it is not clear whether these changes are a cause or simply a consequence of the disorder.

Larsen et al. [92] showed that the proportions of phyla Firmicutesterm and Clostridia are significantly reduced in the T2D sufferers compared to the healthy group. Furthermore, the ratios of Bacteroidetesterm to Firmicutesterm, the ratios of Bacteroides-Prevotella, and Betaproteobacteria are highly increased in T2D sufferers compared to healthy patients and positively correlated with plasma glucose.

A metagenome-wide association study of gut microbiotaterm of T2D Chinese sufferers [94] demonstrated that patients with type 2 diabetesterm were characterized by a decrease in butyrate-producing bacteriaterm such as Roseburia spp. and an increase in various opportunistic pathogensterm ( Clostridiumterm spp., Bacteroidesterm caccae) as well as an enrichment of other microbial functions conferring sulfate reduction ( Desulfovibrionaceae spp.).

Gut microbiotaterm interact with various host sensing and signaling pathwaysterm, leading to a modulation of the endocrine systemterm, immune responsesterm, nervous system activityterm, and hence, the predisposition to metabolic diseasesterm. Gut microbiota dysbiosisterm drives and implies new therapeutic strategies for diabetes and related metabolic diseasesterm [95].

3.2.2. Central Nervous Systemterm (CNS)-Related Disorders The brainterm and the gutterm are connected via the gut–brain axisterm with bidirectional interactionsterm between the central nervous systemterm, the enteric nervous systemterm, and the gastrointestinal tract. These bidirectional interactionsterm enable the brainterm to influence gastrointestinal functions as well as immune functions.

##### Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s Diseasesterm Today, over 46 million people live with dementia worldwide [96]. Alzheimer’s disease (AD)term and Parkinson’s disease (PD)term are considered the most common forms of dementia in the elderly.

Alzheimer’s disease (AD)term is a chronic, rapidly progressive neurodegenerative disorder associated with impaired cognition and cerebral accumulation of amyloid-beta peptidesterm. Cattaneo et al. [97] demonstrated that an increase in Escherichia/Shigella abundance and a reduction of E. rectale abundance are possibly associated with a peripheral inflammatory state in patients with cognitive impairmentterm and brainterm amyloidosis. Vogt et al. [98] compared gut microbiotaterm composition from patients with and without a diagnosis of AD and revealed a lower microbial diversityterm compositionally distinct from control-age and sex-matched individuals, with a decrease in Firmicutesterm and Bifidobacteriumterm abundance and an increase in Bacteroidetesterm abundance in the microbiome of AD participants.

The gut microbiotaterm variations are also involved in the pathogenesis of PD. Hopfner et al. [99] suggested, in agreement with most other studies [100, 101, 102], an increase in Lactobacillaceae abundance in Parkinson patients compared to healthy controls. Moreover, none of the differences in alpha diversityterm has been observed between PD cases and controls, whereas beta diversityterm has been revealed for three bacterial families ( Lactobacillaceae, Barnesiellaceae, and Enterococcaceae) [99]. PD is associated with changes in gut microbiotaterm and this gut microbiota dysbiosisterm may be the mechanism of neuroinflammationterm that leads to PD pathologyterm [99].

##### Hepatic Encephalopathy Hepatic encephalopathy (HE)term is a complication of cirrhosis and is defined as a spectrum of neuropsychiatric abnormalities in patients with liver dysfunctions. Bajaj et al. [103] linked the gut microbiotaterm composition with cognition and inflammation in HE. Fecal microbiota of cirrhotics were significantly higher in Enterobacteriaceaeterm, Alcaligenaceae, and Fusobacteriaceae and lower in Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae compared with controls [103]; moreover, altered higher levels of Veillonellaceae were found in HE patients compared with cirrhotics without HE. In the cirrhosis group, Alcaligenaceae and Porphyromonadaceae were positively correlated with cognitive impairmentterm. Fusobacteriaceae, Veillonellaceae, and Enterobacteriaceaeterm were positively and Ruminococcaceae was negatively related to inflammation. Thus, cirrhosis, especially when complicated with HE, is associated with significant gut microbiotaterm alterations compared with healthy individuals. Specific bacterial families ( Alcaligenaceae, Porphyromonadaceae, and Enterobacteriaceaeterm) are strongly associated with cognition and inflammation in HE [103].

##### Autism Spectrum Disordersterm Compared with the gut microbiotaterm of children without ASD, the gut microbiotaterm of children with ASD is less diverse and exhibits lower levels of Bifidobacteriumterm and Firmicutesterm and higher levels of Lactobacillus, Clostridiumterm, Bacteroidetesterm, Desulfovibrio, Caloramator, and Sarcina [104, 105, 106, 107]. A study by Wang et al. [108] showed a decrease in Bifidobacteriumterm spp. and mucolytic bacteria Akkermansia muciniphilaterm in the gut microbiotaterm of autism subjects compared to control subjects.

Williams et al. [109] found an increase in the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratioterm and a significant increase in Sutterella compared to their abundance in controls. These findings demonstrated that gut microbiota dysbiosisterm has close relationships with gastrointestinal and behavioral manifestations of autismterm.

##### Stress The bidirectional interactionsterm of gutterm–brainterm mean that the brainterm can also alter gut microbiotaterm composition [110]. A model of social disruption among adult mice [111] demonstrated that exposure to stressterm results in substantial changes in gut microbiota compositionterm: a decrease in Bacteroidesterm spp. abundance and an increase in Clostridiumterm spp. abundance have been observed in stress-induced mice relative to controls. The mechanisms by which stress influences gut microbiotaterm composition are unclear but include microbial habitat alterations following stress-induced changes in intestinal mobilityterm and mucin secretionterm [110, 112].

Other studies [62] revealed that chronic psychological stress is reflected in the microbiota composition. Indeed, depression due to chronic stressful life events is associated with an increase in Enterobacteriaceaeterm, whereas psychological stress is associated with a reduction in Lactobacilli spp. and an increase in Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas spp.

Gut microbiotaterm variations are closely associated with the onset of these neurological disorders, and the gut bacterial communitiesterm may be a target for therapeutic interventionsterm.

4. Conclusions Each healthy human is provided with unique gut microbiotaterm. Core native microbiota are shaped in early life (4–36 months) by gutterm maturation developing from enterotypeterm, birth gestational ageterm, type of deliveryterm·var, methods of milk feeding, weaning period, lifestyle, and dietary and cultural habits. After a child reaches 2–3 years old, a relative stability in gut microbiotaterm composition has been demonstrated. Richness and diversityterm of gut microbiotaterm shaped in early life characterize a healthy gut microbiotaterm composition. However, this optimal healthy gut microbiotaterm composition is different for each individual.

Throughout life, the richer and more diverse the microbiota, the better they will withstand external threats. Indeed, gut microbiotaterm represent a changing ecosystem that is severely tested by many factors such as unbalanced diet, stress, antibiotic use, or diseasesterm. A healthy host–microorganism balanceterm must be respected in order to optimally perform metabolic and immune functionsterm and prevent diseaseterm development. Indeed, disturbances to the delicate host–microbe relationshipterm may disrupt the development of the immune system, which may in turn result in diseasesterm [113]. Although the role of gut microbiotaterm is still poorly understood, the close association between gut microbiota dysbiosisterm and intestinal and extra-intestinal disordersterm has been demonstrated. This is the reason why dysbiosisterm can be considered as a biomarkerterm of such disorders and that the study of gut microbiotaterm balances shall be one of the priorities for future therapiesterm to prevent and treat diseasesterm.
