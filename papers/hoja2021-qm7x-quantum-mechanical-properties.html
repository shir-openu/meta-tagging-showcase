mechanismnormal-mode displacement sampling Each equilibrium structure supplies 100 non-equilibrium geometries obtained from linear combinations of DFTB3+MBD normal modes. — “In order to sample some of the non-equilibrium sectors of CCS, we generated 100 non-equilibrium structures for each of the (meta-)stable equilibrium structures described above. Background & Summary A crucial aspect of drug discovery 1 and molecular materials design 2 is an extensive exploration and understanding of chemical compound spaceterm (CCSterm)—the extremely high-dimensional space containing all feasible molecular compositionsterm·var and conformations. Recently, the combination of quantum mechanical (QM) calculations with machine learning (ML) has led to considerable insight into CCSterm 3 – 9 . However, progress along these lines can only happen with the availability of extensive and comprehensive QM-based datasets that adequately describe the complex structure–property relationships in molecules across CCSterm. In this regard, one challenge that is encountered during the generation of such datasets is the fact that their dimension scales exponentially with molecule size, thereby making it difficult to explore increasingly large swaths of CCSterm. The second challenge is the steep computational cost of tightly converged QM calculations, which are critical for obtaining an accurate and reliable description of the structure and physicochemical propertiesterm of each molecule.

To begin such an extensive exploration of CCSterm, the GDB datasets 1 , 10 , 11 have enumerated up to 166 B organic molecules containing up to 17 heavy (non-hydrogen) atoms. Encoded as canonical SMILESterm (simplified molecular-input line-entry systemterm) strings, the GDB datasets only provide the molecular formula and chemical connectivity, and do not contain any structural or molecular property information. As such, QM calculations on small subsets of the GDB datasets have subsequently been used to generate meta-stable conformations for each molecular compositionterm·var. This has led to seminal QM-based datasets like QM7 10 , 12 , 13 and QM9 11 , 14 , which are comprised of a single meta-stable molecular structure per SMILES string with up to seven and nine heavy (non-hydrogen) atoms, respectively. The QM7 and QM9term datasets have been widely used for benchmarking ML approaches and exploring molecular structure–property correlations 3 . In addition, molecular dynamics (MD) based datasets have become available for a few selected molecules and solids, and contain both equilibrium and non-equilibrium structuresterm 15 – 21 ; such datasets are becoming increasingly more useful for constructing advanced interatomic potentials 16 , 22 , 23 .

A more substantial coverage of CCSterm for small organic molecules was provided by Smith et al . 24 , 25 with the generation of the ANI-1 dataset, which consists of more than 20 M equilibrium and non-equilibrium conformations of molecules containing up to eight heavy (C, N, O) atoms from GDB-11 26 , 27 . More recently, the ANI-1x dataset 28 was introduced, which also contains 20 physicochemical propertiesterm for about 5 M structures computed using the semi-empirical ω B97X 29 density functional. In addition, Smith et al . 28 also provided local CCSD(T) energies for a smaller subset of 0.5 M structures. To date, the ANI-1 datasets contain the largest available collection of GDB-based QM calculations of molecular structures and properties. However, four challenges still remain to enable a systematic exploration of CCSterm for small organic molecules: (i) providing a systematic sampling of CCSterm in terms of constitutional/structural isomers and stereoisomersterm ( e.g ., enantiomers and diastereomers, including cis-/trans- and meta-stable conformational isomersterm), (ii) assessing the accuracy and reliability of QM structures and properties with respect to the employed density-functional approximation (especially for non-equilibrium conformations), (iii) offering a large set of local (atom-in-a-molecule) and global (molecular) physicochemical propertiesterm that would enable a comprehensive exploration of structure–property relationships throughout CCSterm, and (iv) providing accurate and reliable QM data that will enable the construction of models for describing covalent and non-covalent van der Waals (vdW) interactions.

In order to convincingly address these four challenges in this work, we present QM7-Xterm, which aims to provide a systematic, extensive, and tightly converged dataset of QM-based physical and chemical properties for a fundamentally important region of CCSterm covering small organic molecules (see Fig. 1 ). To do so, we performed a systematic and exhaustive sampling of the (meta-)stable equilibrium structuresterm of all molecules with up to seven heavy (C, N, O, S, Cl) atoms in the GDB13 databaseterm 10 using a density-functional tight binding (DFTB) approach; this includes constitutional/structural isomers and stereoisomersterm, e.g ., enantiomers and diastereomers (including cis-/trans- and conformational isomersterm). This was followed by the generation of 100 non-equilibrium structuresterm ( via DFTB normal-mode displacements of each equilibrium structureterm) for a total of ≈4.2 M molecular structures. For each of these equilibrium and non-equilibrium molecular structures, QM7-Xterm also includes an extensive number of QM-obtained physicochemical propertiesterm, most of which were computed using non-empirical hybrid density-functional theory (DFT) with a many-body treatment of vdW dispersion interactions ( i.e ., PBE0+MBDterm) in conjunction with tightly-converged numeric atom-centered orbitals 30 . In total, QM7-Xterm contains 42 molecular (global) and atom-in-a-molecule (local) properties, which range from ground state quantitiesterm (such as total and atomization energies, atomic forces, HOMO-LUMO gaps, dipole/quadrupole moments, Hirshfeld quantities, etc.) to response quantitiesterm (such as polarizability tensors and dispersion coefficients)—all of which could be utilized for the construction of next-generation intra- and inter-molecular force fields. As such, we expect that QM7-Xterm will be useful for the development of accurate and reliable ML-based techniques that will provide new insight into the complex structure–property relationships in molecules, and ultimately allow for more extensive exploration of CCSterm and the rational design of molecules with tailored properties. Methods Generation of equilibrium structuresterm As a basis for the QM7-Xterm dataset, we considered all molecules containing up to seven heavy (non-hydrogen) atoms in the GDB13 databaseterm 10 , which provides an enumeration of the CCSterm spanned by small organic molecules comprised of H, C, N, O, S, and Cl atoms. These molecules range in size from N = 4–23 atoms (see Fig. 1 ). For each molecular formula, the GDB13 databaseterm only includes chemical connectivity information ( i.e ., some structural/constitutional isomersterm) encoded as SMILES strings. To generate all of the corresponding structural/constitutional isomersterm and stereoisomersterm, e.g ., enantiomers and diastereomers (including cis-/trans- isomers) for each molecular formula, we created canonical SMILESterm strings for each possible molecular structure. Based on these SMILES strings, initial 3D structures were obtained using the MMFF94 force fieldterm 31 – 35 via the gen3d option in Open Babelterm 36 . For each of these structures, we also generated a set of sufficiently different (meta-)stable conformational isomersterm, i.e ., isomers which can be interconverted by rotations around single bonds. To do this, we performed a conformational isomerterm search with the Confab toolterm 37 in conjunction with the MMFF94 force fieldterm. At the MMFF94 level, we retained the set of conformers that were within 50 kcal/mol of the most stable structure and differed by a root-mean-square deviationterm (RMSDterm) of 0.5 Å.

All molecular structures were subsequently re-optimized with third-order self-consistent charge density functional tight binding (DFTB3) 38 – 40 supplemented with a treatment of many-body dispersion (MBD) interactions 41 – 44 , using the 3ob parameters 45 , 46 . All DFTB3+MBDterm calculations were performed using in-house versions of the DFTB+ code 47 and the Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE) 48 . Data Records The QM7-Xterm dataset is provided in eight HDF5term based files in a ZENODO.ORG data repositoryterm 50 . One can also find there a README file with technical usage details and examples of how to access the information stored in QM7-Xterm (with and without considering duplicates, see createDB.py file).

HDF5term file format The information for each molecular structure is stored in a Python dictionaryterm (dict) type containing all relevant properties and recorded in groups in HDF5term file format. HDF5term keys to access the atomic numbers, atomic positions (coordinates), and physicochemical propertiesterm in each dictionary are provided in Table 2 . The dimension of each array depends on the number of atoms and the required property, e.g ., for a methane (CH 4 ) molecule ‘atNUM’ is a 1D array of N = 5 elements ([6, 1, 1, 1, 1]) while ‘atXYZ’ is a 2D array comprised of N = 5 rows and three columns ( x , y , z coordinates). All structures are labeled as Geom-mr-is-ct-u, where r enumerates the SMILES strings, s the stereoisomersterm (excluding conformational isomersterm), t the considered (meta-)stable conformational isomersterm, and u the optimized/displaced structures ( u = opt indicates the DFTB3+MBDterm optimized structures and u = 1, …, 100 indicates the displaced non-equilibrium structuresterm). We note in passing that the indices used in the QM7-Xterm dataset reflect the order in which a given structure was generated and do not correspond to sorted DFTB3+MBDterm (or PBE0+MBDterm) energies.

Technical Validation In contrast to many other QM-based datasets like QM7 and QM9term, which contain a single structure per molecule, the QM7-Xterm dataset not only includes constitutional/structural isomers, but also stereoisomersterm ( e.g ., enantiomers and diastereomers, including cis-/trans- and conformational isomersterm) as well as non-equilibrium structural variations thereof. While many of the considered molecules do overlap with those in the ANI-1 dataset 24 , QM7-Xterm also contains a more extensive sampling of equilibrium stereoisomersterm and a significantly different sampling of non-equilibrium structuresterm ( vide infra ). Moreover, the number of reported physicochemical propertiesterm and the employed level of electronic structure theory in QM7-Xterm is substantially more advanced compared to prior work. Therefore, the QM7-Xterm dataset contains significantly more data for flexible molecules with stereocenters than for rigid molecules without stereocenters. Since stereochemistry can play an important role in drug design, we consider that the data provided in this work will enable ML models to capture the subtle physicochemical differences existing between stereoisomersterm.

Another important target of the QM7-Xterm dataset is efficient PESterm sampling for a large number of small organic molecules. Since the most crucial regions of the PESterm are reasonably close to the relevant (meta-)stable equilibrium structuresterm, QM7-Xterm contains 100 distorted/non-equilibrium structuresterm for every such conformer. While most of these structures are in the vicinity of the optimized/equilibrium structuresterm, some correspond to more highly distorted structures on the PESterm. Since it was only computationally feasible to consider 100 non-equilibrium structuresterm at the PBE0+MBDterm level, these structures were chosen to cover as much of the PESterm surrounding each (meta-)stable equilibrium structureterm as possible. As such, we created these non-equilibrium structuresterm by displacing the atoms in a given molecule along linear combinations of normal modes. Although a similar approach was utilized during the construction of the ANI-1 dataset 24 , we have eliminated all issues related to the inaccuracy of the harmonic approximation by recomputing the energy of every candidate structure and only accepting those structures with a Boltzmann energy distributionterm (see Methods). In doing so, we ensure that the relevant regions of each PESterm are well-sampled and only a small (and pre-determined) number of high-energy non-equilibrium structuresterm are included in our dataset. The inclusion of non-equilibrium structuresterm for each (meta-)stable equilibrium structureterm also provides better coverage of the conformational space than initially provided by the equilibrium structuresterm, as seen in the pairwise distance distribution plots shown in Fig. 2 . In this work, the structure generation process was performed using DFTB3+MBDterm, while the subsequent energy, force, and property calculations were performed at the more robust PBE0+MBDterm level of theory. Since the focus of this work is the physicochemical propertiesterm of non-equilibrium structuresterm surrounding (meta-)stable equilibrium points on each PESterm, the use of these two methods does not introduce any significant complications and/or errors. Although the PBE0+MBDterm (relative) energies will not strictly follow the same Boltzmann distribution as that used to generate the molecular structures at the DFTB3+MBDterm level, these two distributions are often very similar and lead to virtually the same <Δ E> . An average over all histograms is depicted in Fig. S1 , where one can see some minor variations in the heights of the first few bins and only an insignificant amount of structures located slightly outside of the initially envisioned window of Δ E values. In addition, we have also quantitatively analyzed the structural agreement between conformers optimized at the DFTB3+MBDterm and PBE0+MBDterm levels. To do this, the DFTB3+MBD-optimized structures of 10 flexible molecules with at least five (meta-)stable conformers (see SI for more details) were randomly selected and re-optimized with PBE0+MBDterm, followed by a (harmonic) vibrational frequency analysis at the same level of theory. In doing so, it was found that all 63 so obtained PBE0+MBDterm geometries constitute local or global minima ( i.e ., with no imaginary frequencies), and the average RMSDterm between the DFTB3+MBDterm and PBE0+MBDterm structures amounts to only 0.1 Å. These structural differences are visualized for several conformers in Figs. S3 – S5 in the SI. Even for the conformer with the largest observed RMSDterm of 0.66 Å, we note that the PBE0+MBDterm structure did not move to any of the other considered conformers, and resulted from modifications to the backbone structure. From this analysis, we would argue that the DFTB3+MBD-optimized structures are in excellent agreement with those optimized at the PBE0+MBDterm level, and hence provide a high-fidelity representation of the critical points on the PBE0+MBDterm PESterm.

Over 40 different physicochemical propertiesterm were computed to describe the structure–property and property–property relationships in QM7-Xterm. Regarding ML applications, we are confident that the data provided by QM7-Xterm will enable continued development of ML approaches, identification of novel QM-based molecular descriptors, as well as determination of robust training sample selectionterm·var methods that will improve the accuracy and reliability of molecular property predictions. For instance, Stöhr et al . 61 have recently employed different methods for selecting the molecular structures from QM7-Xterm to define training sets for the prediction of DFTB repulsive potentials. In this work, it was found that randomly selected training samplesterm have a better performance compared to more refined training setsterm ( e.g ., consisting of equilibrium structuresterm together with a given number ( X ) of non-equilibrium structuresterm) when developing accurate many-body repulsive potentials for the DFTB method via deep tensor neural networks. Hence, QM7-Xterm can be used to improve the accuracy of electronic structure methods using ML approaches such as Δ-learning. Further improvements of ML models may also be accomplished by considering physics-/chemistry-based approaches for selecting training samples ( e.g ., based on the local chemical environment surrounding each atom).
