However, few global studies have demonstrated that protectionterm·dom benefits a broad range of species. Here, using a new global biodiversity database with unprecedented geographic and taxonomic coverage, we compare four biodiversity measures at sites sampled in multiple land usesterm·key inside and outside protected areasterm·key. Protected areasterm·key are considered an essential strategy for habitat and species conservation 1 . Parties to the Convention on Biological Diversity have committed to increase the terrestrial area currently under protectionterm·dom 2 from 15.4% to at least 17% in ‘effectively and equitably managed, ecologically representative and well connected' protected areasterm·key by 2020 (Aichi biodiversity target 11) 3 . A recent assessment of progress suggests that the coverage target will be met, and that protected areaterm·key representativeness and management are improving 4 .

However, there is some doubt over the success of protected areasterm·key. Management effectiveness reports suggest only 22% of protected areasterm·key have ‘sound management' 5 , experts estimate that only half of all tropical reserves are effective 6 and human pressures are increasing in Latin American, African and Asian protected areasterm·key 7 . Declines in animal and plant abundanceterm·key have been documented inside protected areasterm·key 6 , 8 , 9 and in many countries the effectiveness of protectionterm·dom is being compromised by external pressures and inadequate government support 10 , 11 . Protecting all terrestrial sites of conservation significance could cost US$ 76 billion annually 12 , plus associated opportunity costs (up to US$ 6,500 ha −1 for productive agricultural land 13 ). Quantifying the effectiveness of protected areasterm·key is therefore crucial to justify maintaining and expanding the network.

Evidence from remote sensing suggests that protected areasterm·key slow change from ‘natural' to ‘human-modified' land cover 14 and successfully retain forests 1 . In the tropicsterm·key, protectionterm·dom reduces deforestationterm·dom 15 , 16 , 17 , loss of carbon 18 and forest fires 19 . Geldmann et al . 1 found that most of the 42 studies in their meta-analysis reported that abundancesterm·key of species were higher inside protected areasterm·key, but acknowledged the limited evidence base. Coetzee et al . 22 , reviewing 86 studies, found a positive effect of protectionterm·dom on species richnessterm·key and abundanceterm·key. Consequently, it is not yet clear whether protectionterm·dom solely maintains greater numbers of individuals (and therefore higher species richnessterm·key) or additionally maintains a greater variety of niches. If the latter is true, we expect protectionterm·dom to be associated with more species for a given number of individuals (that is, a higher rarefaction-based richness 26 —hereafter ‘rarefied richnessterm·key').

A further limitation of some previous studies is that the apparent success of protected areasterm·key may be caused by their location rather than protectionterm·dom per se 27 . To account for this bias, several analyses of land cover have matched protected locations or pixels to unprotected counterparts having similar values of potential confounding variables such as elevation or slope 14 , 17 , 19 , 28 , 29 , 30 . Alternatively, potential confounding variables have been included as covariates in models of land-use change 31 , 32 , including some analyses that have compared matched pairs of protected and unprotected locations as differences between paired locations remained even after matching 24 . Both previous meta-analyses of protected-area effects on species 1 , 22 were limited in their ability to account for potential confounding factors using either of these approaches as they lacked geographic coordinates for individual sample sites and data on specific land useterm·key types. Therefore, it remains undecided whether protectionterm·dom offers benefits to biodiversity beyond those attributable to reduced land-use change, that is, whether protectionterm·dom can benefit biodiversity even within a particular land useterm·key.

Analyses of spatial comparisons cannot reveal the mechanism behind differences between protected and unprotected sites. Protected areasterm·key may have been selected based on pre-existing biodiversity gradients, or protected areaterm·key management may result in the preservation of populations lost from surrounding areas. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive. Time-series data on species and assemblages inside and outside protected areasterm·key would be helpful for determining their relative importance. Additionally, we may underestimate the overall contribution of protectionterm·dom, as many protected areasterm·key aim to protect biodiversity features not included in our analyses, such as beta diversity, particular rare species, migratory routes or ecological processes 20 .

Protected areasterm·key with more restrictive management objectives are expected to retain more biodiversity. Although IUCN categories are not necessarily applied consistently across countries 20 , several studies have used them to compare biodiversity across management objectives 16 , 17 , 19 , 21 , 22 , 23 . As most of these studies focus on land cover (particularly deforestationterm·dom), there is little information on how effects on species differ among protected areasterm·key in different IUCN categories; the limited available evidence indicates a positive effect size for both the most restrictive IUCN categories and for one category that allows some extraction 22 . We found no significant differences among protected areaterm·key management category groups (three groups, in order of lowest to highest restriction on human activity: IUCN categoryterm·key III–VI, IUCN categoryterm·key unknown (a mixture of categories, considered an intermediate category on average) and IUCN categoryterm·key I and II; Fig. 2a ; all comparisons among protected groups gave P >0.2), but samples from protected sites in each management category group had higher species richnessterm·key than those from unprotected sites ( Fig. 2a ; χ 2 =11.18, df =3, P= 0.011. There was large variation but no significant difference in abundanceterm·key, rarefied richnessterm·key or endemicityterm·mth among management category groups and unprotected sites ( Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1 ). The heterogeneity within groups could reflect conservation objectives not captured in a protected areaterm·key's IUCN categoryterm·key 35 or differences in how these categories have been applied 20 .

Effects of protectionterm·dom within and among land usesterm·key As all analyses above accounted for differences in elevation, slope and agricultural suitability, differences in land useterm·key are the most likely explanation for higher species richnessterm·key and total abundanceterm·key in samples from protected sites. First, we included site-specific land useterm·key as a fixed effect in our models; and second, we restricted our dataset to sites matched by land useterm·key across the protected areaterm·key boundary ( Fig. 1d ). In both cases, we also explored whether the effect of protectionterm·dom varied with latitudinal zone and taxonomic group.

Perhaps surprisingly, endemicityterm·mth was lower at protected than unprotected sites in human-dominated land usesterm·key, particularly in cropland ( Fig. 3d and Supplementary Table 2 ) and for vertebrates and plants ( Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 2d ). This effect suggests that protected areasterm·key in human-dominated land usesterm·key may either benefit species with wide ranges or may have been located specifically to protect them (for example, migratory birds).

Estimating the global effectiveness of protected areasterm·key We extrapolated from our models of local biodiversity inside and outside protected areasterm·key ( Fig. 3 ) to estimate the effectiveness of the current global protected areaterm·key network at retaining site-level biodiversity. Our measure of effectiveness would be 0% if sites within protected areasterm·key are, on average, as diverse as unprotected sites, and 100% if the biodiversity of protected sites is, on average, as high as for ‘pristine' sites (minimally-impacted primary vegetation; note that the scale is not bounded at either 0% or 100%). On this scale, we estimate that on average the global protected areaterm·key network is 41% (95% CI: 2 to 81%) effective at retaining within-sample species richnessterm·key and 54% (95% CI: 0 to 136%) effective at retaining local abundanceterm·key.

How to improve the global protected areaterm·key network An important question for environmental decision makers is whether biodiversity will benefit more from increasing restrictions on human activity in existing protected areasterm·key or from expanding the network 39 , 40 . Although the trend towards higher species richnessterm·key and abundanceterm·key at sites in protected areasterm·key with more restrictive management objectives was not statistically significant, the coefficients suggest the effects of management restrictiveness may be large ( Fig. 2a,b ). The large uncertainty seen in these models reflects both a lack of precise data on the objectives of protected areaterm·key management and on effectiveness, and a lower number of sites with the most restrictive management. Better information on protected areaterm·key management intent and effectiveness is needed to confidently quantify the biodiversity outcome of increasing management restrictions. Discussion In summary, these first detailed global analyses of site-level data on a large, taxonomically broad set of species show that, overall, samples from protected sites contained more individuals and species than samples from unprotected sites. By contrast, protected sites did not consistently have higher rarefied richnessterm·key or levels of endemicityterm·mth—both measures of community characteristics that are often considered when setting conservation priorities. The greatest differences in species richnessterm·key and abundanceterm·key occurred across land usesterm·key: protected areasterm·key are most effective where they minimize human-dominated land useterm·key, especially where they safeguard primary or mature secondary vegetation. However, the positive effect of protectionterm·dom within human-dominated land usesterm·key, particularly in the tropicsterm·key, shows that land useterm·key is not the only cause of higher biodiversity within protected areasterm·key. Better data on management is needed to quantify biodiversity benefits of restricting human activity inside protected areasterm·key but, if the trend in our data is confirmed, more restrictive protected areaterm·key management across the current network could be as important as extending the network. Importantly, we cannot discern whether protectionterm·dom has prevented losses in site-level biodiversity seen in surrounding areas, increased numbers of individuals, or retained a pre-existing biodiversity gradient. Nonetheless, these analyses represent a substantial advance in knowledge about several measures of biodiversity inside versus outside protected areasterm·key. Our results reinforce recent calls 45 for increased support and recognition of the importance of protected areasterm·key worldwide 10 , 11 , but highlight that the network is not currently effective for all measures of local biodiversity.

Analyses We used generalized linear mixed-effects modelsterm·key to account for differences in response variables due to study-specific methodologies and the spatial structure of sites 46 . The PREDICTSterm·mth data present a rare opportunity to compare sites inside and outside protected areasterm·key, but do not have the geographic coverage required for a stricter counterfactual approach 14 , 17 in which sites are individually matched. To reduce the risk that any differences observed between sites inside and outside protected areasterm·key were caused by biases in the location of protected areasterm·key 27 , we considered elevation 47 and derived slope at c. 1 km 2 resolution and agricultural suitability 48 at 10 km 2 resolution as covariates in all models (see Supplementary Information for further details). For example, distances to roads and markets are affected by the presence of protected areasterm·key so are not independent confounding factors (see Supplementary Information for details). We sequentially compared models with and without each fixed effect and at each step dropped the term with the highest P -value, until all terms had P <0.05 (ref. 49 ).
