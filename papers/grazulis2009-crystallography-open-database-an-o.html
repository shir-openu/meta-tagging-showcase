Object of study — an open-accessterm collection of published crystal structures, and the deposition machinery - identifiers, versioning, duplicate detection, validation - that lets a community keep one

Research question — how to gather structural data that was historically split across three specialised databases into one searchable open collection, and keep it correct while it grows

Contribution — the CODterm itself at ~80,000 entries with near-full coverage of IUCr publications, plus a deposition pipeline that is described in enough detail to be re-implemented: permanent identifiers, Subversion revisions written into the file header, a four-condition duplicate rule ending in a human

transferfrom software engineering: software version control - Subversion, revision numbers, change logs, checkout of prior states - carried from programming practice into the curation of a scientific data record — “Since the COD identifier of a structure, once assigned, remains unchanged, a problem might arise when a deposited COD file needs to be changed for some reason, say, a syntax or data error must be corrected after the deposition. Each change of any COD file is recorded in a central COD repository, and the new version of the file automatically gets a new revision number. # Abstract

The Crystallography Open Database (CODterm) is an ongoing initiative by crystallographers to gather all published inorganic, metal–organic and small organic molecule structures in one database, providing a straightforward search and retrieval interface. The CODterm adopts an open-accessterm model for its >80 000 structure files.

The Crystallography Open Database (CODterm), which is a project that aims to gather all available inorganic, metal–organic and small organic molecule structural data in one database, is described. The database adopts an open-accessterm model. The CODterm currently contains ∼80 000 entries in crystallographic information file format, with nearly full coverage of the International Union of Crystallography publications, and is growing in size and quality.

## Introduction

The Crystallography Open Database (CODterm) is a recent tool offered to the scientific community on the Web at http://www.crystallography.net/. It was founded in February 2003 as a response to Michael Berndt’s letter published in the Structure Determination by Powder Diffractometry (SDPD) mailing list (http://tech.groups.yahoo.com/group/sdpd/message/1016).

The historical fragmentationterm of structural data into three databases covering inorganic compounds (Inorganic Crystal Structure Database; Belsky et al., 2002), metals, alloys and intermetallics (Crystal Data for Metals Database, CRYSTMET; White et al., 2002), and organic and organometallic small molecules (Cambridge Structural Database; Allen, 2002) reflects the fact that in the past most public or private research laboratories concentrated their activity on one or the other of these very specialized topics. However, nowadays researchers at a given laboratory frequently extend their activities to all classes of compounds, focusing instead on materials with specific properties within more general classes of compounds, such as nanomaterials, hybrids or gas storage. Consequently, there is now a need to have access to all these databases simultaneously [together with powder data from the International Center for Diffraction Data (Kabekkodu et al., 2002) that are generally added for identification purposes].

Thus, a group of scientists (Armel Le BailpersName, Luca LutterottipersName and Lachlan Cranswick) responded quickly to Michael Berndt’s letter and teamed up to create an open crystallography database. The group contacted Professor Robert T. DownspersName who generously offered strong support for the concept, including the data set from the American Mineralogist Crystal Structure Database (AMCSD; DownspersName & Hall-Wallace, 2003; DownspersName, 2008), along with MySQL/PHP scripts written by Hareesh Rajan. At the same time, Daniel ChateignerpersName joined, and less than three weeks after the letter from Michael Berndt, the CODterm project was announced through various Internet media (newsgroups, various mailing lists and what’s new pages).

This announcement introduced some new members to the effort (Brian Toby and Alexandre YokochipersName) and, by the end of March 2003, the number of entries in the CODterm had increased to more than 5000. In order to ensure quality and standardization of uploaded files, the CIF2COD computer program was built by modifying CIF2SX with permission from Louis Farrugia. The first CODterm search page was coded in the PHP language. Uploads of crystallographic information files (CIFs) continued in April 2003 (1200 files from the Institut de Physique de la Matière Condensée, Grenoble) and after four months the number of entries in the CODterm surpassed 12 000 (Le BailpersName, 2003) as a result of uploads by individuals, laboratories and data shared by the AMCSD.

In December 2003, a subset of the CODterm was created and named PCOD (Predicted Crystallography Open Database) with the goal of gathering computationally predicted structures and with the expectation that the number of predicted entries could easily exceed the number of experimentally determined ones. In January 2004, the PCOD offered 200 entries.

By October 2005, the CODterm contained 20 000 entries. The database also had 30 new volunteers along with three new CODterm Advisory Board members (Saulius GražulispersName, Miguel QuiróspersName Olozábal and Peter MoeckpersName). With the help of these volunteers, the number of entries in the CODterm increased to 48 000 by December 2006, now including 10 000 structures from the AMCSD (DownspersName & Hall-Wallace, 2003; DownspersName, 2008). In February 2007, a massive PCOD update boosted the number of entries to more than 60 000, with the help of the GRINSP software (Le BailpersName, 2005) for crystal structure prediction. At the same time, using the PCOD data, the Predicted Powder Diffraction Database (P2D2; Le BailpersName, 2008) was created, which provides identification by a search–match procedure similar to that of the Powder Diffraction File (Kabekkodu et al., 2002).

In September 2007, the IUCr Executive Committee decided that the CIFs associated with structural papers published in IUCr journals should be made freely available to all databases, including the CODterm, giving the CODterm permission to routinely download new files from the IUCr site. This very welcome decision brought about a reorganization of the CODterm with the center of operations being transferred from Le Mans (France) to Vilnius (Lithuania) in December 2007.

Five years after its foundation, in 2008, the CODterm passed a major milestone by archiving the 50 000th entry, while PCOD climbed over the 100 000 structure limit in the same year. Our actions to date are but the start of this database, and the CODterm hopes that more crystallographers will upload their results in order to accelerate its completion.

New developments at the CODterm including automation of data deposition, data validation and correction, a novel search interface, and mirror sites and their synchronization, as well as the calculation of powder diffraction patterns, are briefly described in this paper.

## Methodology

### CODterm and PCOD contents

The CODterm and PCOD each consist of two major parts: an SQL database and a collection of structure data files. The structure files record crystallographic data that were published in peer-reviewed scientific journals, or that were determined or predicted and donated by established crystallographic laboratories. The master copy of the data is recorded in CIFterm format (Hall et al., 1991).

From the master copy of the (P)CODterm data collection, data tables for the (P)CODterm SQL databases are generated. These tables abstract the most important crystallographic, chemical and bibliographic information and are used for online searches. Currently, the data tables contain cell constantsterm (a, b, c, α, β, γ), cell volume, Hermann–Mauguin space-group symbol, a summary chemical formula, the number of distinct chemical elements, and a descriptive text that includes the chemical names of the substance and bibliographic references. A special field, coeditor code, is also included in the database, in order to generate URL links to the original papers for those journals that accept data sharing (currently, the IUCr journals).

We check and, when possible, restore systematic and trivial names of the reported chemical compounds and their formulas (IUPAC, structural and summary), since this information is vital for identification of the material. Information about chemical and hydrogen bonds is preserved in the CODterm CIFs if present in the original data file, but is not otherwise inferred from the structure.

Each structure deposited in the CODterm and the PCOD gets a unique seven-digit number, a (P)CODterm identifier. If a structure of a compound is redetermined, with higher precision or under different conditions, it will be deposited in the (P)CODterm under a new (P)CODterm number.

Since the CODterm identifier of a structure, once assigned, remains unchanged, a problem might arise when a deposited CODterm file needs to be changed for some reason, say, a syntax or data error must be corrected after the deposition. Currently, we have adopted a version control system called Subversion (Collins-Sussman et al., 2008). Each change of any CODterm file is recorded in a central CODterm repository, and the new version of the file automatically gets a new revision numberterm. These numbers, along with the CODterm repository address, are inserted by the software into the CODterm file header. There is a publicly accessible interface to the CODterm that allows older revisions of any file to be extracted and the CODterm change logs recorded by CODterm maintainers to be read. Having a CODterm number and the revision numberterm of a file, it is always possible to restore a previous version of that file.

Structures are accepted in two formats – standard CIFterm format (http://www.iucr.org/resources/cifterm/) and a very simple REF (http://www.crystallography.net/ref.html) format, devised by A. Le BailpersName. The REF format is intended to be used in those cases where old data, predating the CIFterm era, need to be keyed in by hand or converted from some other format.

### CODterm deposition procedure and validation

Data in REF format, or occasionally in some other formats, are converted into the CIFterm format and then enter the same validation and deposition procedure as CIFs (Fig. 1 ▶). Each CIFterm is checked for syntax errors, using both the publicly available ‘vcif’ tool from the IUCr (McMahon, 1998) and our own CIFterm parser written in Perl (http://www.perl.com/; Wall et al., 2000). Syntactic errors, if any, must be corrected manually; this task is currently performed by a CODterm maintainer responsible for deposition. When the syntax is correct, structures are assigned a new range of sequential CODterm numbers. Bibliographic information is taken either from the data sections of the CIFs, from the data_global sections, or from auxiliary files in BibTeX (Patashnik, 2003) or PubMed XML format (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/query/static/overview.html). As a last resort, the bibliographic information may be taken from the names of the directories containing CIFs, which are then chosen to reflect journal, year and journal issue, or the name of the donating person and laboratory. Each separate CIFterm is given a full copy of available bibliographic information, so that it can be further processed and stored independently.

The CIFs now can be validated to check whether all necessary data items such as cell parameters, symmetry or bibliography are present. When all quality checks are passed, the existing CODterm database is scanned for duplicatesterm. Duplicate structures are as a rule not deposited into the CODterm. A structure is considered a potential duplicate if its cell constantsterm are within 0.5 Å and cell angles within 1.2° of any existing entry, the summary chemical formulas match, and both structures have been published in the same paper. If pressure and temperature are specified, these are also checked, and structures are considered duplicatesterm only if they were measured under identical conditions. All potential duplicatesterm are flagged and reviewed manually.

The final step involves insertion of the CIFs into the Subversion repository and insertion of the data dump into the CODterm SQL table. The checked CIFs are presented to the CIF2COD program, which computes some derived data and creates a data dump that can be loaded into the CODterm MySQL table. The new structures become available on the Web immediately after deposition.

Automated procedures have been developed to simplify the submission of data for users. For several years now, the MAUD software (LutterottipersName et al., 1999) has included algorithms for submission. Such functionality makes it simple to submit data to the CODterm; submission does not even require a visit to the CODterm Internet page. The ‘Submit Structure to CODterm’ submenu lists the CIFterm of one of the actual phases of a given analysis. The corresponding window allows manual modification of the file, if necessary, before the submission is completed by simply clicking the ‘Submit to CODterm’ button. After submission, the uploaded CIFterm is treated identically to other ‘regular’ submissions from the Internet.

## Discussion

### Current status of CODterm and PCOD

Currently, the CODterm stores over 80 000 structures of small organic and metal–organic molecules, inorganics, and minerals. The PCOD contains over 100 000 predicted inorganic crystal structures in CIFterm format, generated by the GRINSP programs (Le BailpersName, 2005). The number of different structure types is close to 30 000, the total number being attained by adding series of isostructural virtual compounds. For instance, there are ∼6400 different (Al/P)O4 compounds, and three other series of isostructural compounds with formulations SiO2, (Al/Si)O4 and (Al/S)O4. Besides the possibility of searching through the PCOD web interface, in a similar way to the CODterm, 48 complete series of compounds (characterized by the presence of the same chemical elements) are downloadable for prospective research.

For anybody who wishes to use the CODterm and the PCOD databases, the collected files are presented using standard open protocols and formats. The database can be searched online on the CODterm server using a simple web-based search form, and the structural results can be downloaded either one by one or in a compressed .zip file. Alternatively, the whole collection of the CODterm files and database tables can be downloaded from the CODterm web site (using the http protocol) as a compressed .zip, .tar.gz or .tar.bz2 file, or updated via an rsync protocol (http://samba.anu.edu.au/rsync/) from rsync://www.crystallography.net/cod-cif and rsync://www.crystallography.net/pcod-cif so that the files can be used and examined on a user’s local machine. Finally, the CODterm and PCOD CIFs, database dumps and web scripts are available for anonymous checkouts from the CODterm Subversion server (svn://www.crystallography.net/codterm and svn://www.crystallography.net/pcod). From this server an interested user can reconstitute locally the whole CODterm database and the web site for local searches, and also browse CODterm deposition logs and retrieve older revisions, should they be necessary.

To facilitate the use of the CODterm as a reference database, it is planned that all data published in the CODterm will be assigned persistent URLs. Thus, any structure deposited in the CODterm should be available as http://www.crystallography.net/cifterm/〈CODterm number〉, e.g. http://www.crystallography.net/cifterm/1000000.cifterm.

The open-accessterm nature of the CODterm and the PCOD permits the creation of numerous mirrorsterm of the CODterm and the PCOD. At present, three mirrorsterm are available at http://codterm.ibt.lt/, http://codterm.ensicaen.fr/ and http://nanocrystallography.org/. Currently, one centralized repository is kept as an authoritative source of data, but with the growth of the databases a decentralized implementation is possible.

### Future directions of CODterm and PCOD development

A current challenge for all crystallographic databases, including the CODterm, is an exponential increase in the number of determined structural data entries. Fortunately, there is plenty of room to improve the efficiency of the CODterm deposition procedure. The current procedure involves a step in which a CODterm number is assigned by CODterm coordinator, and a step where the structures are checked by human depositors for possible errors. Both steps can be automated and parallelized. Finally, the structures still requiring human attention can be checked and edited in parallel by numerous CODterm reviewers all over the world, provided there is adequate software and enough volunteers participate in CODterm maintenance. Currently, the number of people contributing or willing to contribute to the development of the CODterm amounts to several dozen, apparently enough to provide qualified peer-review for the incoming structures. The development of the automatic data submission, annotation and CIFterm correction software is under way. Calculation of powder patterns is implemented for the PCOD data in the Match! software (http://www.crystalimpact.com/match/match18.htm).

For researchers who wish to publish their structure-related work, most journals require the deposition of structures with a crystallographic database and ask for the database accession number as proof of deposition. For such structures, a special deposition status, ‘on hold until publication’, will be introduced. The structures submitted to the CODterm with the ‘on hold’ flag will be included in the CODterm SQL database where their cell constantsterm, composition, symmetry and authorship will be indicated. A CODterm number will be assigned to the structure and returned to the author, and will be visible through the search interface of the CODterm. The atomic coordinates themselves, however, will not be released to the public until either the publication describing them appears, the authors inform the CODterm team that the coordinates should be released, or one year elapses from the original deposition of the CIFs. If the structure is not published within one year, an e-mail will be sent to the depositing author asking whether the structure should be released or withdrawn.

At present, one of the main limitations of the functionality of the CODterm is the absence of a substructureterm search engine. In organic and metal–organic chemistry, the best way of defining such similarity is generally the presence of a common group of atoms chemically linked in the same way: this is what we call a ‘substructureterm’. For performing this task with CODterm data, we need to represent the chemical connectivity of the structures included in the CODterm in a suitable format, provide a tool for the user to input into the CODterm the definition of the substructureterm and finally employ a search–match engine that compares the user input against the CODterm data. A specialized chemical format such as CML (http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_Markup_Language) or SMILES (http://www.opensmiles.org/) with molecules already ‘grown’ across any possible crystallographic symmetry elements and simplifying the possible presence of chemically identical but crystallographically different moieties could be used for encoding the necessary information. The tools for user-friendly structure input and search are available under both free and commercial licenses (http://xdrawchem.sourceforge.net/, http://www.cambridgesoft.com/software/ChemOffice/, http://sourceforge.net/projects/joelib/, http://sourceforge.net/projects/cdk/). The remaining task is the integration of these tools with the CODterm.
