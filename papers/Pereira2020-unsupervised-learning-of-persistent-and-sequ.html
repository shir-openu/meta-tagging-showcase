object of study — evidence — “One unresolved issue is whether the learning rules used by brain networks to learn PA are fundamentally different than the ones used to learn SA, or whether the same learning rule can produce both, depending on the statistics of the inputs to the network. Learning rules employed in theoretical studies to learn PA typically do not contain any temporal asymmetry, while rules used to learn SA need to contain such a temporal asymmetry.”
research question — evidence — “It has been hypothesized that both types of dynamics can be “learned” by the relevant networks from the statistics of their inputs, thanks to mechanisms of synaptic plasticity. However, the necessary conditions for a synaptic plasticity rule and input statistics to learn these two types of dynamics in a stable fashion are still unclear. In particular, it is unclear whether a single learning rule is able to learn both types of activity patterns, depending on the statistics of the inputs driving the network.”
contribution — evidence — “Depending on the stimulus parameters, the network is flexible enough to learn selectively both types of activity by repeated exposure to a sequence of stimuli, without need for supervision. transferfrom dynamical systems theory: a full bifurcation diagram computed for fixed connectivity, then used as the map on which learning is a trajectory — “Using the bifurcation diagram derived for fixed connectivity, we study analytically the temporal evolution and the steady state of the learned recurrent architecture as a function of parameters characterizing the external inputs. Slow changing stimuli lead to PA, while fast changing stimuli lead to SA. Our network model shows how a network with plastic synapses can stably and flexibly learn PA and SA in an unsupervised manner.”
transferfrom synaptic physiology: homeostatic scaling, generalized so that its speed depends on how far the rate is from target — “As explained in Zenke and Gerstner ( 2017 ) and Zenke et al. ( 2017 ), in order to prevent the runaway of the synaptic weights produced by Hebbian plasticity, the timescale of any compensatory mechanism should be of the same order or faster than the Hebbian timescale. For generalized homeostatic plasticity, the timescale of the homeostatic variable H i is dependent on the firing rate of neuron i and the target firing rate [i.e., ϕ( u i )/ϕ( u 0 )]. When the network firing rate is close to the target firing rate the homeostatic learning rule is slow, and the homeostatic mechanism seldom play a role in the dynamics. On the other hand, for high firing rates the homeostatic plasticity timescale becomes faster, preventing the runaway of the synaptic weights. There is currently an ongoing debate about whether the timescales of compensatory processes used in theoretical studies, as the ones used here, are consistent with experimental evidence”
transferfrom machine learning: the supervised/unsupervised division, imported as the axis along which sequence-learning models are classified — “The models investigated can be roughly divided in two categories: models with supervised and unsupervised plasticity rules. In models with supervised plasticity rules, the synapses are updated according the activity of the network and an error signal that carries information about the difference between the current network dynamics and the one that it is expected to learn by the network (Sussillo and Abbott, 2009 ; Laje and Buonomano, 2013 ; Memmesheimer et al., 2014 ; Rajan et al., 2016 ). In models with unsupervised plasticity rules, sequential dynamics is shaped by external stimulation without an error signal (Jun and Jin, 2007 ; Liu and Buonomano, 2009 ; Fiete et al., 2010 ; Waddington et al., 2012 ; Okubo et al., 2015 ; Veliz-Cuba et al., 2015 ). In those models SA is generated spontaneously, and the temporal statistics of the stimulation shapes the specific timing of the sequences.”
transferfrom population modelling: the Wilson-Cowan rate description, chosen because it can be solved rather than only simulated — “We use a rate model to describe the activity of populations of neurons (Wilson and Cowan, 1972 ). The connectivity in this model represents the average of the synaptic connections between populations of neurons, allowing to investigate at a mesoscopic level the learning mechanisms of PA and SA. This model has the advantage of analytical tractability.”
mechanismHomeostatic scaling masks the Hebbian-learned excitatory weights while the stimulus is on and unmasks them afterwards, so the strong connectivity never feeds back into the activity that creates it. — “We show that a generalized version of the standard multiplicative homeostatic plasticity (Renart et al., 2003 ; Toyoizumi et al., 2014 ) stabilizes learning by effectively masking excitatory connections during stimulation and unmasking those connections during retrieval.”
mechanismWithout a stabilizer, rising synaptic weights raise firing rates which in turn raise the weights, a positive feedback loop that runs away to saturation. — “This is a consequence of the classic instability observed with Hebbian plasticity rules, where a positive feedback loop between the increase in synaptic connectivity and increase in firing rates leads to an explosive increase in both”
mechanismUnder synaptic normalization the fixed weight budget forces the very connections that sustain co-active assemblies to be depressed as new co-activations appear, so assemblies keep being rebuilt instead of settling. — “This leads to complex dynamics in the synaptic weights, in which the connections sustaining co-active neuronal assemblies (i.e., group of excitatory populations with clustered connectivity) learned via Hebbian plasticity are depressed due to the interplay between synaptic normalization and sequential stimulation.”
mechanismthe stabiliser works by masking the excitatory connections while the stimulus is on and unmasking them at retrieval, so the network cannot drive itself during learning — “Consistent with previous studies, we find that for stable learning of PA and SA, an additional stabilization mechanism is necessary. We show that a generalized version of the standard multiplicative homeostatic plasticity (Renart et al., 2003 ; Toyoizumi et al., 2014 ) stabilizes learning by effectively masking excitatory connections during stimulation and unmasking those connections during retrieval.”
mechanismwithout it the previously stimulated population is still active when the next one arrives, which strengthens the connection between them until every population is persistently active and every weight is maximal — “In our model, unsupervised temporally asymmetric Hebbian plasticity is unstable, and after a number of stimulations, all synaptic weights increase to the maximum value. The destabilization occurs during sequential stimulation when the population previously stimulated in the sequence presents persistent activity at the moment the next population is being stimulated. This leads to the increase of the feedforward synaptic weight between these two populations, producing that both present PA while the next population in the sequence is being stimulated. This process continues in each repetition of the sequential stimulation until all populations in the network present PA leading to an increase in the synaptic weights to maximum values.”

2.2. Unsupervisedterm Temporally Asymmetric Hebbianterm Plasticityterm Rule

Let us consider now a fully connected networkterm of n excitatory populations with plastic synapses and global fixed inhibition. The plasticityterm rule for the excitatory-to-excitatory connectivityterm is described by Equation (7). Using this learning ruleterm, with fixed pre- and post-activity, the connectivityterm tends asymptotically to a separable function of the pre- and post-synaptic activity. The functions f ( r ) and g ( r ) are bounded by zero for small or negative values of the populationterm synaptic current, and by one for large values (see Figures 4A,B ). This learning ruleterm is a generalization of classic Hebbianterm rules like the covariance rule (Dayan and Abbott, 2001 ), with a non-linear dependence on both pre and post-synaptic firing rates.

The delay D in the learning ruleterm leads to a temporal asymmetry (Blum and Abbott, 1996 ; Gerstner and Abbott, 1997 ; Veliz-Cuba et al., 2015 ). This delay describes the time it takes for calcium influx through NMDA receptors to reach its maximum (Sabatini et al., 2002 ; Graupner and BrunelpersName, 2012 ). When this learning ruleterm operates and the networkterm is externally stimulated, the connectivityterm changes depending on the interaction of the input, the networkterm dynamics and the learning ruleterm. Due to the relaxational nature of Equation (7), for long times without external stimulationterm the connectivityterm matrix will converge to a stationary rank-1 matrix with entries of the form f ( r i * ) g ( r j * ) , where r → * = ϕ ( u → * ) is the stationary firing rateterm vector, independent of all inputs presented in the past. Therefore, stimuli learned in the connectivityterm matrix will be erased by the background activity of the networkterm for long times after stimulationterm. To prevent this inherent forgetting nature of the learning ruleterm we introduce an activity-dependent plasticityterm time scale in Equation (10). Thus, when pre and/or post-synaptic currents are below a plasticityterm thresholdterm r w , the time scale becomes infinite, and therefore no plasticityterm occurs. When both are above r w , then the time constant is given by T w [see Equation (10) and Figure 4 ]. Lastly, the time scale T w of these changes are chosen to be several orderterm of magnitude slower than the populationterm dynamics, consistent with the time it takes (~1 min or more) for plasticityterm to be induced in standard synaptic plasticityterm protocols (see e.g., Markram et al., 1997 ; Bi and Poo, 1998 ; Sjöström et al., 2001 , but see Bittner et al., 2017 ). Importantly, the weight changes are proportional to the difference w max f [ r i ( t )] g [ r j ( t − D )]−𝕎 i, j , which depends on both the pre and post-synaptic firing rates and the current value of the synaptic weightterm (see Equation 7). Typically, when both pre and post-synaptic firing rates are at intermediate values (see the red region in Figure 4C ) the corresponding synapse undergoes LTD (weight strength tend to decrease), and when they are both high (see the yellow region in Figure 4C ) it undergoes LTP (wight strength tend to increase). Therefore, the rule goes from no plasticityterm to LTD and then to LTP when both pre and post-synaptic firing rates increase (see diagonal direction in Figure 4C ), which is reminiscent of results in pairing experiments (Ngezahayo et al., 2000 ).

Our goal is to understand the conditions for a sequential stimulationterm to lead the networkterm dynamics to PAterm or SAterm, depending of the temporal characteristics of the stimulus, when this plasticityterm rule is introduced. Here we consider a simple stimulationterm protocol where each populationterm in the networkterm is stimulated sequentially one populationterm at a time (see Figure 5A ). In this protocol, populationterm 1 is first stimulated for some time T . Then, after an inter-stimulation time Δ, populationterm 2 is stimulated for the same duration T . The other populations are then stimulated one at a time (3, 4, …, n ) using the same protocol. The amplitude of the stimulationterm is fixed such that the maximum of the current elicited in each populationterm leads to a firing rateterm that is greater than the plasticityterm thresholdterm of the learning ruleterm. The time interval between each repetition of the sequenceterm is much longer than T and Δ and any time constant of the networkterm. When the duration of each stimulationterm is larger than the synaptic delay (i.e., D < T ), recurrentterm connections increase, since the Hebbianterm term driving synaptic changes ( f [ r i ( t )] g [ r i ( t − D )], where i is the stimulated populationterm) becomes large after a time D after the onset of the presentation. When the inter-stimulation time is smaller that the synaptic delay (i.e., Δ < D ), then the feedforwardterm connections increase, since the Hebbianterm term driving synaptic changes ( f [ r i +1 ( t )] g [ r i ( t 𢄠 D )]) is large in some initial interval during presentation of stimulus i +1 (see also Herz et al., 1988 ).

This is a consequence of the classic instability observed with Hebbianterm plasticityterm rules, where a positive feedback loop between the increase in synaptic connectivityterm and increase in firing rates leads to an explosive increase in both (Dayan and Abbott, 2001 ). Larger feedforwardterm and recurrentterm connections lead to an increase in number of populations active at the same time during stimulationterm (see Figures 6A,D ) which produce an increase of the overall connectivityterm by the synaptic plasticityterm rule ( Figures 6B,C ). 2.3. Synaptic Normalizationterm

The first mechanism we consider is synaptic normalizationterm. This mechanism is motivated by experimental evidence of conservation of total synaptic weightterm in neurons (Royer and Paré, 2003 ; Bourne and Harris, 2011 ). In our model, we enforce that the sum of the incoming synaptic weights to a given populationterm is fixed throughout the dynamics (see Equation 11 in Methods). This constraint prevents the growth of all the synaptic weights to their maximum value during sequential stimulationterm due to the Hebbianterm plasticityterm, as is described in the previous section. This leads to a heterogeneous dynamics in the synaptic weights where they strongly fluctuate in time during the stimulationterm period (see Figure 7B ). We find that the networkterm does not reach a stable connectivityterm structure, and that the connectivityterm after the stimulationterm markedly depends on the specific moment when stimulationterm ended for a large range of stimulationterm parameters.

Overall, it is unclear whether neural circuits can use the observed complex synaptic dynamics to store retrievable information about the external stimuli. 2.4. Generalized Homeostaticterm Plasticityterm

Homeostaticterm plasticityterm is another potential stabilizationterm mechanism that has been characterized extensively in experiments (Turrigiano et al., 1998 ; Turrigiano, 2017 ). The interplay between homeostaticterm plasticityterm and Hebbianterm plasticityterm has recently been the focus of multiple theoretical studies (Renart et al., 2003 ; Toyoizumi et al., 2014 ; Keck et al., 2017 ). Here, we study the effect of a generalization of the standard multiplicative homeostaticterm plasticityterm and Hebbianterm plasticityterm for learning SAterm and PAterm. We consider a model for homeostaticterm plasticityterm in which the overall connectivityterm at each time W i, j ( t ) is given by the multiplication of two synaptic variables with different time scales as is shown in Equation (12). In this equation, the fast plastic variable 𝕎 i, j ( t ) (time scale of seconds) is governed by Hebbianterm plasticityterm, see Equation (7). On the other hand, the slow (with a time scale of tens to hundred of seconds) homeostaticterm variable H i ( t ) scales the incoming weights to populationterm i , ensuring that the networkterm maintain low average firing rates on long time scales. The dynamics of the homeostaticterm variable is given by Equation (13). This is a generalization of the standard homeostaticterm learning ruleterm (Renart et al., 2003 ; Toyoizumi et al., 2014 ), that does not include the quadratic term in the r.h.s. of Equation (13). We term this rule generalized homeostaticterm plasticityterm since is a non-linear generalization of the standard multiplicative homeostaticterm plasticityterm rule in Renart et al. ( 2003 ) and Toyoizumi et al. ( 2014 ). The equation proposed in Toyoizumi et al. ( 2014 ) stabilizes the networkterm's activity during stimulationterm, preventing the runawayterm of the firing rates and synaptic weights. Scaling down the overall connectivityterm during stimulationterm prevents co-activation of multiple populations, and lead to stable learning. However, in the networkterm's steady stateterm (i.e., when times longer than the time scale of the homeostaticterm variable have passed without any stimulationterm), if the equation proposed in Toyoizumi et al. ( 2014 ) is used, then each connection will be proportional to the factor ϕ - 1 ( r 0 ) r 0 multiplied by a number of orderterm one (see section 5 in the Supplementary Material for the mathematical details). This implies that the steady stateterm connectivityterm after learning will depend sensitively on the choice of the value of the objective background firing rateterm (i.e., r 0 ) and the specific functional form of the transfer functionterm (i.e., ϕ( u )). Due to the transfer functionterm non-linearity, small changes in r 0 might produce large values for the factor ϕ - 1 ( r 0 ) r 0 and therefore very strong connections for the steady stateterm connectivityterm. This is due to the fact that steady stateterm large values in the homeostaticterm variable H scale up the connectivityterm learned via Hebbianterm plasticityterm in a multiplicative fashion, see Equation (12). In practice, PAterm is retrieved almost always independently of the type of stimulationterm presented during learning, and in the absence of the quadratic term in Equation (13) no temporal attractorterm other than PAterm can be learned. Note that with this quadratic term, the homeostaticterm plasticityterm rule does not exactly achieve a given target firing rateterm, and therefore is not strictly speaking “homeostaticterm.” Additionally, the timescaleterm of this rule is of the orderterm of minutes (see Figure 8A ), which is an orderterm of magnitude faster of what has been reported experimentally (Turrigiano et al., 1998 ; Turrigiano, 2008 , 2017 ) (see section 3.3 for a discussion).

2.5. Learning and Retrievalterm Is Robust to Noise

Under in vivo conditions neural systems operate with large amount of variability in their inputs. In orderterm to assess the effect of highly variable synaptic input current during learning and retrievalterm, we add a mean zero uncorrelated white noise to the dynamics when both Hebbianterm learning and generalized homeostaticterm plasticityterm are included in the networkterm, as described in Equation (14). We found that both the synaptic weights dynamics during learning and the retrieved spatiotemporal dynamics after learning are robust to noise (see Figure 11 ), even when the amplitude of the noise is large (i.e., inputs with values equal to the standard deviation of the noise lead to a populationterm to fire at 30% of the maximum firing rateterm). During sequential stimulationterm, the learning dynamics is marginally altered for both weak and strong inhibition (compare Figure 11 with Figure 10 ). Importantly, the synaptic weights reach very similar stationary values compared with the case without noise. After learning, even though the rates stochastically fluctuate in time, the retrieved spatiotemporal attractors (i.e., PAterm, SAterm, dSA, or PAterm/SAterm) are qualitatively similar as in the case without noise (compare Figure 11 with Figure 10 ). One qualitative difference in the case with external noise, is that in both SAterm and PAterm/SAterm dynamical regimes random inputs lead to a repetition of the full or partial learned sequenceterm. Altogether, this simulations show that the networkterm can robustly learn and retrieve qualitatively the same spatiotemporal attractors in the presence of external noise.

3. Discussion

We have shown that under sequential stimulationterm a networkterm with biologically plausible plasticityterm rules can learn both PAterm or SAterm depending on the stimulus parameters. Two plasticityterm mechanisms are needed: (1) Hebbianterm plasticityterm with temporal asymmetry; (2) a stabilizationterm mechanism which prevents the runawayterm of synaptic weights while learning. When unsupervisedterm Hebbianterm plasticityterm is present alone the networkterm fails to stably learn PAterm or SAterm, while including a generalized homeostaticterm plasticityterm stabilizes learning. For stable learning, we show that the learning process is described by a low dimensional autonomous dynamical system in the space of connectivities, leading to a simplified description of unsupervisedterm learning of PAterm and SAterm by the networkterm from external stimuli. Depending on the stimulus parameters, the networkterm is flexible enough to learn selectively both types of activity by repeated exposure to a sequenceterm of stimuli, without need for supervision. This suggests that cortical circuits endowed with a single learning ruleterm can learn qualitatively different neural dynamics (i.e., persistent vs. sequential activityterm) depending on the stimuli statisticsterm.

Using the full characterization of the bifurcationterm·mth diagram in the space of fixed feedforwardterm and recurrentterm connections developed here, we mapped the evolution of the connectivityterm during stimulationterm in the bifurcationterm·mth diagram. We analytically and numerically showed that the synaptic weights evolve in the feedforwardterm—recurrentterm synaptic connections space until they reach their steady stateterm (when the number of sequential stimulations is large). The specific point of the steady stateterm in the bifurcationterm·mth diagram depends solely on the stimulationterm parameters—stimulationterm period T and time delay Δ—and the connectivityterm initial conditions. We found that stimulations with long durations and large delays generically leads to the formation of PAterm, whereas stimulations with long enough durations and short delays leads to the formation of SAterm. Thus, persistent stimulationterm leads to persistent activityterm while sequential stimulationterm leads to sequential activityterm.

3.1. Learning of Sequences in Networks

A growing number of networkterm models have been shown to be able to learn sequential activityterm. Models with supervisedterm learning can reproduce perfectly target sequences through minimization of a suitable error function (Sussillo and Abbott, 2009 ; Laje and Buonomano, 2013 ; Memmesheimer et al., 2014 ; Rajan et al., 2016 ), but the corresponding learning rules are not biophysically realistic.

Other investigators have studied how unsupervisedterm learning rules leads to sequenceterm generation. Early models of networks of binary neurons showed how various prescriptions for incorporating input sequences in the connectivityterm matrix can lead to sequenceterm generation (see Kuhn and van Hemmen, 1991 )—or, sometimes, both sequenceterm generation or fixed point attractors depending on the inputs (Herz et al., 1988 ). The drawback of these models is that they separated a learning phase in which recurrentterm dynamics was shut down in orderterm to form the synaptic connectivityterm matrix, and a retrievalterm phase in which the connectivityterm matrix does not change anymore.

Our model removes this artificial separation, since both plasticityterm rule and recurrentterm dynamics operate continuously, both during learning and recall. However, we found that there needs to be a mechanism to attenuate recurrentterm dynamics during learning for it to be stable. The mechanism we propose rely on a modified version of a standard homeostaticterm rule. Other mechanisms have been proposed, such as neuromodulators that would change the balance between recurrentterm and external inputs during presentation of behaviorally relevant stimuli (Hasselmo, 2006 ).

The cost of not having supervision is that the networkterm can only learn the temporal orderterm of the presented stimuli, but not their precise timingterm. Veliz-Cuba et al. ( 2015 ) have recently provided a model which bear strong similarities with our model (rate modelterm·mth with unsupervisedterm temporally asymmetric Hebbianterm plasticityterm rule), but includes in addition a short-term facilitation mechanism that allows the networkterm to learn both orderterm and precise timingterm of a sequenceterm presented in input. However, their mechanisms require parameters to be precisely related.

Models with temporally asymmetric Hebbianterm plasticityterm have also been investigated in the context of the hippocampus (Abbott and Blum, 1996 ; Gerstner and Abbott, 1997 ; Mehta et al., 1997 ; Jahnke et al., 2015 ; Chenkov et al., 2017 ; Theodoni et al., 2018 ). In such models, feedforwardterm connectivityterm is learned through multiple visits of neighboring place fields, and sequential activityterm (“replays”) can be triggered using appropriate inputs mimicking sharp-wave ripples. Other models use unsupervisedterm Hebbianterm plasticityterm but qualitatively distinct mechanisms to generate sequential activityterm. In particular, several studies showed that sequences can be generated spontaneously from unstructured input noise (Fiete et al., 2010 ; Okubo et al., 2015 ). Murray and Escola ( 2017 ) showed that sequences can be generated in networks of inhibitoryterm neurons with anti-Hebbian plasticityterm, and proposed that this mechanism is at work in the striatum.

3.2. Learning of Persistent Activityterm With Unsupervisedterm Hebbianterm Plasticityterm

In our model, unsupervisedterm temporally asymmetric Hebbianterm plasticityterm is unstable, and after a number of stimulations, all synaptic weights increase to the maximum value. The destabilization occurs during sequential stimulationterm when the populationterm previously stimulated in the sequenceterm presents persistent activityterm at the moment the next populationterm is being stimulated. This leads to the increase of the feedforwardterm synaptic weightterm between these two populations, producing that both present PAterm while the next populationterm in the sequenceterm is being stimulated. This process continues in each repetition of the sequential stimulationterm until all populations in the networkterm present PAterm leading to an increase in the synaptic weights to maximum values. However, previous studies have shown that PAterm could be learned without additional stabilizationterm mechanisms. In Del Giudice and Mattia ( 2001 ), Del Giudice et al. ( 2003 ), and Mongillo et al. ( 2005 ) PAterm is learnt without any stabilizing mechanism except for short-term depression. These studies have a number of differences with ours, however, we believe the key difference for stable learning PAterm is that in these models there is PAterm (in the absence of inputs to the networkterm) below the thresholdterm for LTP induction - something is not currently present in our model since PAterm is always at the maximal rate. Therefore, unlike our model, PAterm after stimulationterm does not lead to effective changes in the synaptic connections. Interestingly, they introduced short term depression to stabilize learning by rapidly shutting down high firing rates during stimulationterm or PAterm, which resembles the effect of the generalized homeostatic plasticity ruleterm·def in our model. In Mongillo et al. ( 2003 ), connectivities with stable recurrentterm, feedforwardterm and feedback connections between pairs of populations emerge from unsupervisedterm Hebbianterm learning without any stabilizationterm mechanism. After learning, when one populationterm in the pair presents PAterm the other transition to PAterm stochastically after a period of time due to spike noise. Importantly, as in the previous models discussed above, the learning thresholds are set in such a way that there is no learning when both populations present PAterm. Additionally, during learning the presentations of stimuli are far apart, and unlike our model learning feedback and feedforwardterm connections are due to persistent activityterm in one neuron, and the stimulationterm in the other, followed by a small increase in the synaptic strengths in each presentation. Lastly, another difference with the models above, and with classical models for persistent delay activity (Amit and BrunelpersName, 1997 ), is that in our model the “background state” is at zero activity. This is an unrealistic feature stemming from the simplified piecewise linear transfer functionterm used in our networkterm (see Equation 5).

3.3. Stabilizationterm Mechanisms

Consistent with many previous studies (Dayan and Abbott, 2001 ), we have shown that a networkterm with unsupervisedterm Hebbianterm plasticityterm under sequential stimulationterm leads to a runawayterm of the synaptic weights. This instability is due to a positive feed-back loop generated by the progressive increase of networkterm activity leading to a progressive increase in average synaptic strength when PAterm or SAterm are being learned. One possible solution for this problem was first proposed in the context of attractorterm neural networkterm models (Amit et al., 1985 ; Tsodyks and Feigel'Man, 1988 ; Amit and Fusi, 1994 ). In these models, patterns are learned upon presentation during a learning phase where synapses are plastic but there is no ongoing networkterm dynamics. After the learning phase , the learning of attractors is tested in a retrievalterm phase , where the networkterm dynamics is ongoing but synaptic plasticityterm is not present. Therefore, by compartmentalizing in time dynamics and learning, the networkterm dynamics does not lead to changes in the synaptic weights during retrievalterm, and conversely, changes in synaptic weights do not lead to changes in the dynamics during learning. This separation prevents the observed runawayterm of the synaptic weights due to unsupervisedterm Hebbianterm plasticityterm.

However, it is unclear whether such compartmentalization exists in cortical networks. In this work, we explored the alternative scenario, in which both plasticityterm and dynamics happen concurrently during learning and retrievalterm (see also Mongillo et al., 2005 ; Litwin-Kumar and Doiron, 2014 ; Zenke et al., 2015 for a similar approach in networks of spiking neurons). First, we found that by adding to unsupervisedterm Hebbianterm plasticityterm an instantaneous synaptic normalizationterm mechanism that maintains constant the sum of incoming synaptic weights to each populationterm, PAterm and SAterm cannot be stably learned. Second, we found that adding a generalized homeostaticterm plasticityterm to unsupervisedterm Hebbianterm plasticityterm leads to stable learning of PAterm and SAterm. During sequential stimulationterm, the increase in co-activation between multiple populations due to recurrentterm and feedforwardterm connections learned via unsupervisedterm Hebbianterm plasticityterm is prevented by suppressing its effect in the networkterm dynamics. Homeostaticterm plasticityterm scales down the overall connectivityterm producing a weakly connected networkterm. PAterm and SAterm is prevented to occur during stimulationterm, which weakens the positive feed-back loop generated by the increase in co-activations of neuronal populations. After learning, the dynamic variables of the Homeostaticterm plasticityterm rule reach a steady stateterm with values similar of what they where before stimulationterm (see Figure 8A ) and the connectivityterm learned via unsupervisedterm Hebbianterm plasticityterm can lead to retrievalterm of PAterm and SAterm upon stimulationterm (see Figure 8C ). The homeostaticterm variable reaches its steady stateterm at a value close to one, and the connectivityterm recovers, unmasking the feedforwardterm and recurrentterm learned architecture. We have also tried other stabilizationterm mechanisms such as inhibitoryterm to excitatory plasticityterm (Vogels et al., 2011 ) instead of homeostaticterm plasticityterm. In this case we found that stable learning of PAterm and SAterm is possible, but for distinct sets of networkterm and stimulationterm parameters (data not shown).

As explained in Zenke and Gerstner ( 2017 ) and Zenke et al. ( 2017 ), in orderterm to prevent the runawayterm of the synaptic weights produced by Hebbianterm plasticityterm, the timescaleterm of any compensatory mechanism should be of the same orderterm or faster than the Hebbianterm timescaleterm. For generalized homeostaticterm plasticityterm, the timescaleterm of the homeostaticterm variable H i is dependent on the firing rateterm of neuron i and the target firing rateterm [i.e., ϕ( u i )/ϕ( u 0 )]. When the networkterm firing rateterm is close to the target firing rateterm the homeostaticterm learning ruleterm is slow, and the homeostaticterm mechanism seldom play a role in the dynamics. On the other hand, for high firing rates the homeostaticterm plasticityterm timescaleterm becomes faster, preventing the runawayterm of the synaptic weights. There is currently an ongoing debate about whether the timescales of compensatory processes used in theoretical studies, as the ones used here, are consistent with experimental evidence (see e.g., Zenke and Gerstner, 2017 ; Zenke et al., 2017 ).
