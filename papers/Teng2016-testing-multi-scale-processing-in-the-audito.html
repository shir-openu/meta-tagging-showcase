Abstract

Natural sounds contain information on multiple timescales, so the auditory systemterm·key must analyze and integrate acoustic information on those different scales to extract behaviorally relevant information. However, this multi-scale process in the auditory systemterm·key is not widely investigated in the literature, and existing models of temporal integrationterm·dom are mainly built upon detection or recognition tasks on a single timescaleterm·dom. Here we use a paradigm requiring processing on relatively ‘localterm·key’ and ‘globalterm·key’ scales and provide evidence suggesting that the auditory systemterm·key extracts fine-detail acoustic information using short temporal windows and uses long temporal windows to abstract globalterm·key acoustic patterns. Behavioral task performance that requires processing fine-detail information does not improve with longer stimulus lengthterm·var, contrary to predictions of previous temporal integrationterm·dom models such as the multiple-looksterm·dom and the spectro-temporal excitation patternterm·mth model. Moreover, the perceptual construction of putatively ‘unitary’ auditory events requires more than hundreds of milliseconds. Natural sounds, music, and vocal sounds have a rich temporal structure over multiple timescales 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , and behaviorally relevant acoustic information is usually carried on more than one timescaleterm·dom. For example, speech conveys linguistic information at several scales: 20–80 ms for phonemic information, 100–300 ms for syllabic information, and more than 1000 ms for intonation information 1 . Bird song, similarly, also includes information on one timescaleterm·dom around 10–30 ms and another one around 500–700 ms 5 . Therefore, auditory processing requires sounds to be integrated temporally at different scales to extract critical perceptual information.

The temporal integrationterm·dom of sound is a fundamental property of hearing. Integrating information over time, as we define it operationally here, serves as a basic information accumulation process in the auditory systemterm·key to guarantee that enough information is supplied for extracting meaningful regularities on a certain timescaleterm·dom. It has been demonstrated in various experiments, using different types of signals, that perceptual thresholds typically decrease as sound duration increases 7 , 8 . Such a trading relationship between sound duration and perceptual performance suggests that the auditory systemterm·key accumulates information over time to get better estimates of auditory signal characteristics. Visual perception, especially in dynamic contexts, also incorporates such temporal integrationterm·dom processes 9 . As natural sounds contain information on multiple timescales, fine-grained (informally speaking, temporally ‘localterm·key’) information usually exists on a shorter timescaleterm·dom and requires a fast integration process, while more ‘globalterm·key’ information occupies a larger timescaleterm·dom and a longer integration window is consequently needed. Therefore, by hypothesis, a single integration process may not suffice for the variety of auditory tasks that the system successfully solves.

The existing literature argues that the auditory systemterm·key works over several distinct time ranges - instead of in a unitary manner across the (intuitively simpler and conceptually appealing) continuum of temporal variation 8 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 . Previous models of temporal integrationterm·dom typically assume leaky integrationterm·dom with a time constant of hundreds of milliseconds, based on studies such as loudness summation 15 , 16 , signal detection in noise 17 , 18 , and temporal integrationterm·dom at threshold 13 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , in which listeners’ performance increases continuously with sound duration and typically achieves a plateau above 200 ms. These models, however, are not compatible with the range of results that demonstrate the high temporal resolution of the auditory systemterm·key, such as in modulation detection 23 , gap detection 24 , and non-simultaneous masking 25 . In those studies, perceptual thresholds from 2–30 ms are the norm. This incompatibility has led to the formulation of the resolution-integration paradoxterm·def 8 , 26 .

To resolve this paradox, Viemeister (1991) proposed a ‘multiple-looksterm·dom’ model: the continuous sound is sampled discretely at a high rate (e.g. with integration windows of ~5 ms), and the samples are stored and concatenated/integrated for further processing. The multiple-looks modelterm·def suggests that temporal integrationterm·dom is a process of multiple timescales instead of one, but the longer timescaleterm·dom is assembled on the basis of concatenated short samples. Moore (2003) further relates this concept to the internal representation of sounds, calculated as spectro-temporal excitation patterns (STEP): the internal representation can be treated as a vector of ‘intelligent looks’ or samples of auditory stimuli, with some ‘looks’ weighted more than others for different tasks. Perceptual tasks can be implemented on this type of internal representation 27 .

Recently, based on the results of speech perception experiments as well as neurophysiological studies, one line of argumentation proposes that there are two main temporal windows working concurrently in the auditory systemterm·key 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 : one temporal windowterm·dom, of ~30 ms or less in which acoustic structure is parsed and analyzed with relatively high temporal resolution; and another temporal windowterm·dom of ~200 ms in which acoustic information is extracted at a more globalterm·key scale. Although such a ‘two-timescale integration model’ has a specific physiological constraint on the size of temporal windows, it is supported by a growing body of neural evidence 30 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 . Its core idea builds on the multiple-looks modelterm·def and STEP, which is that temporal integrationterm·dom involves a process of sampling or integrating information discretely on a small timescaleterm·dom (e.g, tens of milliseconds) and on a large timescaleterm·dom (e.g. hundreds of milliseconds), instead of simply integrating information continuously, as suggested by the leaky-integration model.

Although the two-temporal-windows integration model (2TWI) shares some basic features with the multiple-looksterm·dom and STEP models, there are significant differences, as well. For example, 2TWI model suggests that the auditory systemterm·key may process information differently on different scales 32 . The processing may be modulated by acoustic properties on different timescales or by task requirements so that extraction and representation of acoustic information is more efficient. This leads to predictions different from the ones derived from the multiple-looksterm·dom and STEP models. For example, in a task comparing localterm·key details of sounds, the multiple-looks modelterm·def and STEP would predict that, as stimulus lengthterm·var increases and hence more localterm·key details are available, the auditory systemterm·key would form more distinct representations. Therefore, the performance, e.g. of differentiating two sounds, should increase with stimulus lengthterm·var. In contrast, the 2TWI model suggests that increased stimulus lengthterm·var may not necessarily increase performance, because representation and processing of localterm·key details might function best only at a time range of tens of milliseconds 29 , 32 . On the other hand, the 2TWI model further suggests that representation for globalterm·key scales is best only for a time range in the hundreds of milliseconds, while the multiple-looksterm·dom and STEP models would predict the perception of globalterm·key information increases with increased number of ‘samples’ or ‘looks’ of information.

Here, to explore mechanisms of temporal integrationterm·dom and test certain predictions of the different integration models, we investigated in a series of four psychophysical experiments the perceptual performance at shorter and longer scales by manipulating the timescaleterm·dom of acoustic information while biasing the auditory processes implementing localterm·key or globalterm·key task demands. Our approach is conceptually analogous to previous local-global studies 39 , 40 . In Experiment 1A, a same-different task is employed to test how various localterm·key and globalterm·key scales affect participants’ performance on abstracting fine-grained, ‘localterm·key’ spectrotemporal information as well as more ‘globalterm·key’ acoustic patterns. In Experiment 1B, by holding fixed the localterm·key scale, we further test how stimulus lengthterm·var modulates participants’ ability to extract fine-detailed and globalterm·key information. Experiment 2 assesses whether the frequency difference at the onset or offset between two stimuli can affect the findings. In Experiment 3, we first measure how much time the auditory systemterm·key needs to effectively process particular acoustic details (Experiment 3A) and then test whether the integration process functions best over a limited time (Experiment 3B). In Experiment 4, by varying intervals between two stimuli, we ask whether memory factors may affect the results, as the results of the same-different task may be modulated by memory requirements. Analysis Procedures

Responses were transformed into the sensitivity index, d prime, and criterion, denoted as Cterm·stat, as in a simple yes-no paradigm using the Palamedes toolbox 44 .

In Experiments 1, 3 and 4, we used a same-different paradigm. Correct responses to ‘different’ trials were marked as hit and incorrect responses to ‘same’ trials as false alarm. Participants in our experiments may adapt a “differencing” strategy as in the same-different task, since there is no obvious perceptual token in our stimuli as well as stimuli of different segment durations and stimulus lengths were grouped and presented randomly in each task. However, without assuming complex behavior models, treating responses in the same-different paradigm as from a yes-no paradigm can appropriately capture the sensitivity of participants and the behavioral effects caused by experimental modulations, which is the main focus of our study. When a participant responded correctly to all ‘same’ or ‘different’ trials within a block, a half artificial incorrect same or different trial was added to the block 45 . Therefore, the maximum d-prime value for all correct responses is 4.482 (instead of infinity) and the minimum d-prime value is 0.

In Experiment 2, we used a two-interval two-alternative-forced choice paradigm and d prime valuesterm·stat were computed accordingly.

Increasing stimulus lengthterm·var does therefore not necessarily enhance perceptual performance on localterm·key processing tasks. Method

Stimuli and apparatus

In Experiment 1A, two factors, stimulus lengthterm·var and segment durationterm·var , were manipulated. We varied both segment durationterm·var and stimulus lengthterm·var and chose specific combinations of number of segments and segment durationterm·var to fix overall stimulus lengthterm·var at around 90, 150, 220 and 300 ms. We confined the maximum stimulus lengthterm·var to be 300 ms because, as we observed in pilot data, participants’ performance achieved a ceiling effect or floor effect when stimulus lengthterm·var exceeded 300 ms. The minimum number of segments within a stimulus was set at 3, and the duration of segments was varied at 15, 20, 30, 50, 75 and 100 ms. Therefore, for stimuli of ~90 ms total duration, three segment durations were included, 15, 20 and 30 ms; for stimuli of around 150 ms total duration, 15, 20, 30 and 50 ms segments were included; for stimuli of around 220 ms, 15, 20, 30, 50, and 75 ms; and for stimuli of 300 ms, 15, 20, 30, 50, 75 and 100 ms.

Two tasks, a local taskterm·def and a globalterm·key task , were run in Experiment 1A ( Fig. 1a ). On each trial, a pair of stimuli was presented. For both tasks, we generated ‘same’ and ‘different’ pairs. In the local taskterm·def , the sweep directions of each corresponding segment were the same between two stimuli in the ‘same’ pair, whereas the sweep directions of each corresponding segment in the ‘different’ pair were opposite. In the globalterm·key task , the sweep directions of corresponding segments between two stimuli were always different because we wanted to prevent participants from making judgments based on acoustic details. In the ‘same’ pair, both stimuli had a mean frequency either increasing from 1300 Hz to 1700 Hz over the stimulus duration or decreasing from 1700 Hz to 1300 Hz; in the ‘different’ pair, one stimulus had a mean frequency increasing from 1300 Hz to 1700 Hz over the stimulus lengthterm·var, and the other one had a mean frequency decreasing from 1700 Hz to 1300 Hz.

In Experiment 1B, segment durationterm·var was fixed at 30 ms and the factor ‘stimulus lengthterm·var’ (equivalent to the number of segments) was manipulated in each condition, as Experiment 1A showed that overall stimulus lengthterm·var markedly affected performance of the local taskterm·def at this segment durationterm·var. We used the same manipulations as in Experiment 1A: in the local taskterm·def , we generated ‘same’ and ‘different’ pairs of stimuli with varying stimulus lengthterm·var: 30, 90, 150, 210, 270, and 330 ms. In the globalterm·key task , we only selected stimulus lengthterm·var: 90, 150, 210, 270, and 330 ms, because we could not manipulate globalterm·key pattern when there was only one segment. The sweep directions of corresponding segments between two stimuli were always the same in the globalterm·key task (different from Experiment 1A). In the ‘same’ pair, both stimuli had a mean frequency increasing with the number of segments from 1300 Hz to 1700 Hz, or had a mean frequency decreasing with the number of segments from 1700 Hz to 1300 Hz; in the ‘different’ pair, one stimulus had a mean frequency increasing with the number of segments from 1300 Hz to 1700 Hz, and the other one had a mean frequency decreasing from 1700 Hz to 1300 Hz. Because we wanted to examine how acoustic information is integrated over time to build up a globalterm·key pattern, we made the mean frequency increase with the stimulus lengthterm·var instead of fixing the range of mean frequency for each stimulus, as we did in Experiment 1A.

Procedure and design

The experiment employed a same-different task. On each trial, two stimuli were presented sequentially, with an inter-stimulus interval uniformly distributed from 400 to 600 ms. Participants were prompted to make a response 100 ms after the second interval. The local taskterm·def was to judge whether two stimuli were the same or not. In the globalterm·key task , the participants were asked to judge whether the directions of the mean frequency shift were the same between two stimuli. The localterm·key and globalterm·key tasks were presented in two separate blocks, and trials within a block were presented in a pseudorandom order. The order of blocks was counterbalanced across participants. In each block, for trials with the same number of segments, there were 40 same trials and 40 different trials for each condition. In Experiment 1A, 1600 trials in total were presented in the local taskterm·def and the same number in the globalterm·key task . In Experiment 1B, 480 trials in total were presented in the local taskterm·def and 400 trials in the globalterm·key task .

Before the formal experiment, three trials for each stimulus lengthterm·var and segment durationterm·var in each task were presented (with feedback) to familiarize the participants with the stimuli and tasks.

The performance on the local taskterm·def shows that differentiating sounds based on detailed, localterm·key spectrotemporal information does not increase with increasing stimulus lengthterm·var. According to the multiple-looksterm·dom and STEP models, longer stimuli provide a larger amount of acoustic information for the auditory systemterm·key to draw more samples or looks. The auditory systemterm·key should form more distinct representations of stimuli and lead to better performance. However, the results in both Experiments 1A and 1B suggest that the auditory systemterm·key fails to (be able to) utilize the extra information provided in the longer stimuli, since performance did not increase. The performance of the local taskterm·def remained at the lowest level across different stimulus lengths when segment durationterm·var was short (15 or 20 ms). One possible explanation for the floor effect is that the auditory systemterm·key cannot resolve detailed information at such short localterm·key scales. A ceiling effect may also explain that stimulus lengthterm·var has no effect when segment durations are 50, 75 or 100 ms. At those scales, the listeners can resolve the detailed spectrotemporal information and perceive the sweep directions of the segments. Therefore, the effect of increased stimulus lengthterm·var cannot be observed probably because of a ceiling effect. In contrast, when segment durationterm·var was 30 ms, stimulus lengthterm·var exerted a clear negative influence on the local taskterm·def performance, as shown by the main effect of stimulus lengthterm·var in Experiment 1A and again in Experiment 1B. Furthermore, in Experiment 1B, the criterion in the local taskterm·def increased with stimulus lengthterm·var, which indicates that subjects became worse at analyzing localterm·key details and tended to consider two sounds in the ‘different’ pair to be the same. Therefore, these results suggest a more complicated temporal integrationterm·dom process in the auditory systemterm·key, one that might be consistent with the 2TWI model.

In Experiment 1A, the scale of tens of milliseconds seems to apply to perception of fine-grained information in the local taskterm·def . Performance is poor when segment durationterm·var is only 15 ms (mean of d prime value below 1) and becomes very good (and then levels off) when segment durationterm·var is 50 ms (mean d prime value greater than 3). To generate a good estimation of localterm·key acoustic details, the auditory systemterm·key may need to integrate information over tens of milliseconds. The range around 30–50 ms seems to be a lower bound for a timescaleterm·dom that facilitates access to such detailed information. These results are consistent with the fact that speech perception requires fine-detailed decoding of phonemic information, which also falls on a scale of tens of milliseconds 1 .

On the other hand, the globalterm·key task shows the reverse pattern, which is predicted by the multiple-looksterm·dom and STEP models: participants’ performance on the globalterm·key task increases with increasing stimulus duration, arguably because stimuli of longer duration provide more acoustic information, and a globalterm·key pattern can be better recognized as more information is integrated. In contrast, longer segment durationterm·var causes worse performance in the globalterm·key task . When the localterm·key scale increases, each stimulus contains fewer segments. Therefore, less information is available for extracting a globalterm·key pattern.

The results of Experiment 1 are consistent with recent findings by McDermott et al . 46 and Piazza et al . 47 , who also showed that increased stimulus lengthterm·var helps the auditory systemterm·key perceive globalterm·key statistical patterns but can impede access to localterm·key acoustic detail.

Experiment 2

In Experiments 1A and 1B, participants could in principle use a strategy of monitoring the spectral change of a single segment between two stimuli to respond in the globalterm·key task . That is, subjects could make their responses based on the frequency difference between the onsets or the offsets of stimuli. To address this potential confound, in Experiment 2 we kept the frequency difference between two stimuli at the onset and the offset the same and made all segments different between two stimuli. Only a segment durationterm·var of 30 ms is only used in this experiment (as well as in Experiments 3 and 4), because previous behavioral studies 48 found that frequency modulation sweeps of 30 ms ensure good perception of sweep direction and, therefore, good access to localterm·key details. Furthermore, as shown in Experiment 1A, at the segment durationterm·var of 30 ms, participants’ performance did not reveal floor or ceiling effects, so a modulatory effect of stimulus lengthterm·var could be well observed.

Method

Stimuli and apparatus

We generated two groups of stimulus pairs, with 3, 5, 7, 9 and 11 segments. The factor segment number (similar to stimulus lengthterm·var because we fixed the segment durationterm·var at 30 ms) was manipulated in two conditions. In condition 1 , each pair of stimuli included one stimulus with mean frequency shifting from 1300 Hz to 1700 Hz, regardless of the number of segments, and the other stimulus with mean frequency constant at 1500 Hz ( Fig. 2a ). In condition 2 , each pair of stimuli included one stimulus with mean frequency shifting from 1700 Hz to 1300 Hz and the other stimulus with mean frequency kept at 1500 Hz ( Fig. 2a ). The stimuli within a pair always had segments of opposite sweep directions. The frequency difference between two stimuli at the onset was the same as the frequency difference at the offset, so the participants could not rely on spectral cues to judge the trend of the frequency shift. Furthermore, because each segment swept in different directions, participants could not monitor the spectral change of a single segment between two stimuli to generate a response.

Experiment 3

In the local taskterm·def , longer stimulus lengthterm·var did not increase participants’ performance. One explanation is that more segments require more processing time to represent localterm·key details. Within a short period of time, the acoustic details of only several segments can be fully accessed. When the amount of information extends beyond the capacity of processing within a certain time window, the auditory systemterm·key ‘summarizes’ the localterm·key details and forms a representation of a globalterm·key pattern. Another explanation is that increased stimulus lengthterm·var increases the amount of information, namely the number of segments, carried in the working memory. The increased working memory loadterm·var may potentially degraded participants’ performance in the local taskterm·def. In this experiment, we keep the number of segments constant while inserting intervals between segments, to assess whether localterm·key details can be recovered by providing more processing time. If the working memory loadterm·var plays a key role here, inserting intervals while keeping the memory load consistent will not change the participants’ performance.

We hypothesize that acoustic information can only be integrated within a limited temporal range. By keeping constant the information provided by stimuli (the number of segments) while increasing stimulus lengthterm·var by inserting gaps between segments, we can test whether the auditory systemterm·key can store all segments and integrate acoustic details over a longer time range. If the acoustic information can be integrated without a time constraint, the stretched stimuli will not affect the performance in the globalterm·key task . Otherwise, the performance will decrease as the size of intervals increases.

In Experiment 3A, we examine the amount of additional time the auditory systemterm·key needs to recover fine-detailed information. In Experiment 3B, we ask whether temporal integrationterm·dom functions best on a time scale of hundreds of milliseconds. We fix both the number of segments and segment durationterm·var while manipulating overall stimulus lengthterm·var by inserting gaps between segments.

A large IOI may make the first and the last segments more salient than middle segments, because no segment precedes the first segment and no segment follows the last segment. Participants may just pay attention to the first or the last segment to do the tasks instead of resolving acoustic details of the whole stimulus. To avoid this potential confound, the sweep directions of the segments in the beginning and the end of the stimuli were the same between two stimuli in both ‘same’ and ‘different’ pairs; the segments in the middle of the stimuli were the same in the ‘same’ pair, and different in the ‘different’ pair.

In Experiment 3B, stimuli with 5 and 7 segments were used. The same six levels were used for the factor IOI. The stimuli within both ‘same’ and ‘different’ pairs always had segments of opposite sweep directions. For ‘same’ pairs, regardless of the number of segments, the stimuli either had a mean frequency increasing with the number of segments from 1300 Hz to 1700 Hz or a mean frequency decreasing from 1700 Hz to 1300 Hz. For ‘different’ pairs, the stimuli had opposite directions of frequency shifting trend.

Procedure and design

Stimuli with different number of segments were presented in separate blocks, and in each block the order of IOIs was randomized. The order of the blocks was counterbalanced across participants. For each IOI, the stimuli were the same for 30 trials, and different for 30 trials. In total, 720 trials were presented for Experiment 3A and Experiment 3B, respectively. Around ten trials for each number of segments at each IOI were presented with feedback for training purposes before the experiment.

The results from Experiment 3A suggest that the auditory systemterm·key requires time to process and represent fine-grained information. The intervals between segments and, of course, the duration of segments are both important for extracting fine-grained information from the stimuli. Similar results have been found in a speech perception study 49 in which inserting intervals into compressed speech increased intelligibility. Contrary to the results of Experiment 1B, it seems that participants in this experiment are better at the local taskterm·def using 5-segment stimuli than 3-segment stimuli. We suspect that the discrepancy is caused by the fact that we fixed the first and the last segments in Experiment 3A, so differences between two stimuli within the ‘different’ pair are much smaller in 3-segment stimuli (only one segment is different) than in 5-segment stimuli (three segments are different).

The results from Experiment 3A also demonstrate that the working memory loadterm·var cannot explain the effect of stimulus lengthterm·var in the local taskterm·def of Experiment 1. By inserting intervals into the stimuli, we kept the working memory loadterm·var constant and stretched the stimuli to make the task more memory demanding, but the participants’ performance became better rather than worse. Therefore, the effect of stimulus lengthterm·var is related more to properties of auditory processing than to effects of the working memory loadterm·var.

Importantly, in Experiment 3B, the ability to recognize the globalterm·key sound pattern decreases as IOI increases. Grouping acoustic details and abstracting a globalterm·key pattern seem only to work well within a particular, restricted time range. Only if the necessary information falls within a short time range (e.g. hundreds of milliseconds), can the auditory systemterm·key integrate acoustic information and abstract the globalterm·key trend. Otherwise, the stimuli start to sound like a sequence of individual acoustic segments instead of a coherent, integrated sound pattern, so localterm·key details become salient and could potentially affect the integration process.

Experiment 4

In the local taskterm·def of Experiment 1, the same-different judgment of two stimuli based on fine-grained information may be influenced by memory constraints. That is, when we varied stimulus lengthterm·var, this lengthened the amount of time participants needed to remember information about the first stimulus in the stimulus pair. If memory degraded during that time, this may have been one factor contributing to the effect of stimulus lengthterm·var. To examine this hypothesis, the interval between two stimuli within a pair was varied in Experiment 4. If memory plays an important role in the local taskterm·def of Experiments 1A and 1B, performance should decrease as the interval between two stimuli increases.

Method

Stimuli and apparatus

The factors, segment number (i.e. overall stimulus lengthterm·var) and inter-stimulus interval (ISI)term·var, were manipulated. We generated stimulus pairs with 1, 3, 5, and 11 segments. ISIs were varied across four levels: 500, 700, 1000 and 1500 ms.

Procedure and design

The procedure was the same as in the local taskterm·def of Experiment 1. Pairs of stimuli with different segments and ISIs were pseudo-randomly presented. All stimuli were presented in one block. For each number of segments and each ISI, 30 trials were presented, for a total of 960 trials. Around ten trials for each number of segments at each ISI were presented with feedback for training purposes before the formal experiment.

Results and discussion

Figure 4 illustrates that d prime value s did not change across different ISIs for a given stimulus lengthterm·var. A two-way repeated measures ANOVAterm·stat shows that the main effect was significant for number of segments F (3,27) = 94.51, p < 0.001, but not for ISIs F (3,27) = 0.06, p = 0.982. Although memory requirements may come into play in our experiment due to the characteristics of the same-different procedure, the results of this control experiment demonstrate that memory cannot explain the main results obtained in the previous experiments.

General Discussion

We report the findings from four behavioral studies designed to investigate how auditory information on different timescales is integrated. Experiment 1 showed that the auditory systemterm·key can extract fine-temporal-detail information as well as integrate acoustic chunks to form a globalterm·key percept. However, longer stimuli only improved integration in a globalterm·key task , but did not increase performance on extracting fine-grained information in a local taskterm·def . Experiment 2 suggested that potential frequency difference cues between stimuli did not affect the results of temporal integrationterm·dom obtained in Experiment 1. Experiment 3A showed that decoding and representing the detailed acoustic information required processing time. Experiment 3B suggested that temporal integrationterm·dom functions best within a limited time range. Experiment 4, finally, controlled for the possibility that memory factors affected the conclusions of Experiment 1.

How the auditory systemterm·key extracts fine-grained information and integrates acoustic information over time remains a key issue for auditory processing. Recent studies by McDermott et al . 46 , 51 and Piazza et al . 47 argue that the auditory systemterm·key uses a form of statistical summation to integrate acoustic information over time to generate auditory percepts, but the temporal range of putative statistical summation processes in these studies was not clearly articulated. Durations of the stimuli used in McDermott et al . 46 range from 40 ms to more than 2 seconds, and results similar to our findings were found: when discrimination depends on the details of the stimuli, performance decreases with sound duration; when discrimination depends on the long-term statistics of the stimuli, performance increases with sound duration. In Piazza et al . 47 , the sound duration used is more than two seconds, which is a different time range than that used in the present study. In both studies, short-term memory may be involved in the tasks, so the summation process cannot be defined with certainty as a characteristic of the auditory systemterm·key independent from additional memory factors.

In Experiment 1 of the present study, the stimulus lengthterm·var was varied from 30 ms to 330 ms. The data from Experiment 1A demonstrated that access to the fine-grained information of the stimuli improves as segment durationterm·var increased over tens of milliseconds - but deteriorates with increased stimulus lengthterm·var. The results of Experiment 1B showed that the representation of the globalterm·key pattern increased to about 200 ms. These results are consistent with the psychophysical studies that reported two time constants in the auditory systemterm·key 8 . Moreover, these results suggest that auditory processing is different on these two timescales. On the timescaleterm·dom of hundreds of milliseconds, acoustic information can be integrated and well represented while the representation of fine-grained information is compromised; on the timescaleterm·dom of tens of milliseconds, the acoustic information is processed but arguably not ‘fully’ represented to subserve subsequent perceptual analysis.

The results cannot be explained in a straightforward manner by models assuming a standard process such as a leaky integrator, because the stimuli in the present study have a multiplexed temporal structure, and the task does not require integration of either the envelope or the sound energy. Although the multiple-looks modelterm·def implies that the auditory systemterm·key samples acoustic information on a short timescaleterm·dom, the details of the sampling process are not specified. Furthermore, as stimulus lengthterm·var increases, there should be more opportunities for sampling acoustic information. However, we find that the performance of discrimination between two stimuli with different fine-grained information decreases, which is inconsistent with the prediction of a multiple-looksterm·dom model and a STEP model. The findings of the present experiments suggest a more complicated temporal integrationterm·dom process in the auditory systemterm·key: the acoustic segments are analyzed within a short temporal windowterm·dom for extracting localterm·key details and concurrently acoustic information is integrated within a larger temporal windowterm·dom to form a globalterm·key percept.

In Experiment 3, access to fine-detail acoustic information is recovered if sound segments are followed by a silent interval of hundred milliseconds. This suggests that the representation of fine acoustic information can only be well-formed within a temporal windowterm·dom of this range. The results enrich explanations of a recent study of speech perception 49 , in which compressed speech becomes more intelligible after inserting intervals between segments, and also echo earlier findings (for a review, see Massaro, 1972). It is worth bearing in mind that, in a range of neurophysiological studies, ~150–300 ms are around the length of one cycle of neural oscillations in the theta band (~3–7 Hz) that are found in speech perception and other auditory tasks 30 , 31 , 34 , 36 , 37 , 52 , 53 . Experiment 3 provides behavioral evidence that the auditory systemterm·key chunks incoming information into units of the appropriate temporal granularity 29 .

Inspired by the multiple-looksterm·dom and STEP models, we suggest a slightly different temporal integrationterm·dom process to account for the present results. We hypothesize that two stages of temporal integrationterm·dom exist in the auditory systemterm·key. In the first stage, the acoustic information is sampled using two temporal windows, a localterm·key one in the tens-of-milliseconds range and a globalterm·key one in the hundreds-of-milliseconds range. Detailed information is sampled by the localterm·key temporal windowterm·dom so that fast fluctuations and fine-grained information can be analyzed in a high-time-resolution manner; in contrast, acoustic patterns or statistics, such as pitch and loudness, are better abstracted using the globalterm·key temporal windowterm·dom so that robust estimation of these acoustic attributes can be achieved. In a second stage, the sampled information is channeled to a representational stage with limited information capacity and probably with a time constant of hundreds of milliseconds. The information at this representational stage supports making responses and memory storage. Because it is unlikely that all detailed information can be represented or stored, the representation of sounds may not simply be an entire set of acoustic samples. The information is ‘smoothed’ or sparsely coded. Attention driven by task demands at this stage can bias representation so that only selected information is represented.

Such a proposed integration model can explain four main findings in the present study:

Longer stimulus lengthterm·var does not increase performance in the local taskterm·def as demonstrated by Experiment 1. Although the localterm·key details can be well sampled as suggested by the multiple-looks modelterm·def and STEP using a short temporal windowterm·dom, the representation of the localterm·key details is not a vector of samples, as STEP suggests. When the segment size is fixed, a longer stimulus means more samples of segment directions are presented. However, the limited information capacity of the representation stage forces the auditory systemterm·key to smooth or summarize all information. The details are lost at the representation stage, if too much information is squeezed into the representation. In contrast, when there are only a few segments in the stimuli, the amount of detailed information is low and the information can be well represented, since no ‘smoothing’ or ‘summarizing’ process is implemented. Therefore, increasing stimulus lengthterm·var does not improve performance, and the performance in the local taskterm·def is best at short stimulus lengthterm·var.

Globalterm·key perception is best in the range of hundreds of milliseconds. The auditory systemterm·key employs a long temporal windowterm·dom to abstract information on a globalterm·key scale at the first stage and, depending on task requirements, represents globalterm·key patterns in this representation stage. This contrasts with models suggesting that a globalterm·key process is increasingly accumulating information derived from a short integration scale. This can explain a sharp change of performance at around 150 ms of stimulus lengthterm·var in the globalterm·key task of Experiment 1B. As stimulus lengthterm·var increases to a point, a globalterm·key pattern emerges and is sampled by the long temporal windowterm·dom. When the segment durationterm·var is varied, the small segment durationterm·var (<50 ms) does not affect the globalterm·key percept because of the direct sampling on a globalterm·key scale. When segment durationterm·var increases above 75 ms, although each segment still carries information of globalterm·key trend, localterm·key directions of segments are sampled by long temporal windowterm·dom so the recognition of globalterm·key pattern decreases.

An interval is needed for access to localterm·key details shown by Experiment 3. Given a certain amount of time, the representational capacity is limited. After intervals are inserted between segments, information per unit time decreases, so localterm·key details can be represented in a more precise manner. Therefore, the performance in the local taskterm·def of Experiment 3A gets better as the length of intervals increases.

Inserting intervals decreases performance in a globalterm·key task as in Experiment 3B. As the long temporal windowterm·dom is around hundreds of milliseconds, inserting intervals stretches the stimuli and makes few segments fall within one long temporal windowterm·dom, so a globalterm·key pattern of the stretched stimuli cannot be sampled.

Conclusion

Given the results of the present study, the closest heuristic model that accounts for the data is a two-timescale model: one temporal windowterm·dom works on short timescaleterm·dom (<50 ms) to resolve and integrate fine-grained information; concurrently another window works on a long timescaleterm·dom (~200 ms) to extract the globalterm·key pattern of sound segments. Such a multi-time resolution hypothesis, proposed by PoeppelpersName (2001, 2003) for speech is probably a general mechanism for audition, not just a specific model for speech perception as was originally argued. Our results thus build on the previous models of temporal integrationterm·dom and shed light on the multiple timescales of auditory processing.
