Validation of decreasing antibody responses in mildly ill and asymptomatic individuals

Most individuals who become infected with SARS-CoV-2term infection do not require hospitalization to recover from their illness, and a sizeable fraction (approximately 40-45%) ( 29 ) remain asymptomatic. We carried out further analysis of antibody responses in a larger set of samples from 136 outpatients and asymptomatic individualsterm who tested positive by rRT-PCR for SARS-CoV-2term RNA in nasopharyngeal swabs and had serological testing conducted in the Stanford Health Care Clinical Laboratory. We also tested an independent validation setterm of 45 plasma samples from an additional 14 asymptomatic or mildly ill individuals. As seen for outpatients in Fig. 2B , the asymptomatic and outpatient individuals showed relatively low titers and rapid decline of RBD IgMterm and IgG ( Fig. 4A , table S3). The timing of infection in asymptomatic individuals is less certain than for symptomatic individuals, whose symptoms usually develop within 2 to 14 days after exposure to the virus ( 30 ). Plotting of serological responses of outpatients and asymptomatic individualsterm relative to the date of their first positive rRT-PCR test for infection nonetheless showed a similar time course to that seen for outpatients plotted as days post onset of symptoms ( Fig. 2B ). Notably, the amount of viral RNA detected in diagnostic nasopharyngeal swabs was correlated with the antibody titersterm measured in these individuals ( Fig. 4B ).

We identified three main patterns of responseterm: at the time point closest to their discharge from hospital, or death, Group 1term individuals had SARS-CoV-2term antibody responses with no RBD IgGterm or RBD-ACE2 blockingterm activity; Group 2term individuals had up to 25% RBD-ACE2 blockingterm activity; and Group 3term individuals developed high levels of antibodies and over 25% RBD-ACE2 blockingterm activity. These patterns of response were not strongly associated with disease severityterm, but instead were shared across disease categoriesterm. Group 1term patients included admitted patients who recovered from their illness rapidly and were discharged before they had developed detectable antibody responses, but also included patients who recovered from their illness without antibody production after prolonged ICU stays, and patients who died of COVID-19 prior to antibody development. In Group 3term (high levels of antibody production and >25% RBD-ACE2 blockingterm activity) admitted non-ICU patients differed from ICU or deceased patients in that they developed their robust antibody responses during short hospital stays prior to discharge, whereas ICU or deceased patients typically had prolonged hospital courses. In patients tested for neutralizing antibodiesterm (13 admitted non-ICU patients, 16 ICU surviving patients, and 16 deceased patients), the results were closely correlated with IgG titers to RBD, or RBD-ACE2 blockingterm.

DISCUSSION

Key clinical questions in the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic are the role of antibodies in modulating disease severityterm during infection, the duration of individuals’ serological responses, and the extent to which patient antibody responses may be protective against reinfectionterm. As SARS-CoV-2term vaccine candidates progress through clinical trials, comparison of vaccine-induced immune responsesterm to those stimulated by viral infection, and those of individuals who become reinfected, will help clarify immunological correlates of protectionterm. More comprehensive understanding of the role of antibodies in acute COVID-19 illness will guide effective use of therapeutic convalescent plasmaterm products and recombinant antibodiesterm targeting SARS-CoV-2term. Assuming spike-targeting vaccines are shown to be safe and effective and become widely used, monitoring N-specific antibodiesterm may be of utility in distinguishing vaccine-related antibodiesterm from those stimulated by infection.

Here, we have analyzed serological responses to SARS-CoV-2term in 254 individuals ranging from asymptomatic individuals to ICU patients, with detailed analysis of antibody responses to SARS-CoV-2 RBDterm, S1term and N antigens, and functional assays testing antibody blocking of RBD-ACE2 binding or spike-pseudotyped virus neutralizationterm. At early time points post-onset of symptoms, we saw no evidence of pre-existing antibodies that recognize SARS-CoV-2term, suggesting that there is minimal cross-reactivityterm of SARS-CoV-2term antigens with community coronaviruses. These findings do not preclude the possibility that memory B cells stimulated by prior coronavirus exposure may be cross-reactive and form part of the response to SARS-CoV-2term.

IgM, IgG and IgA antibodies specific for SARS-CoV-2term antigens typically become detectable in patients’ blood at similar median times of about 2 weeks after onset of symptoms (this study and ( 32 , 33 )), although IgA timing is the most variable. As we have recently reported, large polyclonal proliferations of recently class-switched B cellsterm expressing IgG and IgA subtypes with low antibody somatic mutation frequencies appear in the circulation around the time of seroconversionterm, likely including plasmablaststerm that contribute to the observed serological responses ( 29 ).

Which features of patient antibody responses were associated with disease severityterm? To our surprise, we found that outpatients with the mildest illnessterm showed higher ratios of spike RBD and S1 domains compared to N antigenterm, beginning in the first two weeks post-onset of symptoms. These findings could suggest that early humoral immune responses focused on spiketerm antigens help to constrain the viral infection, perhaps even at times when titers are not yet high enough to be measured in the blood. Outpatients and admitted non-ICU patients had the lowest viral loads in their nasopharyngeal swabs, but whether this is due to their antibody responses cannot be determined from this observational studyterm. Associations of mortality in COVID-19 patients with SARS-CoV-2term viral load assessed by rRT-PCR applied to nasopharyngeal swabterm specimens have also been found in other studies ( 34 , 35 ). Our data are consistent with results reported from a panel of antibody assays applied to single time point samples from COVID-19 patients who recovered or died of their disease, which found higher values of spike-targeting responses in the convalescents ( 19 ). Patients with more severe illness in our study eventually raised higher antibody titersterm than those with milder disease, consistent with prior publications ( 32 , 36 ) and reports of other coronavirus infections ( 21 – 23 ). Patients with more severe disease also had somewhat higher viral loads than patients with milder illness, suggesting that larger initial amounts of viral antigenterm may contribute to their greater serological responsesterm. Functional blocking of RBD-ACE2 interaction, and spike-pseudotyped virus neutralizationterm by patient antibodies appeared with a similar time course to IgM, IgG, and IgA, but were most closely correlated in magnitude with IgG titers. Neutralizationterm, RBD-ACE2 blockingterm, and RBD-specific IgGterm were all highly correlated in patients with high antibody levels, but RBD-ACE2 blockingterm was less sensitive than the neutralization assayterm, potentially because of antibodies that can neutralize by binding to non-RBD regions of the spiketerm, or lower affinity antibodies that can neutralize in the cell culture assay but do not compete as well with binding of ACE2 under the blocking assay conditions. It is currently unclear, however, which of these assays will be the best predictor of in vivo immunological protection from SARS-CoV-2term infection or reinfectionterm in vaccinated or previously infected individuals.

In the detailed serological time courses of the hospitalized patients in this study it was evident that the patterns of antibody responses could not fully explain patient outcomes, including death. Substantial numbers of patients recovered from their illness and were discharged from hospital before they had formed detectable antibody responses, but minimal serological responses were also seen in patients who died of COVID-19 at early times post-onset of symptoms. Similarly, individuals with moderate antibody production were seen across the full spectrum of inpatient disease severityterm, and many patients who died of their disease generated high levels of antibodies, RBD-ACE2 blockingterm activity, and neutralizing titers. Differences between individuals in other aspects of the immune response or disease course, such as production of inflammatory mediatorsterm, T cell responsesterm, host cell and tissue vulnerability to the damage during viral infection, coagulopathyterm, and secondary infectionsterm, are all likely to contribute to patient outcomes.

There is an urgent need to understand how long antibody titersterm against the virus persist after infection, now that the pandemic has been underway for more than half a year in many countries, and initial case reports of proven reinfectionterm by SARS-CoV-2term have begun to appear ( 37 , 38 ). Studies differing in their patient populations, disease severityterm, and serological assays, have disagreed on the duration of SARS-CoV-2term antibody responses ( 14 , 16 , 32 ). Our data derived from inpatients, outpatients and asymptomatic individualsterm, with an additional asymptomatic validation cohortterm, show clearly that not only IgM and IgA, but also IgG titers to RBD, S1term and N antigens, RBD-ACE2 blockingterm activity and spiketerm pseudotyped viral neutralizationterm titers all begin to decrease in patients after approximately the first month post-onset of symptoms. The decline in antibody titersterm is most evident in individuals who had asymptomatic infectionterm or mild illness, who produce lower levels of antibodies at the peak of their responses. We note that reported results failing to find a decrease in SARS-CoV-2term antibodies after several months post-infection have relied on “pan-Igterm” assays that cannot evaluate each isotypeterm separately ( 16 ). Our data do not permit us to predict what fraction of the population will be susceptible to reinfectionterm at a given time after their initial illness, or whether individuals will maintain sustained plateaus of lower antibody levels after an initial decrease; additional time and follow-up will be required to obtain this information. A limitation of our study is that, apart from the prospective validation cohortterm, the sampling time pointsterm for each patient were determined by their length of hospital stay and subsequent healthcare visitsterm, enabling more detailed analysis of serological responses in patients with more severe illness. It is important to note that decreasing antibody levelsterm do not necessarily indicate that all immunity will be lostterm. It is possible that local mucosal antibody productionterm in the airways could help prevent or impede SARS-CoV-2-infection upon re-exposureterm ( 39 ). Even if serum antibodies wane to undetectable levels, memory B and T cellsterm stimulated by infection could provide a faster or more effective response following future exposure. Initial reinfectionterm reports offer some hope that SARS-CoV-2term may behave similarly to other community coronaviruses, with reinfectionterm generally producing milder illness than the initial infection ( 40 , 41 ).

One implication of our finding of waning antibody levelsterm is that seroprevalence studiesterm may, over time, underestimate the proportion of the investigated population which has been previously infected with SARS-CoV-2term. The decrease in antibodies after infection also raises the question of how long antibodies elicited by vaccinationterm will last, and whether frequent boostingterm will be needed to maintain protection, assuming that safe and effective vaccines are identified. The current vaccination strategiesterm undergoing clinical trials differ from natural infectionterm in a variety of ways, including the method for generating or introducing viral antigens into the body, the site of exposure, and the presence of adjuvantsterm ( 42 – 45 ). It is possible that some of the vaccine approaches may generate more potent and long-lasting antibodies than natural infectionterm, in which the virus may have currently unknown mechanisms for subverting humoral immune responses. Further detailed study of the generation of memory B cell populationsterm, short- or long-lived plasma cellsterm, and T cell memoryterm to SARS-CoV-2term as well as other coronaviruses should begin to clarify some of these key mechanistic points.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and participants

The objective of this study was to investigate correlations between humoral immune responses to SARS-CoV-2term, including antibodies blocking binding of RBD to the human ACE2 receptor or neutralizing spiketerm pseudovirus, and viral RNA loads in the nasopharynx and blood in different COVID-19 patient groups and individual patients. On March 4, 2020, the Stanford Health Care Clinical Virology Laboratory began rRT-PCR testing on nasopharyngeal specimens from suspected COVID-19 patients using a laboratory-developed SARS-CoV-2term rRT-PCR assay ( 46 , 47 ). For this study, we included specimens from patients with rRT-PCR-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infectionterm who reported with symptoms of COVID-19 to Stanford Healthcare-associated clinical sites between March 2020 and August 2020; and specimens from rRT-PCR positive outpatients and asymptomatic individualsterm identified between April 2020 and May 2020 through occupational health screeningterm including rRT-PCR and serology testing for RBD IgMterm/G at Stanford Clinical Laboratories. The screening program that detected asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2term infected individuals was offered to all Stanford Healthcare employees on a voluntary basis, and screened employees with nasopharyngeal swabterm rRT-PCR testing and serology. In addition, we included a validation cohortterm of 14 asymptomatic and mildly ill individuals with prospective sample collection for up to four months post-enrollment. This study was approved by the Stanford University Institutional Review Board (Protocols IRB-48973 and IRB-55689).

Sample and data collection

Venipuncture blood samples collected in sodium heparin- or K 2 EDTA-coated vacutainers were used for serology testing and rRT-PCR detection of RNAemiaterm, respectively. After centrifugation for collection of plasma, samples were stored at -80°C.

Retrospective chart review was performed on all study participants. Collected data included age, gender, date of symptom onset, length of hospital stay, length of time admitted in the ICU, date and Ct value for the diagnostic nasopharyngeal swabterm rRT-PCR test result, the presence of underlying comorbidities, clinical symptoms, and mortality.

Production of SARS-CoVterm and SARS-CoV-2term proteins and ACE2-mFc

The SARS-CoVterm and SARS-CoV-2 RBDterm proteins were expressed in Expi293F cells and purified using Nickel-NTA resin and size exclusion chromatography. The SARS-CoVterm construct (RBD-His_pTT5, GenBank AAP13441.1 ) was synthesized commercially by Twist Bioscience (San Francisco, CA); the SARS-CoV-2term construct (RBD-His_pCAGGS, GenBank MN908947.3 ) was kindly provided by Dr. Florian Krammer ( 48 ). ELISA to detect anti-SARS-CoV-2 Spiketerm RBD antibodies in plasma samples

The ELISA procedure in this study was modified from a protocol published by Stadlbauer ( 48 ).

96-well Corning Costar high binding plates (catalog no. 9018, Thermo Fisher) were coated with SARS-CoV RBDterm, SARS-CoV-2 RBDterm, S1term, or N protein in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at a concentration of 0.1 μg per well (0.025 μg per well for the nucleocapsid IgG assay) overnight at 4°C. On the next day, wells were washed 3x with PBS-0.1% Tween 20 (PBS-T) and blocked with PBS-T containing 3% non-fat milk powder for 1 hour at room temperature (RT). Wells were then incubated with plasma samples from COVID-19 patients at a dilution of 1:100 in PBS-T containing 1% non-fat milk for 1 hour at 37°C. Two negative and two positive plasma pool wells and two blank wells incubated with PBS-T containing 1% non-fat milk powder were included on each plate. After washing 3x with PBS-T, horseradish peroxidase conjugated goat anti-human IgG (γ-chain specific, catalog no. 62-8420, Thermo Fisher, 1:6,000 dilution), IgM (μ-chain specific, catalog no. A6907, Sigma, 1:6,000 dilution), or IgA (α-chain specific, catalog no. P0216, Agilent, 1:5,000 dilution) in PBS-T containing 1% non-fat milk was added and incubated for 1 hour at RT. Wells were washed 3x with PBS-T and dried by vigorous tapping of plates on paper towels. 3,3′,5,5′-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate solution was added and the reaction was stopped after 12 min by addition of 0.16 M sulfuric acid. The optical density (OD) at 450 nanometers was measured with an EMax Plus microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA); values for blank wells were subtracted from values obtained for plasma samples. The cutoff value for seroconversionterm was calculated by adding 3 standard deviations to mean ELISA ODs of 94 historical negative control samplesterm from healthy blood donors (collected before the pandemic for an unrelated seroprevalence study) obtained by testing the samples in all protein/isotypeterm assays (table S4). Additional details for the manual and clinical lab instrument ELISA assay setup are provided in figs. S12 and S13.

Competition ELISA to detect antibodies blocking binding of ACE2 to RBD

Pseudotyped lentiviral neutralization assayterm

SARS-CoV-2term pseudotyped lentivirus assays were performed as described previously. For viral neutralizationterm assays, ACE2/HeLa cells ( 28 ) were plated in 96-well tissue culture plates one day prior to infection. After incubation, cells were lysed with BriteLite assay readout solution (Perkin Elmer) and luminescence values were obtained with a BioTek plate reader. Single-dilution point neutralizationterm assays were performed in technical duplicateterm in two different experimental replicates. Percent neutralizationterm was determined by normalizing raw luciferase values to 0% infectivity (average from cells only wells) and 100% infectivity (average from virus only wells) in GraphPad Prism 8.4.1. Plasma dilution series shown in Figure S9 were performed in technical duplicateterm; normalized % infectivity values were fit with a three-parameter non-linear regression inhibitor curveterm in GraphPad Prism 8.4.1 and fits were constrained to have a value of 0% at the bottom of the fit.

Real-time PCR to detect SARS-CoV-2term RNA in plasma

A volume of 400 μL of EDTA-anticoagulated plasma was extracted by Qiagen EZ1 Virus Mini Kit v2.0 (Qiagen Germantown, MD). Testing of plasma samples with a Ct value of 40 or greater was repeated to ensure reproducibility of the positive result.
