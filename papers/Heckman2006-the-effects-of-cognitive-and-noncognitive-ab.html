phenomenoncognitive and noncognitive skills as determinants of labour-market and behavioural outcomes
claim-typeexistence
transferfrom psychometrics / structural equation modelling (LISREL and MIMIC latent-variable models): The authors take the latent-variable measurement models built in psychometrics and extend them so that a choice variable (schooling) and background variables can enter the measurement equations of an economic model. — “the problems of endogeneity and measurement error. Our method extends the LISREL model”
mechanismA single low-dimensional pair of latent abilities acts as the common cause generating the observed clustering of risky adolescent behaviors together with schooling and wage outcomes. — “Our analysis explains the phenomenon of correlated risky behaviors using the same low dimensional model of latent skills that explains wages, employment and schooling attainment.”
mechanismNoncognitive skills raise wages along two routes: directly by raising productivity, and indirectly by raising schooling attainment and accumulated work experience. — “and family background effects, raise wages through their direct effects on productivity as well”
mechanismThe GED wage penalty arises because GED holders' deficit in noncognitive skill lowers employment and work-experience accumulation and removes a trait the market pays for. — “have lower noncognitive skills than dropouts (see Figure 3) and hence are less likely to be employed”
relation — “This evidence contradicts the “g” theory of human behavior espoused by Herrnstein and Murray (1994), Jensen (1998) and others that focuses on the primacy of cognitive skills in explaining socioeconomic outcomes.”
relation — “Building on the analysis of Hansen, Heckman, and Mullen (2004), we correct measured test scores for these problems.”
relation — “Our method extends the LISREL model of J¨oreskog (1977) and the MIMIC model of J¨oreskog and Goldberger (1975) to account for the effects of choice variables (schooling) and background variables on the measurements of cognitive and noncognitive skills where the schooling, in turn, depends on the latent factors.”
relation — “The first finding corroborates the earlier analyses of Neal and Johnson (1996), Hansen, Heckman, and Mullen (2004) and Heckman, Larenas, and Urzua (2004).”
relation — “Contrary to claims made by Herrnstein and Murray (1994) and Herrnstein and Wilson (1985), it is noncognitive ability that is the dominant factor in explaining different rates of participation in crime, and not cognitive ability.”
relation — “Our evidence that multiple abilities determine schooling challenges the conventional single skill signalling model due to Arrow (1973) and Spence (1973).”
replicationuntested
limitationThe paper does not estimate the separate channels (preferences, discount rate, human-capital productivity, market productivity) through which the abilities operate. — “In this paper, we do not estimate relationships for each of the channels through which cognitive and noncognitive abilities might operate.”
limitationCollapsing noncognitive skill into a single latent factor is unlikely to capture all its aspects. — “Since there are many aspects of noncognitive skills – self control, time preference, sociability, and so forth – it is less likely that one trait captures all aspects of these behaviors.”
limitationSupport conditions for semiparametric identification are not met, so results rest on the parametric structure of the model. — “This evidence suggests that the empirical results that we generate are identified from the parametric structure of the model.”
limitationThe underlying theoretical model is static and ignores the timing of decisions. — “Our theoretical model is static and does not consider the timing of decisions.”
limitationThe corroborating CNLSY79 data lack adult outcomes and have small samples. — “A disadvantage of these data is that many of the children are still young and we lack information on their wages, occupational status and employment at age 30.”
limitationThe noncognitive measures used (Rotter, Rosenberg) are narrower than would be ideal and are not linked to economic preference parameters. — “Ideally, it would be better to use a wider array of psychological measurements and, as previously noted, to connect them with more conventional measures of preference parameters in economics.”
limitationThe scale of the two latent abilities is not measured but set: two normalizations are stated to be required for identification, and they fix the scale of the factors. — “As explained in Section (3.5) two normalizations are required to assure identification of the model. These set the scale of the factors.”
limitationThe direction of the bias in the conventional estimates is stated to be unknown, because the problems push in different directions. — “Because these problems likely bias the estimates in different directions, we cannot predict whether OLS estimates will be higher or lower than those of our model.”
limitationThe measures used are declared imperfect: the attitude scales proxying noncognitive ability are affected by family background and are called at best imperfect measures of true ability. — “In addition, scores on the attitude scales used to proxy noncognitive ability, as well as the cognitive scores, are likely to be affected by family background characteristics, and are at best imperfect measures of an individual’s true noncognitive and cognitive abilities.”
limitationWhat the construct covers is bounded by the instrument available in the survey, and the authors say a wider array of psychological measurement would have been better. — “Ideally, it would be better to use a wider array of psychological measurements and, as previously noted, to connect them with more conventional measures of preference parameters in economics.”
limitationThe channels through which the abilities act are not separately estimated; the model is a linear-in-the-parameters approximation. — “Our econometric model is a linear-in-the-parameters approximation to this general model. In this paper, we do not estimate relationships for each of the channels through which cognitive and noncognitive abilities might operate.”
limitationAn interchangeability the paper asserts in principle is explicitly deferred rather than shown. — “In principle, one can determine which factors are common across tests and preference parameters. Test scores and behaviors can be used interchangeably to proxy factors. This task is left for future work.”
limitationThe disputed word in this literature is 'importance', and the paper's claim holds under one sense of it and reverses under another that the paper states in the same sentence: cognitive skills explain much more of the variance of log wages, while the skill gradients of the two abilities are similar. The paper marks its choice ('for the sense of importance adopted in this paper') rather than leaving it implied, which is what makes the reversal checkable at all. — “For many dimensions of behavior and for the sense of “importance” adopted in this paper, noncognitive ability is as important, if not more important, than cognitive ability.”

findingA model with just two latent skills - one cognitive, one noncognitive - accounts for a wide range of labor-market and behavioral outcomes. — “This paper established that a low dimensional vector of cognitive and noncognitive skills explains a variety of labor market and behavioral outcomes.”
findingAcross many domains of social performance the two skill types matter about equally, contradicting the primacy-of-IQ view. — “For many dimensions of social performance cognitive and noncognitive skills are equally important.”
findingNoncognitive skills shape schooling choices and additionally raise wages conditional on schooling. — “Noncognitive skills strongly influence schooling decisions, and also affect wages given schooling decisions.”
findingSchooling itself raises measured test scores, so observed ability-wage relations are partly reverse causation. — “Schooling raises measured test scores.”
findingOnce corrected for schooling and family background, male GED holders look worse than ordinary dropouts on noncognitive ability although they are better on cognitive ability. — “Male GEDs have a worse noncognitive ability distribution than dropouts.”
findingNoncognitive rather than cognitive ability is what mainly explains differences in criminal participation. — “the dominant factor in explaining different rates of participation in crime, and not cognitive ability.”
findingThe two latent skills carry different hedonic prices in the labor markets of different schooling levels. — “The skills are priced differently in different schooling markets.”
findingEstimated returns to ability shrink when test scores are corrected for the schooling attained at test date, showing that endogenous test scores bias conventional OLS estimates upward. — “typically much smaller using corrected rather than actual test scores. Endogeneity of test scores”
