phenomenoncognitive and noncognitive skills as determinants of labour-market and behavioural outcomes— “This paper presents an analysis of the effects of both cognitive and noncognitive skills on wages, schooling, work experience, occupational choice and participation in a range of adolescent risky behaviors.”
claim-typeexistence— “This paper established that a low dimensional vector of cognitive and noncognitive skills explains a variety of labor market and behavioral outcomes.”
method — evidence— “Our model is a factor model with endogenous factor loadings.”
transferfrom psychometrics / structural equation modelling (LISREL and MIMIC latent-variable models): The authors take the latent-variable measurement models built in psychometrics and extend them so that a choice variable (schooling) and background variables can enter the measurement equations of an economic model.— “the problems of endogeneity and measurement error. Our method extends the LISREL model”
transferfrom psychology (the Rotter and Rosenberg scales): two psychology instruments - the Rotter Locus of Control Scale and the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale - are imported into labour economics to stand for noncognitive skill— “The noncognitive measures available in the data set are the Rotter Locus of Control Scale which was administered in 1979 and the Rosenberg 5 Self-Esteem Scale which was administered in 1980.” [checked against the archived source, not this rendering]
transferfrom military psychometrics (the ASVAB): cognitive skill is measured with five subtests of the Armed Services Vocational Aptitude Battery, an instrument built for military selection and re-used as an economic variable— “Our analysis of test scores uses five measures of cognitive skills (arithmetic reasoning, word knowledge, paragraph comprehension, mathematical knowledge, and coding speed) derived from the Armed Services Vocational Aptitude Battery (ASVAB), which was administered to all sample participants in 1980 and 1981.”
mechanismA single low-dimensional pair of latent abilities acts as the common cause generating the observed clustering of risky adolescent behaviors together with schooling and wage outcomes.— “Our analysis explains the phenomenon of correlated risky behaviors using the same low dimensional model of latent skills that explains wages, employment and schooling attainment.”
mechanismNoncognitive skills raise wages along two routes: directly by raising productivity, and indirectly by raising schooling attainment and accumulated work experience.— “and family background effects, raise wages through their direct effects on productivity as well”
mechanismThe GED wage penalty arises because GED holders' deficit in noncognitive skill lowers employment and work-experience accumulation and removes a trait the market pays for.— “have lower noncognitive skills than dropouts (see Figure 3) and hence are less likely to be employed”
contradicts the "g" theory of Herrnstein and Murray (1994) and Jensen (1998)— “This evidence contradicts the “g” theory of human behavior espoused by Herrnstein and Murray (1994), Jensen (1998) and others that focuses on the primacy of cognitive skills in explaining socioeconomic outcomes.”
builds-on Hansen, Heckman, and Mullen (2004)— “Building on the analysis of Hansen, Heckman, and Mullen (2004), we correct measured test scores for these problems.”
extends the LISREL model of Jöreskog (1977) and the MIMIC model of Jöreskog and Goldberger (1975)— “Our method extends the LISREL model of J¨oreskog (1977) and the MIMIC model of J¨oreskog and Goldberger (1975) to account for the effects of choice variables (schooling) and background variables on the measurements of cognitive and noncognitive skills where the schooling, in turn, depends on the latent factors.”
replicates Neal and Johnson (1996); Hansen, Heckman, and Mullen (2004); Heckman, Larenas, and Urzua (2004)— “The first finding corroborates the earlier analyses of Neal and Johnson (1996), Hansen, Heckman, and Mullen (2004) and Heckman, Larenas, and Urzua (2004).”
contradicts Herrnstein and Murray (1994) and Herrnstein and Wilson (1985) on cognitive ability and crime— “Contrary to claims made by Herrnstein and Murray (1994) and Herrnstein and Wilson (1985), it is noncognitive ability that is the dominant factor in explaining different rates of participation in crime, and not cognitive ability.”
contradicts the single-skill signalling model of Arrow (1973) and Spence (1973)— “Our evidence that multiple abilities determine schooling challenges the conventional single skill signalling model due to Arrow (1973) and Spence (1973).”

limitationThe disputed word in this literature is 'importance', and the paper's claim holds under one sense of it and reverses under another that the paper states in the same sentence: cognitive skills explain much more of the variance of log wages, while the skill gradients of the two abilities are similar. findingA model with just two latent skills - one cognitive, one noncognitive - accounts for a wide range of labor-market and behavioral outcomes.— “This paper established that a low dimensional vector of cognitive and noncognitive skills explains a variety of labor market and behavioral outcomes.”
findingAcross many domains of social performance the two skill types matter about equally, contradicting the primacy-of-IQ view.— “For many dimensions of social performance cognitive and noncognitive skills are equally important.”
findingNoncognitive skills shape schooling choices and additionally raise wages conditional on schooling.— “Noncognitive skills strongly influence schooling decisions, and also affect wages given schooling decisions.”
findingSchooling itself raises measured test scores, so observed ability-wage relations are partly reverse causation.
findingOnce corrected for schooling and family background, male GED holders look worse than ordinary dropouts on noncognitive ability although they are better on cognitive ability.
findingNoncognitive rather than cognitive ability is what mainly explains differences in criminal participation.
findingThe two latent skills carry different hedonic prices in the labor markets of different schooling levels.
findingEstimated returns to ability shrink when test scores are corrected for the schooling attained at test date, showing that endogenous test scores bias conventional OLS estimates upward.
