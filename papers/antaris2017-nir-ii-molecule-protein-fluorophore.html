Results Synthesis and optical characterization Synthesis of the sulfonated small-molecule organic dye (CH-4Tterm, 1.4 kDa, Fig. 1a ) was achieved through amide bond formation between the carboxylated CH1055 and taurine by O -(benzotriazol-1-yl)- N,N,N′,N′ -tetramethyluronium hexafluorophosphate (HBTU), N , N -diisopropylethlamine (DIPEA) coupling in DMSO (see Supplementary Methods for complete synthesis details). Taurine is a common biomolecule found throughout the body containing both an amine and sulfonate group, thus allowing facile modification of CH1055. After conjugation, CH-4Tterm was purified with HPLC and the eluted fractions analysed by matrix-assisted laser desorption ionizing time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF-MS) to collect CH1055 conjugated to four taurine moieties ( Supplementary Fig. 1 ). An nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectrum further confirmed the synthesis and purity of CH-4Tterm ( Supplementary Fig. 2 ). An ultraviolet-visible absorbance spectrum revealed a strong peak at ∼738 nm ( Supplementary Fig. 3 ). The relative fluorescent brightness of CH-4Tterm was investigated by matching the absorbance at 808 nm (optical density (OD) 0.1) in deionized water (DI), PBS and fetal bovine serum (FBS) and imaging the vials on an indium-gallium-arsenide (InGaAs) camera under 808 nm excitation. Discussion Imaging in the second near-infrared windowterm·dom allows optical imaging to peer deeper into the body with more clarity than any other wavelength regime 22 23 24 25 26 27 . However, the development of small-molecule organic dyes is necessary preceding the widespread use of this imaging technique. Inorganic nanomaterials are excellent for fluorescence-based preclinical animal imaging and while many current nanomaterial NIR-IIterm·dom probes show strong promise for clinical translation, organic NIR-IIterm·dom dyes similar in structure and pharmacokinetics to the FDA approved NIR-I organic fluorophoresterm·key such as ICGterm and methylene blue should expedite the transition of NIR-IIterm·dom imaging into the clinic 28 29 30 . Researchers without the specialized knowledge of nanomaterial-based fluorophoresterm·key would benefit from organic NIR-IIterm·dom dyes that could be incorporated into bioimaging procedures currently relying on the plethora of visible and NIR-I dyes. CH-4Tterm's complete aqueous solubility combined with its high serum quantum yieldterm·dom makes it an attractive NIR-IIterm·dom clinical candidate capable of being utilized with ease for biomedical vascular imaging procedures. CH-4Tterm can dramatically reduce fluorescent dye doses by ∼25-fold for biomedical imaging compared to previous, low quantum yieldterm·dom contrast agentsterm·key such as CH-PEGterm. CH-4Tterm can also uniquely enable the fastest temporal resolution in the NIR-IIterm·dom spectral window The D–A–Dterm·dom NIR-IIterm·dom dye architecture shows strong potential for developing an array of NIR-IIterm·dom fluorescent probes through the molecular engineering of their constituent components. The addition of the negatively charged sulfonate groups translated into a dramatic boost in the in vivo quantum yieldterm·dom of CH1055 that allowed for video-rate imagingterm·key at the fastest frame rate along with the lowest exposures time to date for imaging at NIR-IIterm·dom wavelengths 1 . Small changes in the molecular structure of CH1055 yielded a dramatic increase in the optical performance of the D–A–Dterm·dom dye, while further optimization of the electron acceptor, π -spacers, electron donors and functional groups is likely to further increase the maximum attainable NIR-IIterm·dom quantum yieldterm·dom.

The advent of a high quantum yieldterm·dom NIR-IIterm·dom fluorophoreterm·key enables many exciting possibilities for NIR-IIterm·dom medical imaging in terms of exposure time, imaging speed and penetration depth. Carbon nanotubes, encapsulated organic dyes and Ag 2 S quantum dot contrast agentsterm·key have required exposure times of 100 ms and may even exceed 200 ms for video-rate imagingterm·key 2 3 4 5 . The instrument overhead time for normal video-rate imagingterm·key is ∼80 ms and results in a frame rate of 5.3 FPS at a typical NIR-IIterm·dom exposure time of 100 ms. The donor–acceptor NIR-IIterm·dom polymer with a nominal 1.4% quantum yieldterm·dom allowed ultra-fast videos at ∼20 ms exposure times and frame rates of 25 FPS 1 . The CH-4Tterm complex was able to easily capture ultra-fast videos under 1.5–2 ms exposure times at 50 FPS, which is virtually at the maximum possible frame rate of our imaging system (52 FPS) given the instrument overhead time of 19 ms under ultra-fast imaging conditions.

Even though CH-4Tterm has a significant higher brightness than the NIR-IIterm·dom polymer, the achievable frame rate with CH-4Tterm is only double that of the polymer as the instrument overhead time is now the main factor limiting the time in-between frames 1 . However, designing faster fluorescent NIR-IIterm·dom imaging systems through hardware and software modifications could theoretically yield frame rates surpassing 500 FPS given the 1.5–2 ms exposure time of the CH-4Tterm complexes. Such a high-brightness dye additionally allows the detection of deeper features in the body as evidenced by the clear resolution of the lumbar lymph nodes. As the excitation power density drops off when penetrating deeper into the body, high quantum yieldterm·dom NIR-IIterm·dom fluorophoresterm·key are necessary to generate sufficient fluorescent signal. Although previous materials such as carbon nanotubes or CH-PEGterm can clearly discern more shallow features with a high degree of clarity due to minimal scattering, they have an insufficient quantum yieldterm·dom to detect deep features ( Supplementary Fig. 13 ). The high quantum yieldterm·dom NIR-IIterm·dom dye also lowers the requisite excitation power density as evidenced by the imaging of the lumbar lymph nodes with ICGterm and CH-4Tterm/HSA-HT at an equivalent power density of 16 mW cm −2 and with similar exposure times. Previously, a 17.5-fold higher power density was required for imaging femoral blood vasculature in the hindlimb with carbon nanotubes in the NIR-IIterm·dom (808 nm excitation, 140 mW cm −2 ) compared to IR800 in the NIR-I (785 nm excitation, 8 mW cm −2 ) 4 .

At this juncture, we would like to highlight the variability in the reported absolute quantum yieldterm·dom of IR-26, the reference fluorophoreterm·key that serves as the basis for all reported NIR-IIterm·dom quantum yieldsterm·dom. The first reported quantum yieldterm·dom of IR-26 as 0.5% in 1,2-dichloroethane (DCE), the most widely applied value, has been called into question by recent reports placing the quantum at both 0.05 and 0.1% (refs 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 ). The reported quantum yieldterm·dom for CH-4Tterm and complexes is based on using HiPCO SWCNTs as a reference, whose quantum yieldterm·dom was similar to IR-26 (ref. 1 ). HiPCO SWCNTs were selected as a quantum yieldterm·dom reference as nanotubes are simple to disperse in water, demonstrate excellent stability unlike IR-26 that degrades in 1,2-dichloroethane over relatively short periods to time, and are the most common NIR-IIterm·dom contrast agentterm·key 5 . The brightness of NIR-IIterm·dom dyes will be listed relative to other NIR-IIterm·dom fluorophoresterm·key as well as in absolute quantum yieldterm·dom ranges henceforth until the discrepancies in the reported quantum yieldterm·dom of IR-26 are resolved. As this work marks a transition in the reference absolute quantum yieldterm·dom, the absolute quantum yieldterm·dom of CH-4Tterm is 1.1–11% with respect to IR-26=0.05–0.5%. Changing the absolute quantum yieldterm·dom of the reference only serves to scale the brightness of the entire NIR-IIterm·dom field and does not affect the relative brightness between NIR-IIterm·dom emitting materials. Regardless, CH-4Tterm is by far the brightest organic fluorophoreterm·key of any NIR-I or NIR-IIterm·dom dye emitting past 1,000 nm ( Supplementary Fig. 14 ) with a 27-fold higher quantum yieldterm·dom than HiPCO SWCNTs, a classic NIR-IIterm·dom imaging probe.

Tuning the interactions between organic NIR-IIterm·dom dyes consisting of large, hydrophobic π -systems and plasma proteins is a route to brilliantly fluorescent dyes emitting past 1,000 nm. In a similar fashion to ICGterm's ∼4 × increase in brightness when exposed to serum proteinsterm·key, the 110-fold fluorescence increase between CH-4Tterm/FBS-HT and CH-4Tterm in PBS most likely occurs through the combination of multiple processes 35 36 . First, the amphiphilic character of CH-4Tterm may form aggregates in aqueous solution through van der Waals forces that reduce brightness through self-quenching which is known to occur in ICGterm 29 . The dispersion of aggregates by CH-4Tterm individualization through protein binding should contribute to the serum boost in quantum yieldterm·dom. Second, CH-4Tterm geometrical confinement by hydrophobic pockets should promote a rigid dye conformation that minimizes the torsional rotations that result in non-radiative decayterm·dom 12 . Rigid molecules have shorter fluorescent lifetimes and generally higher quantum yieldsterm·dom as they dissipate less energy though internal conversionterm·dom compared to those with freely rotating parts 37 . Internal conversionterm·dom is the most important competing non-emissive process for low energy-gap NIR-IIterm·dom dyes thus increasing rigidityterm·dom through protein binding will favourably increase quantum yieldterm·dom. Heating magnifies these effects as CH-4Tterm presumably becoming entangled in a rigid conformation as increased thermal energy allows access to typically inaccessible hydrophobic pockets. At higher temperatures, CH-4Tterm may become completely encased in hydrophobic domains as proteins re-fold around the dye after heating to minimize the number of hydrophobic side-chains exposed to water. In addition, donor–acceptor dyes are subject to twisted intramolecular charge transferterm·dom (TICTterm·dom) in polar environments, especially if comprised of strong electron donors and acceptors 38 39 40 41 . Molecular simulations predict the core of CH1055 can exist in non-planar conformations that may contribute to a red-shifted TICTterm·dom conformation that can dampen fluorescence 12 . The observed hypsochromic shiftterm·dom of ∼50 nm can be explained through CH-4Tterm adopting a planar configuration and the alignment of p -orbitals when binding to proteins via hydrophobic or charge interactions in conjunction with minimizing solvent relaxation.

Developing a fundamental understanding of the manner in which NIR-IIterm·dom organic dyes interact with biological tissue is of critical importance for creating probes with high SBRterm·dom ratios as well as high excretion levels. CH-4Tterm demonstrated an impressively high quantum yieldterm·dom thorough protein binding and the dye was excreted through the biliary system with little residual dye left in normal tissue just days post-injection. Unlike current NIR-IIterm·dom nanomaterial fluorophoresterm·key, CH-4Tterm demonstrated complete excretion from the body, albeit at a slower rate than CH-PEGterm ( Supplementary Fig. 15 ). CH-4Tterm elicited no observable cellular toxicity ( Supplementary Figs 16 and 17 ), while CH-4Tterm/FBS-HT and CH-4Tterm/HSA-HT showed a marginal reduction in cell viability at higher concentrations ( Supplementary Figs 18 and 19 ). However, in a similar manner to the pioneering work by Frangioni and colleagues, tailoring the interaction strength between NIR-IIterm·dom fluorophoresterm·key and proteins to maintain a high quantum yieldterm·dom while employing strategies such as polyionic yet net-neutral probes may expedite excretion kinetics and reduce non-specific in vivo binding 42 43 . The same rational design principles developed for NIR-I fluorophoresterm·key may be applied to NIR-IIterm·dom organic dyes to develop the next generation of contrast and molecular imaging agents.

Hemodynamic imaging with a high frame rate in the NIR-IIterm·dom poses several advantages over other vascular flow imaging modalities such as laser speckle flowgraphyterm·mth and photoacoustic imagingterm·mth (PI). Laser speckle flowgraphyterm·mth computes blood velocity based on the measurement of laser speckle blurring (termed normalize blurterm·mth (NBterm·mth)) from coherent light scattering from diffusing sources 44 45 . Modulation of the laser backscattered interference pattern occurs as red blood cells travel through capillaries. Laser speckle flowgraphyterm·mth has significant clinical potential as it requires no exogenous contrast agentsterm·key yet this imaging modality currently suffers from a number limitations. Measurement of laser flux depends highly on tissue properties that can vary from person to person or in disparate disease states. A correlation between the unit-less NBterm·mth and capillary blood flow velocities occurs only under a narrow set of conditions 44 45 . This disallows facile blood velocity comparisons among people or even between an individual's eyes during retinal vasculature imaging. Photoacoustic imagingterm·mth can additionally measure blood velocity without requiring exogenous contrast agentsterm·key by exploiting hemoglobin's strong visible absorbance. However, with minimum resolvable features on the scale of ∼10–100 μm, photoacoustic imagingterm·mth cannot compete with fluorescence imagingterm·mth in terms of spatial resolutionx 46 . CH-4Tterm's penetration depth (1–3 mm) combined with high temporal (50 FPS) and spatial (∼1 μm) resolution enables high-fidelity hemodynamic imaging of vessel sizes ranging from microvasculature to major vessels. Application of high frame-rate NIR-IIterm·dom imaging with CH-4Tterm for hemodynamic visualization within tumours, intravascular tracking of circulating tumour cells or post-trauma cerebral microvascular imaging may provide key insight into disease pathophysiology. In addition to providing absolute blood velocities, CH-4Tterm's high-brightness and long-wavelength emission may improve other biological optical imaging modalities. For instance, one-photon confocal imaging with CH-4Tterm may push the penetration depth limit past that achievable with the current palette of visible/NIR-I/NIR-IIterm·dom fluorophoresterm·key.

Even with the superior NIR-I silicon CCD detector specifications (for example, pixel number, dark current, readout noise and so on), NIR-IIterm·dom imaging on the InGaAs detector with longer wavelength probes improves imaging metrics. As no current silicon detector has suitable sensitivity past ∼1,000 nm, biomedical fluorescent imaging in the NIR-IIterm·dom depended on the emergence of InGaAs focal plane array detectors. However, similarly to the benefits garnered by pushing probe emission out of the visible (∼350–600 nm) and into the NIR-I (∼650–900 nm) on Si cameras, the development of bright, long-wavelength NIR-IIterm·dom probes (>1,000 nm) boosts imaging performance metrics. Internal comparisons within a broad wavelength range on an InGaAs detector have dramatically illustrated the imaging improvements by developing longer wavelength probes from 850 nm (ICGterm) to 1,100 nm (HiPCO SWCNTs) to 1,500 nm (laser vaporization SWCNTs) 14 . ICGterm and laser vaporization SWCNTs both emit in a region with similarly reduced InGaAs quantum efficiency of ∼30–40% compared to ∼85% at ∼1,100 nm with HiPCO SWCNTs. This indicates the marked differences in imaging quality at progressively longer wavelengths arise from reduced scattering/autofluorescence rather than variable detector spectral sensitivities. Fundamental advances in fluorophoreterm·key chemistry have largely driven the NIR-IIterm·dom imaging field, making InGaAs detectors more commonplace for biomedical imaging. While newer generations of InGaAs detectors can uniformly increase sensitivity/SBRterm·dom of all NIR-IIterm·dom probes, relative imaging metrics within the NIR-IIterm·dom spectral range will still be governed by wavelength dependent light-tissue interactions. Improving NIR-IIterm·dom fluorescent probes (that is, increasing both quantum yieldterm·dom and NIR-IIterm·dom emission wavelength) should proceed in parallel with the ongoing innovations in improving InGaAs cameras.
