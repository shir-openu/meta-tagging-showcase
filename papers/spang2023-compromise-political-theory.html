Introduction In its most basic sense, a compromiseterm·dom can be understood as a form of agreement that has the purpose of accommodating conflicting views or claims. 1 As such, compromiseterm·dom is increasingly discussed in political theory as a legitimate approach to dealing with disagreementterm·key in politics and society ( Baume and Novak, 2020 b; Bellamy, 1999 , 2012 ; Gutmann and Thompson, 2012 ; Mansbridge et al., 2010 ; May, 2005 ; Rostbøll and Scavenius, 2019 ; Warren and Mansbridge, 2016 ; Weinstock, 2006 , 2013 ). 2 But aside from a wide acknowledgment that compromiseterm·dom can constitute a legitimate approach to dealing with disagreementterm·key, the literature on compromiseterm·dom is characterized by significant differences and controversies—which is not surprising, given the complexity inherent in the subject of compromiseterm·dom. This reviewterm·mth article aims to shed light on the complexity of compromiseterm·dom first by clarifying central features of compromiseterm·dom and second by identifying major issues of dispute. 3 The article starts by distinguishing compromiseterm·dom from consensusterm·dom, which simultaneously serves the purpose of presenting those features of compromiseterm·dom that characterize it as a distinct concept. The fourth section deals with the process of achieving a compromiseterm·dom, thereby portraying a development in the literature from negotiationterm·key to more deliberative processes. Compromiseterm·dom Versus Consensusterm·dom This section distinguishes compromiseterm·dom from consensusterm·dom. It is important to distinguish between both concepts because they have significant features in common and are therefore easily confused. Like consensusterm·dom, compromiseterm·dom is a possible response to disagreementterm·key or conflict. And like consensusterm·dom, compromiseterm·dom can be placed on the “solution side” within the spectrum of possible responses to disagreementterm·key. As potential solutions to disagreementterm·key, both compromiseterm·dom and consensusterm·dom differ from responses that maintain the status quo of disagreementterm·key or conflict ( Bellamy et al., 2012 ).

But even though compromiseterm·dom and consensusterm·dom both constitute potential solutions to disagreementterm·key, they are not the same kind of solution. Unlike compromiseterm·dom, consensusterm·dom requires the parties to a disagreementterm·key to change their minds on the controversial issue. If a consensusterm·dom is achieved, this means that the disagreeing parties consider the agreement to be better than (or at least as good as) their initial positions ( Bellamy et al., 2012 ; Weinstock, 2006 , 2013 ). Compromiseterm·dom, in contrast, is characterized by the fact that disagreeing parties hold on to their opposing views. As Daniel Weinstock (2013 : 540) puts it, “it does not count as a compromiseterm·dom when you change your mind.” In a compromiseterm·dom, disagreeing parties agree to partially concede their claims to the demands of the other party, but they do not agree with the other party’s demands. 4 It can therefore be said that consensusterm·dom resolves disagreementterm·key in an epistemic sense, while compromiseterm·dom resolves disagreementterm·key in a practical sense, that is, by preventing negative consequences of continued disagreementterm·key. As Manon Westphal (2019) points out, compromisesterm·dom settle a given conflict but do not resolve the underlying disagreementsterm·key. But even though the parties to a compromiseterm·dom continue disagreeing on a controversial issue, a compromiseterm·dom can prevent their disagreementterm·key from spiraling into a full-blown conflict.

Compromiseterm·dom also differs from consensusterm·dom in that the former characteristically involves a sense of regret or dissatisfaction ( Lepora, 2012 ; Lepora and Goodin, 2013 ). Since the parties to a compromiseterm·dom continue to believe that they are right and that the other party is wrong, agreeing on a compromiseterm·dom means agreeing to a solution that will partially realize values that one considers to be wrong. This is especially true for compromisesterm·dom on emotional issues, as is typically the case for issues of moral or political significance. Consensusterm·dom, in contrast, does not involve regret or dissatisfaction. On the contrary, consensusterm·dom (at least theoretically) leaves all parties satisfied, since all parties consider their consensual arrangement to be superior to (or equally good as) their original point of view.

Some scholars therefore claim that consensusterm·dom is normatively superior to compromiseterm·dom. Amy Gutmann and Dennis Thompson (2012 : 13), for example, state that “few doubt that consensusterm·dom is desirable if it can be found, and most agree that it is usually preferable to the standard form of compromiseterm·dom, which leaves all parties dissatisfied.” Similarly, Philippe Van Parijs (2012 : 480) claims that “even the best compromiseterm·dom (. . .) is still not quite as good as an (unconstrained) consensusterm·dom.”

Yet, even though consensusterm·dom may be generally more desirable than compromiseterm·dom, compromiseterm·dom can be more desirable under certain circumstances. More specifically, compromiseterm·dom can be more desirable than consensusterm·dom because it is more feasible when it comes to dealing with disagreementterm·key in real life ( Bellamy et al., 2012 ; Gutmann and Thompson, 2004 , 2012 ; Westphal, 2019 ). Furthermore, it has been pointed out that compromiseterm·dom is not only more realistic than consensusterm·dom but it can also enhance creativity and problem-solving capacities ( Al Ramiah and Hewstone, 2012 ).

In addition to the argument from feasibility, compromiseterm·dom has been endorsed as a desirable response to what John Rawls (2001 : 4) has called “the fact of reasonable pluralismterm·key.” 5 The argument here is that compromiseterm·dom, but not consensusterm·dom, can accommodate reasonable disagreementsterm·key that are inevitably part of pluralistic societies. More specifically, in the case of reasonable disagreementterm·key, compromiseterm·dom, but not consensusterm·dom, allows for equal concern and respect for the reasonable views that are in conflict ( Bellamy et al., 2012 ). In addition, it can be argued that if parties to a disagreementterm·key have equally reasonable (but irreconcilable) views, consensusterm·dom is not desirable, because it requires an unjustifiable change of mind from those who hold reasonable views. Compromiseterm·dom, in contrast, allows disagreeing parties to hold on to their reasonable views, thus constituting a more desirable solution to reasonable disagreementterm·key. 6 Different Kinds of Compromiseterm·dom An undisputed feature of compromiseterm·dom is that it consists of mutual and voluntary concessions ( Bellamy et al., 2012 ; Bohman, 1996 ; Jones and O’Flynn, 2012 ; Lepora, 2012 ; Lepora and Goodin, 2013 ; Margalit, 2010 ; May, 2013 ). As Van Parijs (2012 : 469) points out “a compromiseterm·dom is an agreement, but not just any agreement. Its distinctiveness resides in the mutual concessions involved.” Concessions can, however, be of different kinds, leading to different kinds of compromiseterm·dom. More concretely, depending on the kind of concessions on which a compromiseterm·dom is based, we can distinguish between three kinds of compromiseterm·dom: intersection compromiseterm·dom, conjunction compromiseterm·dom, and substitution compromiseterm·dom ( Lepora, 2012 ; Lepora and Goodin, 2013 ).

Intersection compromiseterm·dom is based on overlapping principles, while controversial principles are excluded from the agreement. 7 Intersection compromiseterm·dom therefore applies to cases of disagreementterm·key where the involved parties have partially overlapping principles. For example, if party P1 endorses principles A, B, and C, and party P2 endorses principles A, D, and E, an intersection compromiseterm·dom would be based exclusively on principle A, while principles B, C, D, and E would be excluded from the compromiseterm·dom agreement ( Lepora, 2012 ; Lepora and Goodin, 2013 ).

Disagreeing parties might, however, not be willing (or able) to base an agreement exclusively on shared principles; or they might simply not share relevant principles in the first place. In such cases, where no intersection compromiseterm·dom is possible, two other kinds of compromiseterm·dom are available. Both will be illustrated with reference to the following example: Valerie is a committed vegan for ethical reasons. She plans to have brunch with her non-vegan friend Nancy. Nancy desires to eat scrambled eggs (E) with bacon (B), while Valerie desires a plant-based breakfast (P) in a vegan location (L). In this case, Valerie and Nancy do not have overlapping principles on which they could base an intersection compromiseterm·dom. Nancy’s principles (E, B) and Valerie’s principles (P, L) are mutually exclusive: you cannot have eggs and bacon in a vegan restaurant. Two kinds of compromiseterm·dom are available in this case.

One option is a conjunction compromiseterm·dom, which integrates some of the conflicting principles ( Lepora, 2012 ; Lepora and Goodin, 2013 ). 8 In the above example, a conjunction compromiseterm·dom could be a vegetarian (but not vegan) restaurant that offers vegan options. In this case, Nancy would be able to eat eggs, but she would have to refrain from eating bacon, while Valerie would have the option to eat vegan food, but in a non-vegan location. In this conjunction compromiseterm·dom, Valerie gets (P) while sacrificing (L), and Nancy gets (E) while sacrificing (B).

Another option to accommodate Valerie and Nancy’s disagreementterm·key is a substitution compromiseterm·dom. Substitution compromiseterm·dom consists only of principles that are not part of the original disagreementterm·key ( Lepora, 2012 ; Lepora and Goodin, 2013 )–it “changes the subject,” so to speak ( Weinstock, 2013 : 545). For example, a substitution compromiseterm·dom for Valerie and Nancy’s disagreementterm·key could be to skip brunch altogether and have a glass of wine in the evening instead. This solution still qualifies as a compromiseterm·dom, since neither party gets what they initially wanted; and it qualifies as a substitution compromiseterm·dom because it does not involve the conflicting principles (i.e. principles (P) and (L) for Valerie, and principles (E) and (B) for Nancy).

Another important distinction is between compromiseterm·dom that accommodates moral disagreementterm·key (“moral compromiseterm·dom”) and compromiseterm·dom that accommodates non-moral disagreementterm·key (“non-moral compromiseterm·dom”). The conceptual distinction between principles and interests serves as a useful framework for addressing the difference between compromisesterm·dom on moral and non-moral disagreementsterm·key. Moral compromiseterm·dom can be understood to involve principles, that is, beliefs and values that are based on moral convictions and that are often part of one’s identity. Non-moral compromiseterm·dom, in contrast, pertains to mere interests, including material interests such as income and wealth ( Gutmann and Thompson, 2012 ).

Regarding moral compromiseterm·dom, the question arises whether it is realistic to expect disagreeing parties to partially concede what they consider to be morally right, and to partially accept what they consider to be morally wrong. Furthermore, as Lepora emphasizes, not only does a moral compromiseterm·dom require us to partially accept what we consider to be wrong in theory, but it also requires us to partake in (what we consider to be) wrongdoing in practice. Therefore, as Chiara Lepora (2012 : 2) puts it, “from each party’s perspective, compromiseterm·dom necessarily involves interacting with, and sometimes contributing to, wrongdoing.” Moreover, Theodore Benditt points out that moral compromiseterm·dom can negatively affect one’s sense of self. If we accept a moral compromiseterm·dom, we might lose esteem not only for the other party but also for ourselves ( Benditt, 1979 )—we are compromised, as the saying goes.

In contrast to moral compromiseterm·dom, the realization of non-moral compromiseterm·dom is usually less problematic, because mere interests tend to be less attached to our sense of integrity, our conception of right and wrong, and they also tend to be less emotionally salient. Indeed, according to Benditt (1979 : 27–28), “it is much easier to accept a compromiseterm·dom between competing interests—particularly when they are expressible in terms of a numerical scale like money—than between opposed principles which purport to be objectively valid.”

One might object that the very distinction between principles and interests is problematic, especially in the political sphere. 9 Alin Fumurescu (2013 : 19), for example, considers this distinction to be “an almost impossible endeavor” when it comes to politics, because crucial questions, such as budget allocations, can be portrayed as either principles or interests, depending on what is deemed more suitable. In a similar vein, Andrew Sabl (2018 : 274) points out that “every non-trivial political question seems a matter of deep principle to some” and that the distinction between interests and principles is therefore hard to sustain in politics.

Admittedly, the distinction between principles and interests is fuzzy, especially in the political sphere, where, indeed, the question arises of whether there are ever non-moral compromisesterm·dom, that is, compromisesterm·dom that do not, in one way or another, involve principles that are based on moral convictions. While this may be true for political decision-making, strictly speaking (e.g. law-making), compromisesterm·dom more generally speaking (e.g. between citizens) can and should be classified along categories of moral versus non-moral concerns. Otherwise, if there is no distinction between moral and non-moral compromisesterm·dom, we are bound to assume that there is no significant difference between a compromiseterm·dom on, say, the price for a pound of apples and abortion rights. As Benditt (1979 : 32) remarks, however we draw the difference between principles and interests, the point remains that “there is undoubtedly a difference in the character of the conflict when principles and ideals are explicitly involved as opposed to when the parties see the conflict as between interests.” Therefore, while the distinction between principles and interests may be hard to sustain at the political level, it may still apply to compromisesterm·dom in general—even though it is, perhaps, less a question of “either-or,” in the sense that a compromiseterm·dom belongs to either the moral or non-moral domain; and more a question of degree, in the sense that a compromiseterm·dom should be located along a spectrum of moral and less moral matters.

Who Compromisesterm·dom?

Compromiseterm·dom can be conceived of in an interpersonalterm·dom and intrapersonalterm·dom sense. In the interpersonalterm·dom sense, compromiseterm·dom occurs between different persons who participate in a decision-making process ( May, 2013 ). As such, a compromiseterm·dom can be struck between individual citizens or between their representatives, for example, legislators or lawyers ( Jones and O’Flynn, 2012 ). 10 In the intrapersonalterm·dom sense, compromiseterm·dom occurs within a person’s head. This conception of compromiseterm·dom refers to the fact that a person also must compromiseterm·dom with herself if she is to compromiseterm·dom with another person.

Peter Jones and Ian O’Flynn claim that the standard meaning of compromiseterm·dom refers to the interpersonalterm·dom sense. The authors argue that the intrapersonalterm·dom notion of compromiseterm·dom is “figurative and parasitic upon the standard notion of compromiseterm·dom as an interpersonalterm·dom or inter-party matter” ( Jones and O’Flynn, 2012 : 118). Similarly, Simon May (2013) holds that compromiseterm·dom is essentially an interpersonalterm·dom matter.

In contrast, Lepora (2012) argues that intrapersonalterm·dom compromiseterm·dom is, in fact, the more fundamental of both conceptions and logically prior to interpersonalterm·dom compromiseterm·dom. According to her conception of intrapersonalterm·dom compromiseterm·dom, a person compromisesterm·dom with herself if she sacrifices principles that conflict with other principles that she endorses: “in the intra-personal case, the compromiseterm·dom is among principles all of which you harbour but not all of which can be simultaneously pursued” ( Lepora, 2012 : 3). Thus understood, intrapersonalterm·dom compromiseterm·dom consists in a decision on which of the conflicting principles we are willing to sacrifice, and it therefore logically precedes the possibility of achieving an interpersonalterm·dom compromiseterm·dom: interpersonalterm·dom compromiseterm·dom requires first an internal compromiseterm·dom between our own principles ( Lepora, 2012 ).

Taking Lepora’s argument a step further, we might consider that intrapersonalterm·dom compromiseterm·dom can entail the decision to compromiseterm·dom in the first place. This would be the case if, for example, a person’s core principle is to never give up on his ideals—in other words, not to compromiseterm·dom on his values. In this case, the decision to compromiseterm·dom is itself a compromiseterm·dom, because the person decides to compromiseterm·dom on his core principle not to compromiseterm·dom.

How to Compromiseterm·dom?

Traditionally, compromiseterm·dom is considered to be the outcome of negotiationterm·key processes during which the involved parties bargain for the best possible outcome. In contrast, deliberative processes during which the participants exchange reasons for their respective views are deemed appropriate only for achieving consensusterm·dom. This traditional view is deeply anchored in everyday language, where compromiseterm·dom is often tied to negotiationterm·key or bargaining. Furthermore, this view is also represented in the academic literature, for example, when Van Parijs (2012 : 469) states that “ negotiationterm·key can lead to a compromiseterm·dom that avoids the costs and risks of conflict, exit or arbitration, whereas deliberationterm·key can lead to a consensusterm·dom about what is required for a fair solution” (emphasis in original). Similarly, May (2018 : 150) ties compromiseterm·dom to negotiationterm·key by emphasizing that “compromiseterm·dom is the paradigmatic feature of negotiationterm·key.” From this perspective, the idea is that we can achieve a compromiseterm·dom by negotiating or bargaining with our opponents, while deliberationterm·key is only required if we aim for a consensusterm·dom.

Limits of Compromiseterm·dom While compromiseterm·dom can be normatively desirable (whether for pragmatic or principled reasons), it is also clear that a compromiseterm·dom is not always justified. In his essay “On Compromiseterm·dom,” Morley specifically sets out to address the limits of compromiseterm·dom.
