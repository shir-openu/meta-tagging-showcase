transferfrom information theory: the classical measurement of information as reduction of uncertainty in bits, applied to brain states and conscious experience — “Classically, information is the reduction of uncertainty among alternatives: when a coin falls on one of its two sides, it provides 1 bit of information, whereas a die falling on one of six faces provides ~2.6 bits.”
transferfrom information theory: the bit and the reduction of uncertainty among alternatives, imported wholesale as the measure of how much experience a brain state constitutes — “Classically, information is the reduction of uncertainty among alternatives: when a coin falls on one of its two sides, it provides 1 bit of information, whereas a die falling on one of six faces provides ~2.6 bits.”
transferfrom network science: small-world topology, used to explain why a small pharmacological insult to a few long-range connections can fragment the whole system — “Small-world networks have mostly local connectivity with comparatively few long-range connections. Augmented with hubs, such networks maximize interactions while minimizing wiring.”
transferfrom information theory / time-series analysis: transfer entropy, borrowed as a directional measure so that feedforward and feedback interactions can be scored separately — “transfer entropy, a measure of directional interactions among brain areas, is balanced in the feedforward (green) and feedback (red) directions”
transferfrom probability, via games of chance: the die as a model of a state repertoire, extended so that losing integration shatters it into many dice and losing information flattens it to two faces — “If integration is lost (as in anesthesia or sleep), the die disintegrates into many two-faced dice, each generating 1 bit of information.”
mechanismAnesthetics abolish consciousness by blocking the corticothalamic system's capacity to integrate information, not simply by silencing neurons — “Thus, anesthetics seem to cause unconsciousness when they block the brain’s ability to integrate information.”
mechanismBecause the corticothalamic system is a small-world network, cutting only a few long-range connections fragments it into functionally disconnected components — “By the same token, anesthetics need only disrupt a few long-range connections to produce a set of disconnected components.”
mechanismDeep anesthesia collapses the response repertoire into a stereotypic two-state burst-suppression regime, so activity carries almost no information even while remaining globally integrated — “However, the repertoire of responses has shrunk to a stereotypic burst-suppression pattern, with a corresponding loss of information, essentially creating a system having only two possible states (on or off).”
mechanism — “According to this framework, anesthetics would produce unconsciousness either by preventing integration (blocking the interactions among specialized brain regions) or by reducing information (shrinking the number of activity patterns available to cortical networks).”
mechanism — “Thus, during deep anesthetic unconsciousness the corticothalamic system can still be active in fact hyperexcitable and can produce global, integrated responses. content-observations: finding · quantity · variable · definition · key-term — grounded in the full text
findingMost anesthetics produce unconsciousness by acting, directly or indirectly, on a posterior lateral corticothalamic complex around the inferior parietal lobe rather than on the whole brain uniformly. — “Despite different mechanisms and sites of action, most anesthetic agents appear to cause unconsciousness by targeting, directly or indirectly, a posterior lateral corticothalamic complex centered around the inferior parietal lobe, and perhaps a medial cortical core.”
findingThe thalamic deactivation seen under anesthesia is better interpreted as a readout of cortical state than as the switch that turns consciousness off. — “Thus, the effects of anesthetics on the thalamus effect may represent a readout of global cortical activity rather than a consciousness switch, and thalamic activity may not be a sufficient basis for consciousness.”
findingBehavioural unresponsiveness under anesthesia does not reliably indicate that the patient is unconscious. — “Thus, clinical unresponsiveness is not necessarily synonymous with unconsciousness.”
findingPost-operative amnesia is likewise not evidence that the patient was unconscious during surgery. — “Thus, retrospective oblivion is no proof of unconsciousness.”
findingEvidence from both anesthesia and sleep converges on two routes to unconsciousness: loss of cortical integration or collapse of the repertoire of cortical activity patterns. — “loss of consciousness is associated with a breakdown of cortical connectivity and thus of integration, or with a collapse of the repertoire of cortical activity patterns and thus of information”
findingOn the integrated-information account, consciousness is graded rather than all-or-none, scaling with the size of a system's repertoire of discriminable states. — “Finally, from this theoretical perspective, consciousness is not an all-or-none property, but it is graded: specifically, it increases in proportion to a system’s repertoire of discriminable states.”
findingintraoperative awareness occurs about once in every 1000–2000 operations — “once in every 1000–2000 operations a patient may temporarily regain consciousness or even remain conscious during surgery ( 1 )”
findingsevoflurane sedation cuts relative thalamic metabolism by 23% in subjects who are still awake and responsive, so thalamic reduction is not sufficient for unconsciousness — “sevoflurane sedation causes a 23% reduction of relative thalamic metabolism when subjects are still awake and responsive ( 30 )”
findingcortical EEG changed at the instant of loss of consciousness while thalamic EEG did not change for another ten minutes — “the cortical EEG changed dramatically the instant the patients lost consciousness ( 38 ). However, there was little change in thalamic EEG activity until 10 minutes later.”
findingin REM sleep the two dissociate the other way: cortical EEG activated as if awake, thalamic EEG showing slow waves as if asleep — “in epileptic patients, during REM sleep (usually associated with dreaming) the cortical EEG was activated as if awake, but the thalamic EEG showed slow wave activity as if asleep ( 39 )”
findingafter thalamic ablation the cortex still produces an activated EEG — “after thalamic ablation, the cortex still produces an activated EEG ( 37 )”
findingthe thalamic effects of anesthetics disappear if the cortex is removed, which makes them look consequent on cortical deactivation — “the metabolic and electrophysiological effects of anesthetics on the thalamus in animals are abolished by removal of the cortex ( 33 , 34 , 36 )”
findingketamine raises global metabolism, especially thalamic, going the opposite way from every other agent — “Ketamine increases global metabolism, especially in the thalamus ( 16 ).”
findingpropofol and thiopental both deactivate posterior areas at equivalent hypnotic doses but only propofol deactivates frontal cortex, so frontal deactivation is not required — “For instance, at equivalent hypnotic doses, both propofol and thiopental deactivate posterior brain areas, but only propofol deactivates frontal cortex ( 44 ).”
findinglarge frontal lesions do not by themselves produce unconsciousness — “Furthermore, large lesions of the frontal cortex do not by themselves produce unconsciousness ( 45 ).”
findinga clinically unresponsive woman with large frontal lesions produced cortical activation patterns indistinguishable from healthy subjects when asked to imagine playing tennis — “a woman with large frontal lesions who was clinically unresponsive was asked to imagine playing tennis or to navigate her room and she showed cortical activation patterns indistinguishable from those of healthy subjects ( 19 )”
findingas consciousness fades, EEG coherence in the gamma range drops between the two frontal cortices and between frontal and occipital regions — “When consciousness fades during anesthesia there is a drop in EEG coherence in the γ frequency range (20 to 80 Hz) between right and left frontal cortices and between frontal and occipital regions ( 4 ).”
findingthe coherence loss is far stronger for long-range than for local connections — “The effect is gradual and much stronger for long-range than for local coherence ( 53 ).”
findingin rats losing responsiveness, information transfer falls in the feedback direction first — “When rats become unresponsive under anesthesia, information transfer first decreases in the feedback direction ( Fig. 3 ) ( 56 ).”
findinganesthesia suppresses the late component of visual responses but not the early feedforward one, and abolishes contextual and attentional modulation — “Also, anesthesia suppresses the late component (>100 ms) of visual responses, possibly by inhibiting feedback connections ( 57 ), but not the early feedforward components. Moreover, anesthesia abolishes contextual and attentional modulation of firing, presumably mediated by feedback connections ( 58 ).”
findingTMS during waking evokes a 300 ms sequential response whose identity depends on the stimulation site; in early non-REM sleep the same pulse produces a short local response under 150 ms — “During early non-REM sleep, presumably due to the induction of a local down-state, TMS pulses produce instead a short (<150 ms) local response ( 64 ), suggesting a loss of integration.”
findingTMS to mesial parietal cortex, over the main connectional hub, triggers a stereotypic high-amplitude slow wave closely resembling a spontaneous one — “Intriguingly, TMS pulses to mesial parietal regions, overlying the main hub in the cortical connectional core ( 49 ), trigger a stereotypic, high-amplitude slow wave closely resembling spontaneous slow waves ( 63 ).”
findingnitrous oxide at intermediate doses selectively deactivates posterior mesial cortex, yet subjects report dreamlike experience rather than none — “It is intriguing that at intermediate doses, certain anesthetics, such as nitrous oxide, produce a fairly selective deactivation of posterior mesial cortex ( 51 ), yet when these areas start to turn off, subjects report dreamlike feelings with depersonalization and out of body experiences, rather than unconsciousness.”
findinggeneralized convulsive seizures lose consciousness with high, hypersynchronous activity — integration intact, repertoire stereotypic — “Generalized convulsive seizures provide another example in which consciousness can be lost even though neural activity remains high and highly synchronized: a large portion of the corticothalamic complex is engaged in strong, hypersynchronous activity, but this activity is stereotypic ( 60 , 61 ).”
findingGABA agonists in the intralaminar nuclei put rats to sleep, and nicotine there wakes them from sevoflurane — the switch-like evidence the paper then qualifies — “For example, GABA agonists (mimicking anesthetic action) injected into the intralaminar nuclei cause rats to rapidly fall asleep, with a corresponding slowing of the EEG ( 26 ). Conversely, rats under anesthetic concentrations of sevoflurane can be awakened by a minute injection of nicotine into the intralaminar thalamus ( 27 ).”
findingcomputer simulations show a rapid state transition at a critical anesthetic dose, matching the abruptness of clinical loss of consciousness — “Indeed, computer simulations demonstrate a rapid state transition at a critical anesthetic dose ( 59 ), consistent with a breakdown in network integration.”
findingpatients can be aware and responsive during surgery while the bispectral index says otherwise — “The isolated forearm technique has shown that individual patients can be aware and responsive during surgery even though their bispectral index value suggests they are not ( 25 ).”
