phenomenongender gap in voting
claim-typemechanism
mechanismModernization converges men's and women's roles through the paid labour market, education and changed family structure, which in turn changes their political preferences — “In postindustrial societies gender roles have increasingly converged due to a structural revolution in the paid labor force, in educational opportunities for women, and in the characteristics of modern families.”
mechanismValue change — the spread of postmaterialist and egalitarian/feminist attitudes — pulls women's vote leftwards — “That is, women in advanced industrialized societies are shifting left because of a broad process of value changes, particularly the shift towards more egalitarian attitudes associated with postmaterialism and feminism.”
mechanismGenerational turnover: as younger cohorts replace older ones the aggregate gap keeps moving left — “If this is a generational rather than a life-cycle effect, as seems most likely, it suggests that the process of generational turnover will probably continue to move women leftwards.”
mechanismOccupational segregation concentrates working women in low-paid and public-sector jobs, encouraging support for parties of the left — “Women’s support for parties of the left may be encouraged by pervasive patterns of horizontal and vertical occupational segregation.”
relation — “By the 1990s these predictions appear to have been confirmed.”
relation — “What this suggests, as Studlar, McAllister and Hayes (1998) noted in their comparison of the United States, Australia, and Britain, is that the modern gender gap is more strongly the product of attitudinal rather than structural variables.”
replicationuntested
limitationThe survey data do not cover consistent over-time trends for most of the countries compared, so the wider comparison is cross-sectional (1990s only). — “We lack evidence in the World Values Survey for consistent trends over time in a wider range of societies but we can compare the size and direction of the voting gap in the 1990s in the 36 countries shown in Table 3 and Figure 2.”
limitationNo systematic comparative evidence exists before the 1980s, so long-term secular change has to be inferred from age-cohort differences rather than observed directly. — “Although we lack systematic comparative evidence in the World Values Survey from the era prior to the 1980s, we would expect to find long-term secular trends reflected in differences between the respective age cohorts, with the younger cohort of women most strongly influenced by the transformation of sex roles in advanced industrial societies.”
limitationThe analysis covers only one dimension of a multidimensional phenomenon: vote choice, not partisanship, attitudes, or civic engagement. — “We recognize that the term “gender gap” concerns a multidimensional political phenomenon that can refer to any political differences between women and men, such as in their voting behavior, partisanship, attitudes and opinions, or civic engagement, at mass or elite levels, but the concern of this article is restricted to comparing voting choices.”
limitationThe realignment is uneven across countries and may reflect country-specific circumstances the theory does not model. — “It should be stressed that the process is far from uniform, probably reflecting particular circumstances within each country, such as the pattern of party competition, the predominant issue agenda, and the strength of the organized women’s movement.”
limitationThe headline realignment is explicitly not global, although the title says global perspective: the old pattern is reported as persisting in postcommunist and developing societies. — “The modern gender gap is not confined to the United States, as particularistic accounts suggest, but is also evident by the 1990s in some West European states. Nevertheless this pattern was not yet evident in postcommunist societies or developing societies”
limitationSympathy for feminist ideals is measured by two proxies chosen as indicators, so the construct is interpretive rather than observed. — “the size of the gender gap controlling for the effects of confidence in the women’s movement and also attitudes towards abortion, understood as key indicators of sympathy for feminist ideals”

content-observations: finding · quantity · variable · definition · key-term — grounded in the full text
findingBy the 1990s women in postindustrial societies voted significantly further to the left than men, and the difference survived a battery of social controls. — “By the 1990s women voters in these nations proved significantly more leftwing than men, even after introducing a range of social controls.”
findingThe leftward shift did not appear in postcommunist or developing societies, where women were still voting further right than men in the mid-1990s. — “Nevertheless this pattern was not yet evident in postcommunist societies or developing societies, where the traditional gender gap persists into the mid-1990s with women voters more rightwing than men.”
findingAveraged by type of society, the voting gap was +.10 (women left) in advanced industrialized democracies, -.08 in postcommunist societies and -.14 in developing societies (women right). — “Across all advanced industrialized democracies the gender gap in voting was +.10 (with women leaning left), whereas it was –.08 in postcommunist societies, and –.14 in developing societies (with women leaning right).”
findingIn postindustrial societies the modern gender gap survives structural controls but shrinks once cultural attitudes are added to the model. — “In postindustrial societies the modern gender gap persists even after introducing a range of social controls but the size of the gap diminishes once we take into account cultural factors.”
findingThe gap is therefore attributed mainly to women's and men's differing value orientations — postmaterialism and support for the women's movement — rather than to differences in their lifestyles. — “This finding suggests that the modern gender gap is more strongly the product of cultural differences between women and men in their value orientations, especially attitudes towards postmaterialism and the women’s movement, rather than differences in their lifestyles.”
findingWithin established democracies the gap reverses across age groups: in the youngest cohort women are far to the left of men, while among the over-65s women are more conservative than men. — “In established democracies the pattern of the gender gap by age group did prove to be an important indicator of generational change: among the youngest group women are far more left wing than men (see Figure 4) whereas among the over-65s the gender gap reversed, with women more conservative.”
findingCountry by country, the modern gap held up under structural controls in the United States, France and Germany, but only the United States kept a significant gap after cultural attitudes were controlled. — “The modern gender gap was maintained in the United States, France, and Germany after introducing structural controls, but it became insignificant in every country except the United States once we controlled for cultural attitudes.”
findingOn ideological self-placement in the 1990s, women were significantly more right wing than men in only six of 57 nations, overturning the old orthodoxy of female conservatism. — “The results confirm that by the 1990s women were significantly more right wing than men in only a few societies (six out of 57 nations).”
findingIn Europe the convergence came from women moving left while men stayed put: the mean male position was essentially unchanged from 1973 to 1999 while the mean female position moved from 5.61 to 5.20. — “The mean position of women changed from 5.61 in 1973 to 5.20 in 1999.”
findingThe American gender gap is not exceptional in size; larger ideological gaps appear in Germany, the Netherlands and Denmark. — “Although this phenomenon has received most attention in the United States, it is striking that the size of the ideological gap there is far from exceptional, since even larger gaps are evident in comparable democracies like Germany, the Netherlands, and Denmark.”

definitiontraditional gender gap — “women were found to be more apt than men to support center–right parties in Western Europe and in the United States, a pattern which we can term the traditional gender gap”
definitionmodern gender gap — “During the last decade, however, there has been much speculation, although little concrete evidence, that women were continuing to realign towards the left throughout advanced industrial societies, a situation that we will term the modern gender gap, replicating the pattern evident since the early 1980s in the United States.”
definitiondevelopmental theory of the gender gap — “Our argument is based on a developmental theory of the gender gap suggesting that long-term structural and cultural trends, which have transformed women’s and men’s lives, have gradually produced realignment in gender politics in postindustrial societies.”
