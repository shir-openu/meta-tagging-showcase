phenomenongender gap in voting— “The analysis demonstrates that gender differences in voting behavior have been realigning in postindustrial societies.”
claim-typemechanism— “Our argument is based on a developmental theory of the gender gap suggesting that long-term structural and cultural trends, which have transformed women’s and men’s lives, have gradually produced realignment in gender politics in postindustrial societies.”
method — evidence— “For data we draw on the World Values Surveys (WVS), carried out in three waves in the early 1980s, the early 1990s, and the mid-1990s.”
mechanismModernization converges men's and women's roles through the paid labour market, education and changed family structure, which in turn changes their political preferences— “In postindustrial societies gender roles have increasingly converged due to a structural revolution in the paid labor force, in educational opportunities for women, and in the characteristics of modern families.”
mechanismValue change — the spread of postmaterialist and egalitarian/feminist attitudes — pulls women's vote leftwards— “That is, women in advanced industrialized societies are shifting left because of a broad process of value changes, particularly the shift towards more egalitarian attitudes associated with postmaterialism and feminism.”
mechanismGenerational turnover: as younger cohorts replace older ones the aggregate gap keeps moving left— “If this is a generational rather than a life-cycle effect, as seems most likely, it suggests that the process of generational turnover will probably continue to move women leftwards.”
mechanismOccupational segregation concentrates working women in low-paid and public-sector jobs, encouraging support for parties of the left— “Women’s support for parties of the left may be encouraged by pervasive patterns of horizontal and vertical occupational segregation.”
extends Inglehart (1977) and Norris (1988) predictions that women's relative conservatism would disappear— “By the 1990s these predictions appear to have been confirmed.”
builds-on Studlar, McAllister and Hayes (1998)— “What this suggests, as Studlar, McAllister and Hayes (1998) noted in their comparison of the United States, Australia, and Britain, is that the modern gender gap is more strongly the product of attitudinal rather than structural variables.”
sensegender gap - TRADITIONAL: women voting more conservatively and participating less than men, the orthodoxy the article sets out to test— “Studies carried out in many countries in previous decades found that women were more conservative than men and less likely to participate in politics.”
sensegender gap - MODERN: women voting to the LEFT of men, the opposite sign under the same term, reported for postindustrial societies by the 1990s— “By the 1990s women voters in these nations proved significantly more leftwing than men, even after introducing a range of social controls.”
sensesympathy for feminist ideals - operationalised as two survey items: confidence in the women's movement and attitudes towards abortion— “the size of the gender gap controlling for the effects of confidence in the women’s movement and also attitudes towards abortion, understood as key indicators of sympathy for feminist ideals”
controlstructural controls were introduced and the gender gap re-estimated in each country — partial — rules out that the modern gender gap is a by-product of structural differences - education, income, labour-force participation - rather than a value shift— “The modern gender gap was maintained in the United States, France, and Germany after introducing structural controls, but it became insignificant in every country except the United States once we controlled for cultural attitudes.”
edgetraditional gender gap —contrasted-with→ modern gender gap— “Here, it is examined whether this traditional gender gap persists today, or whether gender cleavages in the electorate have converged, and whether the phenomenon of the modern gender gap, with women more left wing, has become evident elsewhere.”

limitationThe survey data do not cover consistent over-time trends for most of the countries compared, so the wider comparison is cross-sectional (1990s only).— “We lack evidence in the World Values Survey for consistent trends over time in a wider range of societies but we can compare the size and direction of the voting gap in the 1990s in the 36 countries shown in Table 3 and Figure 2.”

limitationThe analysis covers only one dimension of a multidimensional phenomenon: vote choice, not partisanship, attitudes, or civic engagement.— “We recognize that the term “gender gap” concerns a multidimensional political phenomenon that can refer to any political differences between women and men, such as in their voting behavior, partisanship, attitudes and opinions, or civic engagement, at mass or elite levels, but the concern of this article is restricted to comparing voting…
limitationThe realignment is uneven across countries and may reflect country-specific circumstances the theory does not model.— “It should be stressed that the process is far from uniform, probably reflecting particular circumstances within each country, such as the pattern of party competition, the predominant issue agenda, and the strength of the organized women’s movement.”
limitationThe headline realignment is explicitly not global, although the title says global perspective: the old pattern is reported as persisting in postcommunist and developing societies.
limitationSympathy for feminist ideals is measured by two proxies chosen as indicators, so the construct is interpretive rather than observed.
