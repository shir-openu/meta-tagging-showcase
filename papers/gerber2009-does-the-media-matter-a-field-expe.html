phenomenonmedia effects on voting behavior
claim-typeexistence
transferfrom psychology: The online-processing model of attitude formation, taken from psychological work on impression formation and persuasion, is used to explain why candidate preference moved without any measurable gain in factual knowledge — “Drawing on work from psychology (Norman H. Anderson and Stephen Hubert 1963, and W. A. Watts and W. J. McGuire 1964), political scientists have constructed models of online processing of political information”
mechanismOnline processing: readers update their candidate evaluations as information arrives but do not retain the facts that moved them, which is why opinions shift while measured knowledge does not — “political scientists have constructed models of online processing of political information, where citizens update their judgments in response to the flow of information but do not retain memory of the particular facts that caused them to revise their views”
mechanismShared content beats slant: both papers covered war casualties and administration scandals during a period bad for Republicans, so what the two papers had in common moved readers in the same direction — “It may be that what the coverage had in common was more important than any differences between the newspapers.”
mechanismContinued exposure after the election — leftover subscription weeks plus voluntary renewals — carries the treatment forward and explains why turnout moved in 2006 but not 2005 — “This could be a result of the post-election receipt of the remainder of the ten-week newspaper subscriptions or the treatment that resulted from the 17 percent of the Post treatment group who renewed their subscription after the free period ended.”
relation — “Our finding that turnout among the treatment groups was about 3 percentage points higher than the control group is consistent with previous work showing positive turnout effects from newspaper reading (Gentzkow 2006) and suggests newspaper exposure might have an important long term effect on the level of political interest.”
differs inmedia slant → media exposure — WHICH ONE THE EXPERIMENT COULD MOVE THE OUTCOME WITH. Both papers - the conservative Times and the liberal Post - produced more support for the DEMOCRATIC candidate, so the effect cannot be attributed to slant. The design separates the two by giving opposite slants and finding the same direction — “We conducted a field experiment to measure the effect of exposure to newspapers on political behavior and opinion. Before the 2005 Virginia gubernatorial election, we randomly assigned individuals to a Washington Post free subscription treatment, a Washington Times free subscription treatment, or a control treatment. We find no effect of either paper on political knowledge, stated opinions, or turnout in post-election survey and voter data. However, receiving either paper led to more support for the Democratic candidate, suggesting that media slant mattered less in this case than media exposure. Some evidence from voting records also suggests that receiving either paper led to increased 2006 voter turnout”
replicationuntested
limitationSmall sample, so treatment effects are estimated imprecisely. — “Our study has some important limitations, the most important of which is the relatively small sample size.”
limitationDelivery was randomised but actual reading could not be verified. — “One limitation of this study is that while we know which households received newspapers, we cannot be sure that the newspapers were read.”
limitationAttrition on unobservables could bias the estimates. — “If there is attrition based on unobserved variables that are correlated with the outcome measures but not predicted by the observables, our results may be biased.”
limitationThe sample frame excludes existing subscribers, so the estimated effect applies to an unusually low-media-exposure population. — “We are studying individuals who do not already subscribe to a newspaper, hence are examining the effect of exposing individuals who, on average, are less exposed to the media than the average individual.”
limitationThe turnout effect appeared only in 2006 and was of borderline significance, so both the effect and its mechanism are held provisional. — “Therefore, both the existence of an effect and its potential mechanism should be treated guardedly until further study.”
limitationWhat was randomised is delivery, not reading: the authors state they cannot be sure the newspapers were read. — “One limitation of this study is that while we know which households received newspapers, we cannot be sure that the newspapers were read.”
limitationThe authors name small sample size as their most important limitation, with consequences for the standard errors. — “Our study has some important limitations, the most important of which is the relatively small sample size. The sample size for the post-election survey was 1,081 interviews and the sample size for the data merged with administrative voting records is still only 2,571.”
limitationThe orthogonality claim is conditional: unobservables correlated with the outcome but unpredicted by the observables would still bias the result. — “based on unobserved variables that are correlated with the outcome measures but not predicted by the observables, our results may be biased.”
limitationJust under a third of the assigned sample supplied the survey outcomes. — “During the week after the November election, we reinterviewed 1,081 of the 3,347 individuals in our sample for the follow-up survey.”
limitationWHAT WAS RANDOMISED IS DELIVERY, NOT READING, and the authors say so plainly: they know which households received newspapers but cannot be sure the newspapers were read. The treatment the design controls is not the treatment the theory is about, and the gap is bridged by three self-reported survey measures. — “One limitation of this study is that while we know which households received newspapers, we cannot be sure that the newspapers were read”

content-observations: finding · quantity · variable · definition · key-term — grounded in the full text
findingTen weeks of a free newspaper subscription had no detectable effect on political knowledge, stated opinions, or turnout in the 2005 election. — “We find no effect of either paper on political knowledge, stated opinions, or turnout in post-election survey and voter data.”
findingReceiving either newspaper increased support for the Democratic candidate, so exposure to news rather than the paper's slant drove the effect. — “However, receiving either paper led to more support for the Democratic candidate, suggesting that media slant mattered less in this case than media exposure.”
findingReceiving the Post raised the probability of voting for the Democrat by 11.2 percentage points among those who voted and by 7.2 percentage points across all respondents. — “Getting the Post is estimated to increase the probability of selecting the Democrat by 11.2 percentage points ( p , 0.014) among those who reported voting (column 4) and by 7.2 percentage points ( p , 0.043) across all respondents.”
findingThe conservative Washington Times also raised the probability of a Democratic vote, contrary to what a slant-based account predicts. — “Contrary to initial expectations, the right leaning Times was also associated with an increase in the probability of a Democratic vote in the Virginia governor’s race.”
findingThe estimated effects of the two papers cannot be statistically distinguished from one another. — “The difference between the Post and Times point estimates is not statistically significant.”
findingAdministrative records show turnout in the 2006 election 2.8 percentage points higher in the treatment groups, an effect of only borderline significance. — “In November 2006 (column 3), however, the administrative data show a 2.8 percentage point higher voter turnout, with a standard error of 1.6 percentage points (p , 0.11).”
findingNeither newspaper improved respondents' ability to answer factual questions about recent news, individually or in aggregate. — “Neither of the newspapers improved the subject’s ability to answer factual questions about the recent news, and no effect is found in aggregate.”
findingAbout 17 percent of the Post treatment group chose to buy a subscription after the free ten weeks ended, evidence that the papers were not simply thrown away. — “The Post informed us that, as of March 2006 (three months after the free subscription ended), approximately 17 percent of the treatment group had decided to subscribe to the Post.”
findingRandomization succeeded: no baseline characteristic differed significantly across the three groups, the weakest balance being on gender. — “The lowest p-value for a test of independence across groups was 0.18 (for gender).”
findingThe study is underpowered for small effects: turnout effects below 3.6 points, Democratic-vote effects below 7 points, and Post-versus-Times differences below 8.6 points could not have been detected. — “Our standard errors are such that we fail to reject (two-sided, at the five percent level) the null hypothesis of no effect if we obtain a treatment effect estimate of less than a 3.6 percentage point change in voter turnout (using state voting data), a 7 percentage point increase in likelihood of voting for the Democratic candidate (using survey data), or less than an 8.6 percentage point differential between the Post and Times groups in likelihood of voting for the Democratic candidate.”
quantity3,347 — Households that reported taking neither newspaper at baseline and were randomized into the three arms — “From the 3,347 households reporting that they received neither the Post nor the Times, we randomly assigned households to get subscriptions to either the Post or the Times for approximately ten weeks, or to a control group that was not sent either paper.”
