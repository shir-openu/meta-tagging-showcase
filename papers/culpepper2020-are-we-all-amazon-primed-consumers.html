transferfrom psychology / social-science research on identity priming: The concept of priming - that individuals hold multiple identities whose salience can be cued, thereby shifting their political preferences - is imported to explain when consumers defend or abandon platform firms.— “Our theoretical position draws on the priming literature in social science, which posits that individuals have multiple identities, the salience of which can influence their political preferences on a given political issue.”
transferfrom European Union integration studies: The permissive-consensus model of EU public opinion is borrowed as the analogy for how consumer support licenses platform firms until salient issues break it.— “The orientation of today’s consumers with respect to companies enjoying platform power can perhaps best be made by analogy to what Hooghe and Marks (2009) called the permissive consensus in public opinion.”
mechanismConsumer appreciation verging on dependence on platform convenience translates market power into political clout, because politicians anticipate the backlash from depriving constituents of that convenience— “The mechanism that translates market power into political clout, we argue, flows from the appreciation, verging on dependence, that consumers have for the convenience these companies provide.”
mechanismWhich of a person's identities is made salient shifts their regulatory preferences: priming the citizen identity produces sociotropic preferences, priming the consumer identity produces a narrow focus on self-interest— “Priming citizen identity can lead to more sociotropic preferences, while priming consumer identity can lead to a narrower focus on individual self-interest.”
mechanismPlatform power operates automatically in politicians' minds as anticipated political fallout, not as fear of disinvestment or job loss— “The power these companies wield operates not through politician’s fear of the pain that these firms can visit upon the economy so much as the anticipated political fallout to which overeager regulators would expose themselves by messing with the infrastructure of people’s lives.”
mechanismA TERM IS COINED IN ONE SENTENCE AND GIVEN A STRUCTURAL DEFINITION IN THE NEXT: this article articulates a distinctive source of political influence of some technology firms, **WHICH WE CALL PLATFORM POWER**. The coinage is announced as the article's contribution, so the word arrives already carrying the claim it was invented to make— “This article articulates a distinctive source of political influence of some technology firms, which we call platform power.”
mechanismAND THE DEFINITION IS A STRUCTURAL POSITION RATHER THAN A PROPERTY OF THE FIRM: platform power **INHERES IN COMPANIES OF ECONOMIC SCALE THAT PROVIDE THE TERMS OF ACCESS** through which large numbers of consumers access goods, services, and information. What the term denotes is a place in a structure - who controls the terms of access - not a size, a sector, or a behaviour— “Platform power inheres in companies of economic scale that provide the terms of access through which large numbers of consumers access goods, services, and information.”
mechanismTHE TERM IS THEN DEFINED BY WHAT IT IS NOT, RULING OUT THE FIELD'S TWO STANDARD MECHANISMS BY NAME: firms with platform power benefit from a deference from policymakers, but this deference **IS NOT PRIMARILY A FUNCTION OF DIRECT INFLUENCE THROUGH LOBBYING OR CAMPAIGN CONTRIBUTIONS**, **NOR DOES IT COME FROM THE THREAT OF DISINVESTMENT**. The new construct is positioned by excluding the two things political economy already had words for— “Firms with platform power benefit from a deference from policymakers, but this deference is not primarily a function of direct influence through lobbying or campaign contributions, nor does it come from the threat of disinvestment.”
mechanismTHE THRESHOLD FOR THE TERM IS SET DELIBERATELY BELOW THE LEGAL ONE, IN A PARENTHETICAL: platform firms that achieve **A CERTAIN ECONOMIC SCALE - WHICH NEED NOT REACH MONOPOLY PROPORTIONS -** benefit from the direct relationship they enjoy to a large number of consumers **WHO RELY ON THE PLATFORM AS IT BECOMES INTEGRATED INTO THE FABRIC OF THEIR DAILY LIVES**. The concept is built to apply where antitrust does not reach, and the gap is stated rather than left to be noticed— “We argue that platform firms that achieve a certain economic scale – which need not reach monopoly proportions – benefit from the direct relationship they enjoy to a large number of consumers who rely on the platform as it becomes integrated into the fabric of their daily lives.”
mechanismA PRIOR DISTINCTION IS ATTRIBUTED TO THE SCHOOL THAT BUILT IT, AS SCAFFOLDING: **MARXIST SCHOLARS BUILT SOME OF THE SCAFFOLDING FOR THESE MODERN DEBATES BY DISTINGUISHING BETWEEN THE INSTRUMENTAL AND STRUCTURAL POWER OF BUSINESS**. The two inherited terms are named, dated and credited before the paper argues that neither covers its case— “Marxist scholars built some of the scaffolding for these modern debates by distinguishing between the instrumental and structural power of business (e.g., Miliband 1969, Poulantzas 1972, Block 1980).”
mechanismAND THE INHERITED VOCABULARY IS DECLARED INSUFFICIENT RATHER THAN WRONG: however, **IT IS NOT AT ALL CLEAR THAT THE TOOLS AND STRATEGIES OF INSTRUMENTAL POWER REPRESENT THE PRINCIPAL FORM OF INFLUENCE THESE COMPANIES WIELD**. The older term is left standing and denied primacy, which is a different move from refuting it— “However, it is not at all clear that the tools and strategies of instrumental power represent the principal form of influence these companies wield.”
mechanismAN ASSUMPTION IS ATTRIBUTED TO THREE RIVAL APPROACHES AT ONCE, AND IT IS THE ASSUMPTION THE PAPER DENIES: **LEGAL APPROACHES THAT STRESS MONOPOLY CONTROL ALONE, JUST AS INSTRUMENTAL AND STRUCTURAL POWER APPROACHES, ASSUME THAT ECONOMIC INCENTIVES ARE FOREMOST IN THE MINDS OF POLICYMAKERS** who are considering adopting regulations that constrain business. One premise is extracted from three literatures and named as the thing they share— “Legal approaches that stress monopoly control alone, just as instrumental and structural power approaches, assume that economic incentives are foremost in the minds of policymakers who are considering adopting regulations that constrain business.”
mechanism`BY DEFINITION` IS USED TO CONCEDE A GENERAL TRUTH AND THEN SET IT ASIDE: **CONSUMERS ARE BY DEFINITION IMPORTANT TO ANY CAPITALIST ENTERPRISE**, but the relationship to today's dominant platforms **GOES BEYOND FAMILIAR MODELS OF CONSUMER LOYALTY OR COST-BASED CHOICE**. The phrase marks what does not need arguing so that the contrast can carry the argument— “Consumers are by definition important to any capitalist enterprise, but the relationship to today’s dominant platforms goes beyond familiar models of consumer loyalty or cost-based choice.”
mechanismAND THE PAPER REPLACES ONE OF ITS OWN WORDS MID-ARGUMENT, SAYING WHICH ONE IT MEANS: once these firms reach scale, **IT IS NOT SO MUCH CONSUMER LOYALTY THAT INSPIRES THE COALITION BETWEEN USERS AND PLATFORM AS CONSUMER DEPENDENCE**. Two candidate terms for one relationship, and the substitution is performed in the open rather than by drifting usage— “Once these firms reach scale, it is not so much consumer loyalty that inspires the coalition between users and platform as consumer dependence.”
mechanismDEPENDENCE IS OPERATIONALISED BY A BEHAVIOURAL CONTRAST CONCRETE ENOUGH TO CHECK: **CONSUMERS LOVE LEGO BUT THEY ARE NOT DEPENDENT ON THE COMPANY IN THE SAME WAY** that they are dependent on Amazon, **WHICH ACTUALLY ALLOWS THEM TO ORDER THEIR LEGOS WITHOUT HAVING TO CHANGE OUT OF THEIR PAJAMAS**. Affection and dependence are separated by what the firm lets you avoid doing, and the test is a scene rather than a measure— “By contrast, consumers love Lego but they are not dependent on the company in the same way that they are dependent on, say, Amazon, which actually allows them to order their Legos without having to change out of their pajamas.”
mechanismTHE DIFFERENCE FROM EARLIER DOMINANT FIRMS IS LOCATED IN THE CONNECTION, NOT THE SIZE: **UNLIKE PREVIOUS GENERATIONS OF LARGE COMPANIES ON WHICH THE BROADER ECONOMY CAME TO DEPEND**, such as railways or utility companies, today's largest platforms enjoy **A TIGHT, EVEN INTIMATE, CONNECTION TO THEIR USERS**. The comparison class is named - railways, utilities - so the novelty claim can be checked against it— “Unlike previous generations of large companies on which the broader economy came to depend, such as railways or utility companies, today’s largest platforms enjoy a tight, even intimate, connection to their users.”
builds-on Hooghe and Marks (2009), permissive consensus— “The orientation of today’s consumers with respect to companies enjoying platform power can perhaps best be made by analogy to what Hooghe and Marks (2009) called the permissive consensus in public opinion.”
builds-on Naoi and Kume (2015) on priming consumer identity and trade preferences— “Empirical support for this proposition can be found in the area of international trade preferences, where Naoi and Kume (2015) show that the priming of consumer identities in Japan raised support for free trade by up to 9 percent, compared to a control group.”
senseplatform power - NOT A THING ANYONE DOES: it is 'LARGELY AUTOMATIC - IT HAPPENS IN THE MINDS OF POLITICIANS', an anticipated reaction rather than an act of lobbying, disinvestment or campaign spending. A power exercised by nobody, which is what makes it hard to observe and easy to deny— “In common with Lindblom’s (1982) punishing recoil mechanism, platform power is largely automatic – it happens in the minds of politicians. The difference, though, is that in the case of platform power, the potential loss is not disinvestment or job loss.”
sensethe resource that carries the power - CONSUMERS, NOT MONEY OR JOBS: firms 'benefit from THE TACIT ALLEGIANCE OF CONSUMERS, who can prove a formidable source of opposition to regulations that threaten these platforms'. The asset is an alliance the firm does not have to organise and cannot fully control— “Companies with platform power instead benefit from the tacit allegiance of consumers, who can prove a formidable source of opposition to regulations that threaten these platforms.”

Both work the same way - 'In common with Lindblom's (1982) punishing recoil mechanism, PLATFORM POWER IS LARGELY AUTOMATIC - IT HAPPENS IN THE MINDS OF POLITICIANS.' Two powers with one mechanism and different currencies, which is why a measure built for disinvestment threat would score platform firms at zero— “In common with Lindblom’s (1982) punishing recoil mechanism, platform power is largely automatic – it happens in the minds of politicians.
