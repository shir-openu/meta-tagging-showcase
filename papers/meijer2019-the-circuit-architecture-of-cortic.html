method — evidence— “In this review, we expand the framework on the circuit-level mechanisms of cortical multisensory processing by highlighting that multisensory processing is a family of functions – rather than a single operation – which involves not only the integration but also the segregation of modalities.”
mechanismsensory noise is independent across sense organs but shared within one modality, so pooling across modalities cancels more noise and yields a bigger behavioral gain than pooling within a modality— “A possible explanation is that sensory noise is largely non-overlapping between different sensory organs whereas it is mostly shared within a specific sensory modality.”
mechanismsound enhances vision by disinhibition: A1 layer-5 axons drive layer-1 interneurons in V1, which inhibit the layer 2-3 interneurons that normally suppress pyramidal cells— “Excitatory influences were mediated by projections from layer 5 in A1 to layer 1 interneurons in V1; these interneurons would then further inhibit layer 2–3 interneurons impinging on pyramidal cells, which would thus be disinhibited, resulting in enhanced visual responses”
mechanisma cross-modal stimulus resets ongoing oscillations, imposing a cycle of high and low excitability so that later same-modality inputs are amplified or depressed according to oscillation phase— “In this way, the stimulus instantiated a rhythmic fluctuation of the excitability of the auditory cortical circuit (mediated via neurons in supragranular layers), by which responses to auditory stimuli occurring at the peaks of the oscillation were enhanced and responses occurring at the troughs were depressed.”
mechanismprefrontal input to the thalamic reticular formation sets thalamic sensory gain, gating how much of each modality reaches primary cortex and thereby implementing sensory selection— “The reticular thalamic formation acts as a relay station which – based on the inputs received from the medial prefrontal cortex – is able to selectively control thalamic sensory gain and therefore gate the level of stimulation that reaches primary cortices (Wimmer et al., 2015)”
mechanismselecting neurons at the low tail of a normal response distribution guarantees they drift upward on remeasurement, manufacturing an apparent inverse-effectiveness effect out of nothing— “Therefore, even without any quantitative difference between condition A and B, neurons that have been selected to be on the low end of the normal distribution in condition A will ‘regress to the mean’ in condition B resulting in a seemingly higher response and vice versa.”
mechanisma loud auditory distractor overshadows a concurrent visual cue, capturing processing resources and cutting the behavioral response to the visual stimulus— “This indicates that the loud auditory noise overshadowed the visual stimulus, resulting in a reduced behavioral output in response to the visual cue.”

findingThe review concludes that different multisensory processes reuse the same overlapping anatomical network but are carried out by several distinct micro-circuits running in parallel, each recruited according to stimulus features and behavioral constraints.— “We conclude that distinct multisensory processes share overlapping anatomical substrates, are implemented by diverse neuronal micro-circuitries that operate in parallel, and are flexibly recruited based on factors such as stimulus features and behavioral constraints.”
findingMultisensory processing is not one operation but a family of functions that includes segregating modalities as well as integrating them.— “In this review, we expand the framework on the circuit-level mechanisms of cortical multisensory processing by highlighting that multisensory processing is a family of functions – rather than a single operation – which involves not only the integration but also the segregation of modalities.”
findingThe outcome of multisensory processing is set jointly by physical stimulus properties (bottom-up) and by cognitive resources and situational demands (top-down).— “We pursue our main aim by considering a scheme in which the outcome of multisensory processing is dependent on both physical stimulus properties (low level factors), cognitive resources and situational demands (high level factors)”
findingOne and the same anatomical connection between primary visual and auditory cortex can produce opposite effects (suppression or enhancement) depending on stimulus volume, frequency, temporal pattern and cross-modal synchrony, so circuit anatomy alone does not fix the function.— “Thus, the same anatomical circuit in the primary sensory cortex (e.g. direct connections between V1 and A1) can harbor distinct connection patterns to exert different effects based for instance on the type (volume, frequency, temporal pattern, cross-modal synchrony) of sensory stimuli, and thus implement distinct forms of context-dependent…
findingThe two population-level mechanisms previously proposed (divisive normalization and phase resetting) cannot account for the whole of multisensory processing; a larger set of computational rules is needed.— “However, while some forms of MP (e.g. cue integration) might be fully explained by the proposed two mechanisms, we believe that a complex, diverse phenomenon such as MP can only be explained by considering a larger set of computational rules.”
findingBecause unisensory integration typically weakens neuronal responses while multisensory integration enhances them, the authors argue that separate circuits evolved for the two and that multisensory integration is not merely extra converging input.— “This indicates that different circuits evolved for multi- versus unisensory processing, and that multisensory integration cannot just be seen as a simple consequence of additional inputs converging onto multisensory neurons.”
findingCorrelating unisensory responsiveness with multisensory modulation can produce a spurious inverse-effectiveness result because the two variables are not independent and are subject to regression to the mean.— “First, computing the correlation between two variables which are not independent from one another might suffer from a ‘regression to the mean’ effect.”
findingThe authors prescribe that any observed inverse-effectiveness correlation be tested against a null distribution built by shuffling before it is claimed as real.— “First, to control for the possibility of regression to the mean, the observed correlation between unisensory responsiveness and multisensory response modulation should be tested against a null-distribution which is obtained by a shuffling procedure.”
findingUsing only simple cross-modal cues biases the field toward primary sensory cortices and hides the contribution of association cortices.— “Thus, using very simple cues in cross-modal paradigms may lead to a biased view on the involved brain areas and neuronal mechanisms, because involvement of the primary sensory cortices may be…
findingPrior multisensory experience shifts cross-modal object recognition from a feedforward route that ships information from posterior parietal to perirhinal cortex to a feedback route in which perirhinal multisensory representations act back on earlier sensory cortices.
findingConscious experience is proposed to be a higher-order form of multisensory integration that sits above cue integration, object recognition and sensory selection, none of which alone suffices for consciousness.
findingIn the authors' own mouse detection data, models in which visual and auditory inputs are combined fit behavior significantly better than a model treating the two modalities as processed independently, so the multisensory gain reflects genuine cue integration rather than redundancy.
findingRodents can combine cross-modal stimuli supra-linearly, exceeding Bayes-optimal performance, implying that cross-modal sampling is an active synergistic process rather than pure statistical fusion.
