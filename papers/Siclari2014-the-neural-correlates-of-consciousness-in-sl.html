phenomenondreaming as conscious experience during sleep— “Using high-density EEG, we contrasted the presence and absence of dreaming within NREM and REM sleep.”
claim-typeexistence— “In both NREM and REM sleep, the presence of dreaming was associated with a local decrease in low-frequency activity in posterior cortical regions.”
method — evidence— “They were performed using a 256-channel highdensity EEG (hd-EEG) system (Electrical Geodesics, Inc., Eugene, Ore.).”
mechanismConscious experience during sleep requires local activation (loss of low-frequency, bistable mode) of the posterior hot zone, which is why dreaming can occur in NREM and REM alike regardless of the EEG in the rest of cortex— “The present results resolve these paradoxes by showing that in both REM and NREM sleep, dreaming, and more generally, conscious experience during sleep, requires a localized activation of a posterior hot zone, irrespective of the EEG in the rest of the cortex.”
mechanismLow-frequency (1-4 Hz) activity indexes neuronal down-states and bistability, which block stable causal interaction between cortical areas and so abolish experience— “EEG slow frequencies between 1 and 4 Hz during sleep are associated with neuronal down-states and bistability 12, which prevent the emergence of stable causal interactions among cortical areas 13, and have been linked to the loss of consciousness 14,15.”
mechanismOnce the hot zone is out of its low-frequency mode, which specific neuronal groups fire strongly determines which contents are experienced, as in waking perception— “A plausible interpretation of this finding is that, as long as the posterior hot zone is not in a low-frequency mode that prevents experiences, the specific contents of experiences when dreaming are dictated by which specific groups of neurons fire strongly and which do not, just as in wake.”
extends the fMRI dream-content decoding study cited as ref. 43 (a wake-trained pattern classifier decoding content reported at the wake–sleep transition)— “Our results with high-density EEG extend these findings beyond falling asleep to periods of consolidated sleep, providing further evidence that dream reports reflect conscious experience during sleep rather than confabulations upon awakening 44,45.”
contradicts the influential neuroimaging-derived notion (ref. 54) that the neural correlates of consciousness correspond to a broad fronto-parietal network— “By exploiting a within state, no stimulus, no task paradigm, our findings indicate that the neural correlate of consciousness, rather than involving the broad lateral fronto-parietal network, is restricted to a posterior hot zone.”
senseabsence of a dream report - AS NO EXPERIENCE (NE): the subject reports not experiencing anything— “or not experiencing anything (no experience, NE, see Online Methods).”
senseabsence of a dream report - AS EXPERIENCE WITHOUT RECALL (DEWR): the subject reports experiencing something but cannot remember the content, which is a different state from having had none— “experiencing something but could not remember the content (dreaming experience without recall of content, DEWR)”
sensedreaming - traditionally IDENTIFIED WITH REM sleep and its wake-like high-frequency EEG, an identification the paper reports as broken because dreaming also occurs in NREM sleep— “Traditionally, dreaming has been identified with REM sleep, characterized by a wake-like, globally ‘activated’, high-frequency EEG. However, dreaming also occurs in NREM sleep, characterized by prominent low-frequency activity.”
controla longer 20-second pre-awakening window used for the experience/no-experience contrast than the 0 to -8s windows used for content-specific contrasts — held — rules out a report being assigned to the wrong stretch of sleep - subjects could tie CONTENT to a moment but not the bare presence or absence of an experience, so that contrast is given more room— “Therefore, we opted to include slightly longer timeframes (20s before the awakening) to account for possible variability when comparing DE, DEWR and NCE.”
differs inthe influential neuroimaging-derived notion (ref. 54) that the neural correlates of consciousness correspond to a broad fronto-parietal network — THE AXIS IS THE PARADIGM, AND IT IS NAMED IN THE SAME SENTENCE AS THE CONCLUSION. 'BY EXPLOITING A WITHIN STATE, NO STIMULUS, NO TASK PARADIGM, our findings indicate that the neural correlate of consciousness, RATHER THAN INVOLVING THE BROAD LATERAL FRONTO-PARIETAL NETWORK, IS RESTRICTED TO A POSTERIOR HOT ZONE.' The fronto-parietal result is not called wrong - it is attributed to paradigms that require a task and a report, which this one removes. A bare `contradicts` records a dispute about ANATOMY; the parameter shows it is a dispute about METHOD.— “By exploiting a within state, no stimulus, no task paradigm, our findings indicate that the neural correlate of consciousness, rather than involving the broad lateral fronto-parietal network, is restricted to a posterior hot zone.”— “By exploiting a within state, no stimulus, no task paradigm”

for which uselimitation — sleep stage as an indicator of whether a subject was having an experience: the paper states this cannot be determined from behavioural state, traditional EEG features or neuroimaging correlates— “Thus, whether one experiences something or not during sleep cannot be determined simply by assessing one’s behavioral state based on traditional EEG features or neuroimaging correlates”
for which uselimitation — REM sleep as the state of dreaming: the paper reports a consistent minority of REM awakenings with no reported experience, and dreaming occurring in NREM sleep— “Conversely, in a small but consistent minority of cases, subjects deny having had any experience when awakened from REM sleep.”
replicationuntested
limitationThe hot zone was identified by averaging across many awakenings and subjects, so individual episodes of experience may involve additional regions.— “Of course, since the posterior hot zone was highlighted by averaging EEG recordings preceding many awakenings from many subjects, the full neural correlates of experiences ins sleep could occasionally include additional brain regions.”
limitationParts of the posterior hot zone may support unconscious dream-generating processes rather than experience itself.— “Conversely, we cannot rule out that the posterior hot zone may include, besides brain regions supporting experiences, areas that might support unconscious aspects of dream generation.”
limitationSource modelling does not fully remove volume conduction, which inflates absolute low-frequency power values.— “Indeed, source modeling strongly reduces but does not completely eliminate the effects of volume conduction.”
limitationThe behavioural state does not settle whether there was an experience, and the paper says so of both the traditional EEG features and the neuroimaging correlates.— “Thus, whether one experiences something or not during sleep cannot be determined simply by assessing one’s behavioral state based on traditional EEG features or neuroimaging correlates”
limitationThe identification the field worked with is reported as failing in both directions: dreams are absent from some REM awakenings and present in NREM sleep.— “Conversely, in a small but consistent minority of cases, subjects deny having had any experience when awakened from REM sleep.”

findingIn both NREM and REM sleep, reporting a dream on awakening was preceded by lower low-frequency (1-4 Hz) EEG power in a localised posterior parieto-occipital region, while reporting no experience was preceded by higher low-frequency power there.— “In both NREM and REM sleep, the presence of dreaming was associated with a local decrease in low-frequency activity in posterior cortical regions.”
findingReports of having experienced something without recalling its content also showed the posterior low-frequency decrease relative to no-experience reports, so the posterior marker tracks having an experience rather than remembering it.— “Thus, decreased low-frequency power in this posterior cortical zone was correlated with the occurrence of dreaming experiences, irrespective of the ability to remember the contents of the experience.”
findingAwakenings triggered in real time by a bispectral low/high-frequency threshold over the posterior hot zone predicted the presence or absence of dreaming in NREM sleep with 87% overall accuracy (91.6% for dreaming predictions, 80.7% for no-experience predictions).— “After removal of trials containing artifact (see Online Methods), there remained 36 awakenings initiated by the algorithm with a DE prediction, out of which 33 were accurate (DE mean accuracy = 91.6%, p=0.00001), and 26 awakenings with a NE prediction, out of which 21 were accurate (NE mean accuracy = 80.7%, p=0.0003); total prediction…
findingHigh-frequency activity during REM dreams was higher in frontal regions when the dream was rated thought-like and higher in parietal, occipital and temporal regions when it was rated perceptual, an anterior-posterior gradient mirroring wakefulness.— “We found that high-frequency activity correlated with the thinking dimension in frontal brain regions and with the perceiving dimension in parietal, occipital and temporal areas (r>0.14, Fig. 4A).”
findingDreams containing faces were accompanied by increased high-frequency activity in a temporo-occipital region matching the fusiform face area, confirmed by a right-FFA region-of-interest test.— “As shown in Fig. 4B, DEs containing faces, compared to DEs without faces, were associated with increased high-frequency activity in a circumscribed temporo-occipital region that closely matched the fusiform face area (FFA).”
findingDreams with a definite spatial setting showed increased high-frequency activity in right posterior parietal cortex, an area supporting spatial perception and visuo-spatial attention in wakefulness.— “We found that DEs with a definite spatial setting were associated with increased high-frequency activity in the right posterior parietal cortex (rPPC) (Fig. 4C), an area involved in spatial perception and visuo-spatial attention 20, whose lesion can cause spatial…
findingDreams containing speech showed increased high-frequency activity in a left posterior temporal region corresponding to Wernicke's area.
findingThe results showed no reliable hemispheric asymmetry, apart from a borderline right-sided effect for the high-frequency dreaming-versus-no-experience contrast in REM sleep.
findingThe low- and high-frequency effects on dreaming survived control for participant age, with a significant main effect of report type and no age effect.
findingBecause the correlate of experience was confined to a posterior region rather than a lateral fronto-parietal network, the authors conclude the neural correlate of consciousness is posterior rather than fronto-parietal.
