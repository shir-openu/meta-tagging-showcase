phenomenonsmoothness (roughness) of the volatility process
claim-typeexistence
mechanismTHE TERM'S TWO SENSES ARE SEPARATED BY THEIR HISTORY: 'long memory' initially meant the slow decay of the autocorrelation function - anything slower than exponential - and over time acquired the more precise meaning that the function is not integrable, and more precisely still that it decays as a power law with exponent less than one — “Initially, it appears that the term long memory referred to the slow decay of the autocorrelation function (of absolute returns for example), anything slower than exponential. Over time however, it seems that this term has acquired the more precise meaning that the autocorrelation function is not integrable, see [8], and even more precisely that it decays as a power-law with exponent less than 1.”
mechanismTHE TECHNICAL SENSE IS DEFENDED AS THE ONLY ESTIMABLE ONE: the recent literature assumes long memory in the technical sense, and the authors add that meaningful results can probably ONLY be obtained under such a specification, since the asymptotic behaviour of the covariance function cannot be estimated without assuming a specific form for it — “Much of the more recent literature, for example [7, 11, 13], assumes long memory in volatility in this more technical sense. Indeed, meaningful results can probably only be obtained under such a specification, since it is not possible to estimate the asymptotic behavior of the covariance function without assuming a specific form.” [checked against the archived source, not this rendering]
mechanismTHE QUESTION IS DECLARED UNANSWERABLE AS POSED, and the authors then supply a different instrument rather than a different answer: stated in terms of the asymptotic behaviour of the autocorrelation function the long memory question simply cannot be answered, but explicit expressions still allow the dependence structure to be analysed — “that when stated in term of the asymptotic behavior of the autocorrelation function, the long memory question can simply not be answered. Nevertheless, we are able to provide explicit expressions enabling us to analyze thoroughly the dependence structure of the volatility process.” [checked against the archived source, not this rendering]
mechanismA WIDELY ACCEPTED STYLIZED FACT IS EXPLAINED AS AN ARTEFACT OF ITS OWN DETECTOR: the model has no long memory property, and yet the classical statistical procedures designed to detect volatility persistence TEND TO CONCLUDE THAT LONG MEMORY IS PRESENT in data the model generated — “Our main finding is that although the RFSV model does not have any long memory property, classical statistical procedures aiming at detecting volatility persistence tend to conclude the presence of long memory in data generated from it. This sheds new light on the supposed long memory in the volatility of financial data.”
mechanismthe evidence for the stylized fact is dated rather than dismissed: the analyses supporting it use data that PREDATE high-frequency electronic trading, and the authors note the evidence has never satisfied the remaining doubters — “Nevertheless, analyses such as that of Andersen et al. [3] use data that predate the advent of high-frequency electronic trading, and the evidence for long memory has never been sufficient to satisfy remaining doubters” [checked against the archived source, not this rendering]
mechanismTHE NEW NAME IS CHOSEN TO CARRY THE DEPARTURE: an existing model is adopted and renamed, with the new word picked to underline the single parameter inequality that separates it from its parent — “Specifically we adopt the fractional stochastic volatility (FSV) model of Comte and Renault [16]. We call our model Rough FSV (RSFV) to underline that, in contrast to FSV, we take H < 1/2.”
mechanismin the process used, ONE PARAMETER CONTROLS BOTH PROPERTIES AT ONCE - regularity and long memory stand in one-to-one correspondence through the Hurst parameter - so a claim about smoothness is inseparably a claim about memory — “Note that in the case of the fBM, there is a one to one correspondence between regularity and long memory through the Hurst parameter H.”
mechanismA WIDESPREAD BELIEF IS MET WITH A COUNTEREXAMPLE RATHER THAN AN ARGUMENT: the model is offered as a counterexample to the widespread belief that the explosion of the volatility smile at short expiries implies the presence of jumps — “This is interesting in and of itself in that it provides a counterexample to the widespread belief that the explosion of the volatility smile as τ → 0 (as clearly seen in Figures 1.1 and 1.2) implies the presence of jumps” [checked against the archived source, not this rendering]
mechanismTHE MODEL IS OFFERED AGAINST TWO SEPARATE REQUIREMENTS rather than one - consistency with the empirically observed properties of the volatility time series AND consistency with the shape of the volatility surface - and the claim is stated as POTENTIAL, not as achievement — “The volatility model that we will specify in Section 3.1, driven by fBM with H < 1/2, therefore has the potential to be not only consistent with the empirically observed properties of the volatility time series but also consistent with the shape of the volatility surface.”
mechanismthe smoothness estimate is reported as a RANGE ACROSS ASSETS rather than a single fitted number, and it is accompanied by two further empirical properties of the increments - approximate normality and a monofractal scaling of the moments - which together are what licenses the modelling choice — “In Section 2, we report our estimates of the smoothness of the log-volatility for selected assets. This smoothness parameter lies systematically between 0.08 and 0.2 (in the sense of H¨older regularity for example). Furthermore, we find that increments of the log-volatility are approximately normally distributed and that their moments enjoy a remarkable monofractal scaling property. This leads us to model the log of volatility using a fBM with Hurst parameter H < 1/2 in Section 3.” [checked against the archived source, not this rendering]

findingFrom high-frequency data, log-volatility behaves like a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst exponent H≈0.1 (H<1/2, rough) at all reasonable time scales — motivating the Rough FSV (RFSV) model, which fits financial time series and improves realized-volatility forecasts. — “Our main result is that log-volatility behaves essentially as a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst exponent H of order 0.1, at any reasonable time scale... We call our model Rough FSV (RFSV) to underline that, in contrast to FSV, H < 1/2.” [checked against the archived source, not this rendering]

This sheds light on why long memory of volatility has been widely accepted as a stylized fact.”
