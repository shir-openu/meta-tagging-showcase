The concept of stigmergyterm·dom originated from the study of coordination in groups of animals [ 3 ]. For example, ants release pheromones in the environment [ 4 ] to guide other ants back to the nest once they find food [ 5 ], or to cooperatively transport large food items [ 6 ]. Likewise, pumas and other large Felidae leave tracesterm·dom in the environment to mark territory so that other conspecifics would avoid the area [ 7 ]. In animal colonies that build structures [ 8 , 9 ], such as wasps [ 10 ] and termites [ 11 , 12 ], stigmergyterm·dom allows the incredible coordination of thousands of individuals to build intricate nests, without any pre-planning and central coordination.

With the advent of robotic systems, engineers have taken inspiration from stigmergyterm·dom in natural swarmsterm·key to build self-organizing groups of robots that coordinate to achieve a desired goal [ 13 – 15 ]. In stigmergyterm·dom, robots in a swarmterm·key not only coordinate to sense the environment [ 16 , 17 ], but they actively modify it to communicate with each other. Through stigmergyterm·dom, robotic swarmsterm·key can gather objects [ 18 ], sort them [ 15 ], navigate unknown environments [ 19 ], and search and track a moving target [ 20 ]. Stigmergyterm·dom is particularly promising for collective constructionterm·key, as the structure that is being built can be utilized as the stigmergic signal itself [ 21 , 22 ]. In this vein, extensions of stigmergic approaches have been proposed to achieve precise and accurate collective constructionterm·key [ 23 , 24 ]. Stigmergyterm·dom is an important coordination strategy not only in biological and robotic systems but also in so-called ‘mixed societies’ that integrate biological and robotic individuals [ 25 , 26 ]. Therein, robots are used to create new collective responses or to elicit a desired behaviour from the animal group [ 27 ]. In addition to direct social interactions between animals and robots, stigmergic stimuli released by robots have been often used to achieve the desired swarmterm·key behaviour, for example through light signals [ 28 ] or pheromone release [ 29 ] by robots.

A fundamental challenge for biologists is to reconstruct how environmental modifications implemented at an individual level, without a central coordination, trigger a complex behaviour of the swarmterm·key, such as the construction of a nest or food gathering. The literature relies on agent-based modelling grounded in individual behavioural rules [ 13 – 15 ], which do not allow a holistic analysis of the swarmterm·key. Currently, no stigmergic model allows the study of the overall distribution of environmental modifications that enable these complex behaviours. Similarly, engineers are faced with the design problem of how to control robotic agents toward achieving a desired behaviour for the swarmterm·key, be it robotic or mixed. Such a challenge requires the development of a mathematically tractable and interpretable framework for stigmergyterm·dom that could complement existing algorithmic implementations, which rely on a sequence of discrete behavioural rules implemented at the individual level.

Here, we put forward a control-oriented mathematical backdrop to holistically describe environmental modifications in animal swarmsterm·key and engineer stigmergic interactions in robotic and mixed swarmsterm·key, where units of the swarmterm·key interact with each other and with dynamic modifications of the environment that we identify as ‘tracesterm·dom’. Our mathematical framework is based on recent advancements in the field of control of large multi-agent systems, which relies on a ‘continuificationterm·key’ of the equations of motion of the swarmterm·key [ 30 , 31 ] (see figure 1 ). In this vein, rather than modelling and controlling individual motions [ 32 ], we model the swarmterm·key as a continuous fluid and describe the spatio-temporal evolution of its density, similar to thermodynamic approaches for large-scale systems [ 33 ] and former robot density control algorithms [ 34 – 38 ]. Our work differs from previous studies on continuification-based control of large swarmsterm·key [ 30 , 31 ] as we do not apply a control action to each swarmterm·key unit. In animal swarmsterm·key and mixed societies, it is not possible to directly control biological units, and even in robotic swarmsterm·key controlling each unit would require a centralized approach or, at least, some form of connectivity in the swarmterm·key [ 39 ]. Based on these groundings, we focus on the realistic case where the control engineer can only design dynamic modifications of the environment, such that no centralization or communication between the units is needed.

We examine a prototypical example of a task that can be achieved through stigmergyterm·dom: shape formation [ 21 , 40 ]. We seek to compute the distribution of tracesterm·dom that make the swarmterm·key achieve a desired formation, that is, a prescribed density profile. Once the density of tracesterm·dom is known, it is discretized to allow for deployment in a real environment, in a discrete form. We do not focus on implementation details of how such density profile of tracesterm·dom can be generated, as we concentrate on the mathematical modelling of the overarching stigmergic control strategy, generalizable to a variety of practical implementations. While one could describe the dynamics of deposition of the tracesterm·dom, such a process would be heavily context-dependent (for example, the dynamics of ants’ pheromone release [ 13 ] is different from that of robotic collective additive manufacturingterm·dom (AMterm·dom) [ 23 ]), thus hindering the generality of our theory.

We demonstrate the potential of our approach through a series of one- (1D) and two-dimensional (2D) case studies. In particular, we find that the profile of the density of the tracesterm·dom closely resembles the opposite of the desired density of the swarmterm·key, see equation (2.16) . Notably, we observe that there are areas where the density of tracesterm·dom is negative. The indeterminacy in the distribution of tracesterm·dom allows us to obtain a non-negative density. As clear from equation (2.16) , the actual density of the tracesterm·dom can always be made positive from the addition of a constant (that is, a self-equilibrating field of tracesterm·dom). From the analysis of the error over time ( figure 2 b ), we find that the error at the peak quickly decays to zero (more than 90 % of its initial value within 10 time units), while the error in the tail of the function has a longer decay time (with similar decays in 50 time units).

Also when considering the travelling waveterm·key in a non-dispersive medium ( figure 2 c ), we observe an almost perfect tracking of the desired formation ( K L < 10 − 5 after 60 time units). In this case, the density of the tracesterm·dom does not resemble the opposite profile as the instantaneous desired density, as it is corrected to anticipate the change in the desired density over time. Results in two dimensions Our approach can be easily extended to 2D and three-dimensional (3D) problems, as described in §2.6. Herein, we consider 2D simulations to show realistic applications of our method. Specifically, we consider the problem of a swarmterm·key inspired by Leonardo da Vinci’s lion robot [ 42 ]. This ‘automaton’ was designed by Leonardo to autonomously move on a trajectory, stop in front of Francis I King of France, opening its breast full of flowers, and return to its initial position. We use these steps as inspiration for a model example of coordination in 2D, where a swarmterm·key tracks and deforms over a wavy circle, similar to a flower with six lobes; see electronic supplementary material and figure 4 . Specifically, we task the swarmterm·key to aggregate in a compact formation ( figure 4 , bottom) and reach a target location by moving along the wavy circle ( figure 4 , right). Upon reaching the target location, the swarmterm·key shall change the formation (simulating the lion opening its breast; figure 4 , top) to perform a collective task (for example, collective constructionterm·key). Upon completion, the swarmterm·key shall resume its original formation (representing the lion closing its breast) and return to the starting location by moving on the wavy circle ( figure 4 , left). Videos of the simulation are in the electronic supplementary material.

In these simulations, for simplicity, we assume quasi-static changes for the desired density, whereby we assume ρ ‾ t = 0 in our computations. Similar to the 1D case, we start from a uniform distribution with unitary integral. We set the final time to t F = 300 , and divide this time-span in three phases with equal duration: translation along the first half of the wavy circle ( 0 − 100 ), opening/closing of the breast ( 100 − 200 ) and return to the initial position along the second half of the wavy circle ( 200 − 300 ). We require the swarmterm·key to rigidly translate along the trajectory while maintaining a formation described by a 2D von Mises function with k x = k y = 2 . To design the desired trajectory, we let the mean of the 2D von Mises function translate along the wavy circle. The opening and closing of the lion’s breast is simulated by dynamically varying k x of the 2D von Mises function from 2 to 3 .

Our results indicate that our approach is successful in replicating the motion of Leonardo’s automaton ( figure 4 ). Based on the KLterm·mth divergence ( figure 4 , centre), the density of the swarmterm·key quickly reaches the desired one, with overall small differences ( K L < 0.3 after 11 time units). After the initial settling, the KLterm·mth divergence is larger in the first and third phases (around 0.25 ), while it decreases in the second phase during the opening and closing of the breast (around 0.10 ). In principle, adopting a non-quasi-static approach can decrease the KLterm·mth divergence, paying the price of a higher computational cost.

Discussion and conclusions Complex systems composed of many units often display surprising self-organization phenomena [ 53 ]. Self-organization is inevitably associated with information transfer between the units of the system, which occurs through direct social interactions or indirect cues mediated by the environment [ 54 ]. Stigmergyterm·dom encapsulates the ability of the units of a system of transferring information among them by modifying the surrounding environment [ 1 ]. This concept has found a broad range of application in animal [ 2 ] and robotic [ 18 ] swarmsterm·key, mixed societies of animals and robots [ 26 ], and even human social systems [ 55 ].

A particularly challenging endeavour in biology is the analysis of how stigmergic signals left in the environment lead to self-organization and execution of complex tasks, even in the absence of central coordination. Such a challenge persists in engineering design of stigmergic modifications of the environment in order for the swarmterm·key to behave as desired. This problem requires the formulation of mathematically tractable and interpretable models to be used in the analysis of swarmsterm·key and the design of control systems. However, literature on stigmergyterm·dom typically focuses on agent-based models, where each unit of the swarmterm·key follows a set of behavioural rules. These models are difficult to use for holistic analyses of environmental modifications and control purposes.

Here, we propose a new mathematical framework to study stigmergyterm·dom in swarmsterm·key. Through this framework, we lay out how tracesterm·dom should be left in the environment to enable the coordination of a swarmterm·key, provided a mathematical model for the motion of the swarmterm·key is available. Such a framework is rooted in recent ideas for the control of large multi-agent systems [ 30 , 31 ], which draw on the analogy between robots and particles in a fluid. While for small groups of particles we can describe the motion of each individual unit, when considering large systems a continuum description is preferable. Thus, we continuify the problem by modelling the swarmterm·key as a fluid, design the control at a continuum level in terms of density of tracesterm·dom, and discretize this distribution to deploy them in the environment. This approach transforms the control problem on a large number of coupled ordinary differential equations to that on a single partial differential equation, which becomes analytically tractable.

One of the potential applications of the proposed setting is collective constructionterm·key in robotic swarmsterm·key through collective AMterm·dom, where buildings and infrastructure are built through layer-by-layer deposition [ 56 – 59 ] by a team of robots [ 60 ]. This paradigm has been inspired by other efforts in collective constructionterm·key with teams of robots using pre-made parts [ 61 , 62 ], amorphous deposition [ 63 ], or the robots themselves [ 64 ]. While initial efforts focused on ground-based robots [ 65 – 67 ], the recent breakthrough in Zhang et al. [ 68 ] demonstrated the possibility of aerial AMterm·dom for large-scale buildings and infrastructure, going far beyond previous efforts that only focused on structure assembly through drones [ 69 ]. Robots in construction teams ought to reach a specific location and coordinate with each other. Our work provides a pathway to achieve these goals, making a first step toward collective AMterm·dom algorithms.

Within the context of collective AMterm·dom, tracesterm·dom may be left in the environment by the swarmterm·key or by one or more exogenous robots, not directly involved in the construction. While stigmergic cues could be provided by the built structure itself [ 22 , 23 ], it is tenable that in many applications the traceterm·dom deposition process should be independent from the printing. A potential implementation of the traceterm·dom deposition entails the use of a separate nozzle, utilizing a coloured dissoluble material that can be identified by other robots. Tracesterm·dom can be left as signs on printed structures or as thin marks on the ground and removed after construction, similar to supporting material used in desktop 3D printers [ 70 ]. Beyond collective AMterm·dom, the tracesterm·dom needed for our algorithm may assume different forms. For example, they may represent actual fixed, physical objects, an additional swarmterm·key of mobile robots, spatially extended continuous robots that can actively deform or other environmental modifications that affect the swarmterm·key (such as changes in background lighting in light-foraging swarmsterm·key [ 71 ]).

Regardless of the application, we assume that units in the swarmterm·key are not able to distinguish between other units and tracesterm·dom. Such a possibility is in practice verified in large swarmsterm·key where individual units have limited computing power and may only perform simple operations to avoid collisions with surrounding entities, be they other units or tracesterm·dom. When a unit can discern tracesterm·dom from other units, we have an additional degree of freedom, as we can modify the type of interaction units have with tracesterm·dom. For example, should units be able to distinguish different types of tracesterm·dom, negative densities of tracesterm·dom may be implemented in practice by using tracesterm·dom encoding interactions via an attraction potential.

Interestingly, we find that the distribution of tracesterm·dom has several degrees of indeterminacy. Such indeterminacy is not a drawback, but rather an advantage of the method, as it provides additional degrees of freedom for the control and swarmterm·key designer. For example, if tracesterm·dom represent fixed, physical objects that cannot be removed or relocated after they have been released, their density can only increase over time at each point of the domain: the indeterminacy of the distribution of tracesterm·dom allows to satisfy this condition.

We demonstrate the validity of our framework in a variety of simulations in 1D and 2D. In 1D, we show that the swarmterm·key can achieve a stationary formation, and that we can make this stationary formation rigidly translate similar to a travelling waveterm·key in a non-dispersive medium. We validate our coordination strategy with even more complex scenarios, as we studied a concurrent modification of the formation during the translation of the wave, simulating a travelling waveterm·key in a dispersive medium, where different harmonics of the waves travel at a different speed, thus modifying the waveform over time. Analogous simulations at a discrete level demonstrated the validity and practical viability of our approach, even in very large-scale swarmsterm·key with thousands of units.

In 2D, we selected a historical example to show the potential of our approach. Specifically, we replicated the behaviour of Leonardo da Vinci’s lion-shaped automaton, which was designed to pay homage to the King of France through its complex movements. We showed that our approach allows the swarmterm·key to perform composite, time-modulated tasks, tracking an uneven trajectory, stopping at a precise location, modifying the formation in time, and resuming its motion along the trajectory. All these behaviours can be easily achieved through a stigmergic approach.

Our approach does not come without limitations. In particular, we acknowledge that further work is needed to support real-world implementation. The lack of an explicit model for the traceterm·dom deposition process benefits the generality of the theory, but it limits a prompt translation of the proposed approach to practice. Further, robustness of the approach to different forms of disturbances should be tested, and additional research is required for deployment in collective AMterm·dom. Further, we acknowledge that the proposed control strategy is not suitable for all collective tasks, especially for small teams in which coordination and localization within an absolute frame are more easily achieved. Nevertheless, we believe that the proposed mathematical framework offers a powerful and adaptable tool to design environmental modifications for swarmterm·key coordination, leaving designers the versatility to implement them in different ways.
