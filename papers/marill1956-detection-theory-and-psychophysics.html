phenomenonauditory signal detection in gaussian noise— “The aim of these experiments is to determine the extent to which and the manner in which subjects differ from the "ideal detector" of detection theory.”
claim-typemethod— “There do seem, however, to be certain advantages in our formulation.”
method — evidence— “The two-category symmetrical forced-choice technique was used throughout.”
transferfrom detection theory (statistical decision theory developed for radio/radar receivers): Introducing catch-trials turns the psychophysical experiment into a detection situation, so the whole apparatus of detection theory can be applied to psychophysical problems.— “is relatively new, perhaps, is the realization that introducing them brings the psychophysical experiment under the rubric of detection, thereby rendering psychophysical”
transferfrom radar/receiver detection engineering (Peterson and Birdsall): The likelihood-ratio distribution functions already computed for signals in uniform gaussian noise are taken over directly to derive the psychophysical function for a human listener.— “Fortunately, Peterson and Birdsall (15) have calculated these functions for a wide variety of signals presented in uniform gaussian noise.”
transferfrom communication theory / signal processing: The sampling theorem is imported so that a band-limited receiver input over an interval can be represented as a finite set of 2TW random variables.— “The Sampling Theorem. We shall need one form of the so-called sampling theorem”
mechanismA bandpass filter in the auditory system whose passband is about 13.5 db wider than the ideal filter admits proportionally more noise power, so the detector behaves exactly like the ideal detector but requires a correspondingly higher signal-to-noise ratio.— “a detector consisting of a filter like the ideal filter except for having a passband approximately 13. 5 db wider.”
mechanismWhen two signal components fall in non-overlapping auditory filters, the two filter outputs are statistically independent, so the two detectors' decisions are independent as well.— “the same input containing gaussian noise is applied to both filters, the outputs of the two”
mechanismA decision based on the signs of two independent detector outputs can never beat the better single detector, which is why the 500+1100 cps signal is only as detectable as its more detectable component.— “only assume that 500 cps and 1100 cps fall within the passbands of nonoverlapping filters.”
contradicts Tanner and Swets' model and traditional threshold/criterion psychophysical models— “Part I: Traditional psychophysical models and Tanner and Swets' model are critically examined.”
builds-on Peterson and Birdsall (15)— “The material for this section is drawn from Peterson and Birdsall (15).”
extends Fletcher's critical-band theory— “The auditory theory of the present section is, of course, essentially Fletcher's critical-band theory (3, 4).”

findingHuman listeners were far less sensitive than the ideal detector: the ideal detector reaches 75 per cent correct at a signal-energy-to-noise-spectrum ratio of 1.4, whereas the subjects needed a ratio of about 15, i.e. roughly 13.5 db more signal.— “As the first item of comparison we notice, and this comes as no surprise, that our subjects were not ideal detectors.”
findingOnce the ideal-detector curve was shifted rightward by a single constant, it fitted the observed psychophysical functions closely, accounting for 99.3 per cent of the variance in the best case and 97.3 per cent in the worst.— “(PFL:500 cps) accounts for 99.3 per cent of the variance in the graph; the worst-fitting”
findingApart from a constant loss of about 13.5 db in sensitivity, the subjects' detection behaviour matched the ideal detector's predicted psychophysical function for pure tones.— “the ideal detector only to the extent of being, on the average, 13. 5 db less sensitive at”
findingFor two-component signals at 1060 and 1100 cps, detectability was set by the total energy of the signal, exactly as for the ideal detector, and the energy-summation prediction accounted for 96.5 per cent of the observed variance.— “between the predicted and observed detectability; the predictions account for 96. 5 per”
findingFor two-component signals at 500 and 1100 cps the energy-summation prediction collapsed, accounting for only 25 per cent of the variance; detectability was instead set by the more detectable component alone, which accounted for 93.5 per cent.— “practically nil; the variance accounted for is only 25 per cent.”
findingPredicting the 500+1100 cps two-tone detectability from the better single component alone accounted for 93.5 per cent of the variance and a t-test found no difference between that component's detectability and the two-tone detectability.— “account for 93.5 per cent of the variance; a t-test fails to reveal any difference between”
findingTwo-component signals at 500 and 540 cps fell between the two extremes, with the energy-summation prediction accounting for 73.6 per cent of the variance.— “less than in Table 2; here the predictions account for 73. 6 per cent of the variance in”
findingFor the 1060/1100 and 500/540 pairs, adding the weaker component raised the detectability of the whole signal above that of the stronger component alone, a difference that was highly significant.— “indicating that in these cases the presence of the less detectable component”
findingThe 13.5 db loss cannot be internal noise adding linearly to external noise, because detection accuracy stays constant whenever the external signal-to-noise ratio is constant, no matter how loud the noise.— “should arrive again at a figure close to 13. 5 db.
