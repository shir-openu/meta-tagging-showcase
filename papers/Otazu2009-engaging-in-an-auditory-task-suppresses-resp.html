phenomenontask-engagement suppression of sound-evoked responses in auditory cortex— “To study these preparatory mechanisms, we compared activity in auditory cortex elicited by sounds while rats performed an auditory task (“engaged”) with activity elicited by identical stimuli while subjects were awake but not performing a task (“passive”).”
claim-typeexistence— “Surprisingly, we found that engagement suppressed responses, an effect opposite in sign to that elicited by selective attention.”
method — evidence— “After animals reached criterion performance (> 95% correct; ~1 week), we implanted movable tetrodes in the left primary auditory cortex (area A1) to record neural activity, including single unit responses, multi-unit responses and local field potentials (LFPs).”
object of study — evidence— “To study the neural correlates of one of these non-selective components in auditory cortex, we compared cortical activity elicited by auditory stimuli in animals engaged in an auditory task with activity elicited by identical stimuli when an animal was passive but wakeful; we use the term”
research question — evidence— “Attention is itself complex, consisting of a well-defined selective component and a much less well-defined component encompassing arousal, vigilance and sustained attention.”
contribution — evidence— “We propose that suppression represents the wakeful baseline condition upon which other forms of attentional and non-attentional modulation are superimposed.”
transferfrom somatosensory physiology: the whisking-versus-quiet suppression in barrel cortex, used as the cross-modal parallel for the same effect— “Synaptic depression has also been implicated in the barrel cortex, where suppression has been observed during whisking (“engaged”) compared with quiet wakefulness (“passive”), consistent with the present findings”
transferfrom synaptic physiology: short-term synaptic depression and the inverse relation between release probability and presynaptic rate, imported as the circuit explanation— “Synaptic depression of the thalamocortical inputs 15 , 35 could explain how the relative enhancement of the thalamic spontaneous rate in the engaged condition might lead to suppression of the cortical evoked response, without changing the cortical spontaneous activity.”
mechanismRaised thalamic spontaneous firing depresses thalamocortical synapses, which suppresses the cortical evoked response while leaving cortical spontaneous rate unchanged— “Synaptic depression of the thalamocortical inputs 15 , 35 could explain how the relative enhancement of the thalamic spontaneous rate in the engaged condition might lead to suppression of the cortical evoked response, without changing the cortical spontaneous activity.”
mechanismPreceding spontaneous activity leaves thalamocortical synapses in a depleted state, so identical thalamic evoked responses drive smaller cortical responses— “Furthermore, evoked responses of identical magnitude in thalamus would lead to relatively depressed responses in the cortex in the engaged condition, because the thalamocortical synapses would be in a more depleted state due to the preceding spontaneous activity (for details, see SM8: Thalamocortical depression model).”
mechanismRelease probability falls inversely with presynaptic rate, so a higher thalamic firing rate does not translate into a higher cortical spontaneous rate— “At sufficiently high firing rates, synaptic release probability is inversely proportional to the presynaptic firing rate 36 (P release ∝ 1/F thalamic ).”
mechanismSuppression functions as an early stage of routing the sensory signal to motor centres by silencing neurons irrelevant to the current task— “Task-engaged suppression may represent an initial stage of this routing, in which activity in neurons irrelevant to the task is reduced.”
mechanismthalamic spontaneous rate rises during engagement while thalamic evoked responses do not change— “In contrast to cortex, there was no difference (p=0.80) in the thalamic evoked response ( Fig. 6a ) between the engaged and passive conditions. However, the thalamic spontaneous activity showed a robust and consistent elevation in the engaged condition”
mechanismsynaptic depression at thalamocortical synapses could convert a raised thalamic spontaneous rate into a suppressed cortical evoked response without raising cortical spontaneous rate— “Synaptic depression of the thalamocortical inputs 15 , 35 could explain how the relative enhancement of the thalamic spontaneous rate in the engaged condition might lead to suppression of the cortical evoked response, without changing the cortical spontaneous activity.”
mechanismsuppression concentrates the same stimulus into fewer, harder-firing neurons - the representation becomes sparser by kurtosis— “The kurtosis of the firing rate distribution in the engaged condition was greater than in the passive condition (kurtosis engaged = 8.4 ± 2.4 vs. kurtosis passive = 4.1 ± 0.7; p <0.05; see SM5: Kurtosis of firing rate distribution), indicating that the stimulus representation in the engaged condition was sparser.”
mechanismheadphones deliver identical stimuli regardless of the animal's position, so any difference must come from the animal's state— “Because the sounds were delivered through headphones, differences in neural activity in this paradigm must be due to differences in the animal’s behavior or state, rather than to differences in the stimulus itself arising from uncontrolled changes in the sound path from speaker to the ear.”
mechanismsuppression correlates with a cell's spontaneous rate, a property independent of the stimulus used— “Interestingly, suppression was correlated with spontaneous activity, a property of the cells that does not depend on the stimulus used during the task”
mechanismsynaptic depression is argued to cover the steady-state effects, with other mechanisms left open for the first few stimuli
