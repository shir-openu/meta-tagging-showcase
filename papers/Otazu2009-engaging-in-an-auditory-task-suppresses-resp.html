replicationcontested
limitationSimultaneous multi-neuron recording meant stimuli could not be matched to each neuron's receptive field. — “Because in our experiments we recorded from several neurons simultaneously, we could not optimize the stimulus properties to match the receptive field properties of the recorded neurons.”
limitationThe paradigm differs enough from barrel-cortex whisking studies that the two sets of results cannot be compared in detail. — “However, differences in the behavioral paradigms used in the present work (sensory discrimination tasks modeled after primate studies) and the paradigms used in the barrel system studies (based on active exploration and passive stimulation) preclude a more detailed comparison between these results.”
limitationThe attentional load of the task was modest, and a harder task might have reduced or reversed the suppression. — “The absence of response enhancement to the target stimulus is consistent with the modest attentional load in this task; had the attentional load been greater, the suppression might have been attenuated or even converted into enhancement (assuming that attentional mechanisms are distinct and additive).”
limitationThe synaptic and circuit mechanism of the suppression is not established. — “Our results represent a first step toward understanding the synaptic and circuit mechanisms by which this suppression occurs.”
limitationthe absence of target enhancement is attributed to a modest attentional load, so a harder task might have changed the result — “The absence of response enhancement to the target stimulus is consistent with the modest attentional load in this task; had the attentional load been greater, the suppression might have been attenuated or even converted into enhancement (assuming that attentional mechanisms are distinct and additive).”
limitationa minority of units, 8 of 32, showed the opposite effect — “Although most single units showed a suppression of evoked activity in the engaged condition, a minority showed an increase (8/32 units). This had the effect of concentrating the stimulus-evoked spikes into smaller population of neurons, each with a relatively higher firing rate.”
limitationsuppression interacts with repetition rate and disappears at the highest rates — “However, at all but the highest repetition rates, the responses in the engaged condition were relatively suppressed relative to the passive condition ( Fig. 2b ). Thus suppression interacts with repetition rate, but is not limited to the initial stimulus.”
limitationthe thalamocortical depression account is offered as an explanation that could work, not one that was tested here — “Synaptic depression of the thalamocortical inputs 15 , 35 could explain how the relative enhancement of the thalamic spontaneous rate in the engaged condition might lead to suppression of the cortical evoked response, without changing the cortical spontaneous activity.”
limitationthe two intermodal experiments used a harder task and longer training than the main experiment — “To increase the difficulty of the task and thereby increase the likelihood of detecting an effect of selective attention 21 , we used a tone discrimination task which was substantially more challenging (~3 weeks training, performance ~ 80%) than the spatial discrimination task (~1 week training, performance >95%) presented above.”

findingEngaging in an auditory task suppressed sound-evoked responses in auditory cortex, the opposite sign of the enhancement produced by selective attention. — “Surprisingly, we found that engagement suppressed responses, an effect opposite in sign to that elicited by selective attention.”
findingThe suppression of evoked responses occurred with no accompanying change in pre-stimulus spontaneous cortical activity. — “This suppression of evoked responses was not accompanied by any change in the spontaneous activity preceding stimulus presentation ( Fig. 1b–c,e–f,h–i ), in contrast to other studies reporting an increase in spontaneous activity with task engagement 19 .”
findingResponses to the task-relevant target sound were suppressed just as much as responses to the irrelevant non-target sound, so the effect is not selective withdrawal of attention from non-targets. — “Rather, the suppression of the responses to the target stimulus was indistinguishable ( Fig. 2d–e and Fig. S2c : Single unit responses to task relevant and task irrelevant stimuli are equally suppressed during the task) from that of the last non-target stimulus in the stimulus train (paired t-test, p>0.1).”
findingSound-evoked responses during auditory-attention blocks were not suppressed relative to olfactory-attention blocks, showing the suppression follows task engagement rather than the auditory modality of the task. — “There was no net suppression of sound-evoked responses elicited during the auditory block compared with those elicited during the olfactory block ( Fig. 3a–b ).”
findingThe amount of suppression was unrelated to a neuron's spatial selectivity, frequency selectivity, or the octave distance between its best frequency and the attended frequency. — “However, there was no correlation between the modulation index and spatial selectivity ( Fig. 2f and Fig. S2d : Single unit responses to task relevant and task irrelevant stimuli are equally suppressed during the task), frequency selectivity ( Fig. 3e ), or the distance in octaves between the best frequency and the attended frequency ( Fig. 3f and Fig. S3 : Measurement of cortical tuning curves).”
findingSuppression persisted in a head-fixed task in which the stimulus started randomly rather than being triggered by the animal, ruling out self-triggering as the cause. — “We therefore conclude that the suppression observed in the engaged condition does not depend on the animal triggering the stimulus.”
findingBecause a minority of neurons increased their firing while most were suppressed, the engaged-state representation was sparser, with kurtosis rising from 4.1 to 8.4. — “The kurtosis of the firing rate distribution in the engaged condition was greater than in the passive condition (kurtosis engaged = 8.4 ± 2.4 vs. kurtosis passive = 4.1 ± 0.7; p <0.05; see SM5: Kurtosis of firing rate distribution), indicating that the stimulus representation in the engaged condition was sparser.”
findingIn the auditory thalamus engagement left evoked responses unchanged but raised spontaneous firing rates, the opposite pattern to cortex. — “In the auditory thalamus, however, engagement enhanced spontaneous firing rates but did not affect evoked responses.”
findingThalamic evoked responses were statistically identical between engaged and passive conditions (p=0.80). — “In contrast to cortex, there was no difference (p=0.80) in the thalamic evoked response ( Fig. 6a ) between the engaged and passive conditions.”
findingSleep and ketamine anesthesia reduced spontaneous cortical activity relative to the awake passive state, giving those low-arousal states a neural signature distinct from passive wakefulness. — “Spontaneous and evoked cortical activity showed a characteristic neural signature under each condition ( Table T1 ): spontaneous cortical activity was reduced in the less aroused states (sleep and anesthetized) compared with the awake but passive condition.”
findingNeural activity did not differ between the passive blocks recorded before and after the task, excluding recording drift, satiety and reward expectation as explanations for the suppression. — “There was no change (p>0.2) in neural activity between the first and second passive block of trials (i.e. between the passive block before and after the engaged block), indicating that suppression was not due to nonstationarities in the recording, satiety, reward expectation, arousal, etc. (see SM4: Comparison of neural activity between first and second passive block).”
findingtask engagement suppressed evoked responses across single units, multiunit activity and LFP — “Suppression of the evoked response was observed for most of the responsive (SM2: Stimulus response analysis) single units ( Fig. 1e , p<0.01). Suppression was also observed for measures of neural population activity, including multiunit activity ( Fig. 1f , p<1e–16) and evoked LFP ( Fig. 1g , p<1e”
findingcortical spontaneous activity did not change, unlike other reports — “This suppression of evoked responses was not accompanied by any change in the spontaneous activity preceding stimulus presentation ( Fig. 1b–c,e–f,h–i ), in contrast to other studies reporting an increase in spontaneous activity with task engagement”
findingtask-relevant target and irrelevant non-target stimuli were suppressed equally — “However, the response to the target stimulus was not enhanced ( Fig. 2c ; see also Fig. S2a–b : Single unit responses to task relevant and task irrelevant stimuli are equally suppressed during the task). Rather, the suppression of the responses to the target stimulus was indistinguishable ( Fig. 2d–e and Fig. S2c : Single unit responses to task relevant and task irrelevant stimuli are equally suppressed during the task) from that of the last non-target stimulus in the stimulus train (paired t-test, p>0.1).”
findingthe representation became sparser, kurtosis 8.4 engaged against 4.1 passive — “The kurtosis of the firing rate distribution in the engaged condition was greater than in the passive condition (kurtosis engaged = 8.4 ± 2.4 vs. kurtosis passive = 4.1 ± 0.7; p <0.05; see SM5: Kurtosis of firing rate distribution), indicating that the stimulus representation in the engaged condition was sparser.”
findingno suppression of auditory responses in an auditory block relative to an olfactory block — “These results are inconsistent with the hypothesis that engaging in an auditory task per se suppresses responses in auditory cortex, and support the interpretation advanced above that suppression results from engaging in a task, regardless of sensory modality.”
findingsuppression appeared in both blocks relative to passive, so it does not depend on the task's modality — “As a further test, we compared responses in all three conditions (passive, engaged-auditory, engaged-olfactory) in a single animal performing another intermodal attention task (see Methods: Task 3). As expected, sound-evoked responses were suppressed in both the auditory and the olfactory blocks compared with the passive block, and responses during the auditory block were not suppressed relative to the responses during the olfactory block ( Fig. 3c–d ). Thus, engaging in an auditory task did not elicit a reduction in acoustically-evoked responses relative to engaging in an olfactory task, in marked contrast to the robust suppression of acoustically-evoked responses relative to the passive condition. We therefore conclude that the general suppression observed in the purely auditory task did not depend on the auditory nature of the task.”
findingsuppression was unrelated to spatial selectivity, frequency selectivity or distance from the attended frequency — “This raised the possibility that the suppression we observed in the engaged condition was due to attentional suppression in the majority of neurons which were stimulated suboptimally. To test this hypothesis, we recorded tuning curves and assessed the relationship between the receptive field and the modulation index of the evoked responses. We expected that if suppression arose from the presentation of suboptimal stimuli, then the modulation index should increase with stimulus selectivity or with the degree to which the best frequency matched the attended frequency. However, there was no correlation between the modulation index and spatial selectivity ( Fig. 2f and Fig. S2d : Single unit responses to task relevant and task irrelevant stimuli are equally suppressed during the task), frequency selectivity ( Fig. 3e ), or the distance in octaves between the best frequency and the attended frequency ( Fig. 3f and Fig. S3 : Measurement of cortical tuning curves). Interestingly, suppression was correlated with spontaneous activity, a property of the cells that does not depend on the stimulus used during the task (see Fig. S4 : Cortical units with high spontaneous firing rates show less suppression of the evoked response during the task). Thus in summary, task-engaged suppression cannot be readily predicted from receptive field properties.”
findingthe same suppression appeared when the animal did not trigger the stimulus, with a nearly identical modulation index — “The modulation index of the evoked responses for multiunit activity was −0.20 ± 0.03, which was very similar to the values obtained in the task in Fig. 1 , −0.19±0.02.”
findingsleep and anaesthesia have their own distinct signatures, with reduced spontaneous activity — “Spontaneous and evoked cortical activity showed a characteristic neural signature under each condition ( Table T1 ): spontaneous cortical activity was reduced in the less aroused states (sleep and anesthetized) compared with the awake but passive condition. The distinct neural signatures associated with sleep and anesthesia indicate that these states are different from the passive but wakeful condition.”
