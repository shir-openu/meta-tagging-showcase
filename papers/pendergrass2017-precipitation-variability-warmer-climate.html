Precipitation variabilityterm·dom also connects extreme wet and dry events, floods and droughts, which pose threats to the environment and society 3 . Magnitude of precipitation variabilityterm·dom change By how much do climate modelsterm·key predict precipitation variabilityterm·dom to change in response to changes in climate? We consider the magnitude of the change in precipitation variabilityterm·dom with warmingterm·dom in the context of two null hypotheses. The first hypothesis arises from a statistical argument for a positive-definite variable like precipitation. The simplest form of change for precipitation is to scale an initial timeseries by a constant, which leads to a change in standard deviationterm·mth equal to the change in its mean 11 . Our first hypothesis, then, is that the change in standard deviationterm·mth follows mean precipitationterm·dom. The predicted rate of increase of global-mean precipitation is energetically constrained to about 2% K −1 9 .

Instead of following the mean, extreme precipitationterm·dom increases much more with warmingterm·dom, at a rate similar to the increase in near-surface moistureterm·var 10 . The second hypothesis is that variability is also driven by the change in near-surface moistureterm·var and would be similar in magnitude to its change 15 , 32 . A simple model assuming that precipitation rate is proportional to near-surface moistureterm·var and vertical velocity, which can each be represented by an appropriate distribution 28 , predicts that if moistureterm·key were to increase with no corresponding change in circulationterm·key or relative humidity, the change in standard deviationterm·mth of precipitation would equal the rate of moistening, 6–7% K −1 (this is also noted by ref. 33 ).

For the change in precipitation variabilityterm·dom to follow the change in moistureterm·key, the circulationterm·key must not change, according to the model of ref. 28 . If precipitation rate is proportional to moistureterm·key and vertical velocity (when vertical velocity is upward), then a strengthening of the circulationterm·key (and thus the average magnitude of vertical velocity) would increase precipitation variabilityterm·dom, while a weakening of the circulationterm·key and decrease in magnitude of vertical velocity would decrease precipitation variabilityterm·dom. Climate modelsterm·key project a decrease in variability of the tropical circulationterm·key in response to warmingterm·dom 34 related to an increase in stability 35 – 37 , and so we should expect the influence of circulationterm·key to decrease precipitation variabilityterm·dom below the magnitude that would be predicted from the influence of moistureterm·key alone.

Because the magnitude of precipitation variabilityterm·dom increase is in most cases greater than the change in mean precipitationterm·dom, the first hypothesis can be rejected as an explanation. The second hypothesis, that precipitation variabilityterm·dom is driven by moistureterm·key, can provide a starting point to explain its changes. Because in most models and regions the change in variability is smaller than the change in moistureterm·key, the simple model discussed above implies that circulationterm·key must also weaken. The mean, variability, and extreme of precipitation each measure a different aspect of the distribution of precipitation, but they are not independent of one another. In order to reconcile the differing changes in variability with extreme precipitationterm·dom, there must also be a change in the shape of the distribution of circulationterm·key, because merely changing its strength does not break the symmetry between the changes in mean precipitationterm·dom, variability, and the magnitude of extreme precipitationterm·dom change. Reference 28 showed that an increase in the skewness of the vertical velocity distribution reconciles these differences.

The magnitude of precipitation variabilityterm·dom change varies remarkably little across this wide range of timescales, maintaining an increase between 4 and 5% K −1 in the CMIP5term multi-model global land mean, with a modest decrease in magnitude toward longer timescales. The global average (including ocean) has a similar pattern with a slightly smaller increase, 3–4% K −1 . The smaller increase over ocean might be related to the smaller warmingterm·dom there 40 , to soil-moisture feedbacks over land 41 , or to compensating regional changes over ocean.

Indeed, if we think of precipitation as a red-noise processterm·mth, the effects of moistureterm·key on the change in the characteristics of this process project onto all timescales. Discussion In summary, precipitation variabilityterm·dom simulated by climate modelsterm·key from daily and monthly to interannual and decadal timescales increases robustly over almost all land areas in response to anthropogenic warmingterm·dom in most models. For longer timescales, particularly interannual and longer, the forced increase may well be compensated for or amplified by natural variability (Fig. S5 ). We also show that observed daily precipitation variabilityterm·dom increased over the second half of the 20 th century. In most models, regions, and seasons, the change in precipitation variabilityterm·dom (measured by the mean standard deviationterm·mth) is higher in magnitude than the change in mean precipitationterm·dom and lower in magnitude than the change in moistureterm·key and extreme precipitationterm·dom (heaviest day of rainfall each year). In most regions and seasons, mean precipitationterm·dom increase sets a lower bound on the change in precipitation variabilityterm·dom; the increase in precipitation variabilityterm·dom is at least as large and in many cases greater than the increase in mean precipitationterm·dom. We attribute this to the role of increasing moistureterm·key counteracted by weakening circulationterm·key.

In our analysis, we quantify precipitation variabilityterm·dom by its standard deviationterm·mth, and we examine its change in a set of model simulationsterm·mth subject to just one forcing scenario. While the standard deviationterm·mth is a crude metric of precipitation variabilityterm·dom, especially on short timescales with many dry periods, precipitation variabilityterm·dom also increases when quantified by other metrics (Fig. S8 ). The standard deviationterm·mth has the advantage of being well-defined, with a straightforward behavior in response to simple scaling of the distribution. Our analysis focuses on model simulationsterm·mth driven by a large increase in greenhouse gas forcing along with changes in aerosol forcing. Changes in variability will differ with the type of forcing, e.g., in an extreme example of geoengineering by stratospheric sulfate aerosol injection, where global-mean temperature does not change but mean precipitationterm·dom decreases 44 . Finally, in our interpretation we have not considered mechanisms which might be important regionally, like soil moistureterm·key feedbacks.

Aggregated globally and over long timescales, the increased precipitation variabilityterm·dom from daily all the way to decadal timescales is a robustterm·mth emergent aspect of the water cycle that is changing as a result of anthropogenic warmingterm·dom, yet has received little attention. Both theoretical work and impact studies are required to complete and connect the understanding of changes in mean, variability, and extreme precipitationterm·dom, as well as the risks they pose to society.

Methods Model simulationsterm·mth Model simulationsterm·mth include the CMIP5term multi-model ensemble 45 (the r1i1p1 member only, 37 models with monthly data and 34 with daily data), CESM1 large ensemble 46 (40 members), and GFDL ESM2M large ensemble 47 (30 members). For all ensemble simulationsterm·mth, fully coupled simulationsterm·mth are forced by historical and RCP8.5 scenarios. We used output starting from 1951 because the GFDL large ensemble starts in 1950 and this allows for 1 year of spinup, and ending in 2100 when the GFDL and CESM1 simulationsterm·mth end.

Standard deviationterm·mth We quantify precipitation variabilityterm·dom by its standard deviationterm·mth. We remove the linear trend from each time series before calculating its standard deviationterm·mth. This largely removes the effect of transient warmingterm·dom at the end of the 21 st century in the RCP8.5 scenario.

Extreme precipitationterm·dom We define extreme precipitationterm·dom (shown in Figs 2 and 3 ) as the maximum daily precipitationterm·dom each year (rx1dayterm·dom 48 ), averaged over the same time periods as mean precipitationterm·dom and precipitation variabilityterm·dom.

Statistical significance Error bars or envelopes in Figs 1 and 4 show the 95% confidence interval of the multi-model mean calculated from a two-tailed Student’s t -test, using the standard deviationterm·mth among ensemble members where the degrees of freedom is the number of ensemble members.

Robustness across climate modelsterm·key A change is considered “robustterm·mth” when at least 67% of climate modelsterm·key agree on the sign of the change.

Spatial aggregationterm·mth Spatial aggregationterm·mth follows ref. 29 . We use each model’s own land mask and grid cell area fields to calculate the land fraction contributed by each grid cell. Where summer and winter seasons are identified, summer seasons are defined as June to August (JJA) north of the equator and December to February (DJF) south of it; vice versa for winter. Tropics and extratropics are defined as latitudes less and greater than 30°, respectively. Internal variabilityterm·key is quantified by comparing two members of the CESM1 ensemble drawn randomly without replacement during the base period.

Regridding For maps of the CMIP5term multi-model mean, calculations (mean, standard deviationterm·mth, or extreme precipitationterm·dom) are made on each model’s grid, data are regridded to a common 2.5 × 2.5° grid, the multi-model mean initial field is calculated, each model’s change is scaled by its change in global-mean surface air temperature, and then the multi-model mean absolute change is divided by the initial multi-model mean.

Spatial averaging For Figs 3 and 4 , the spatial-average change in each of mean precipitationterm·dom, standard deviationterm·mth of precipitation, or mean moistureterm·key is calculated by first calculating the variable at each grid cell. In the case of standard deviationterm·mth, it is squared. Then, the spatial average of the variable for the initial and final time periods is calculated, and in the case of standard deviationterm·mth, the square root is taken. Next, the difference of the spatial average between the time periods is calculated, followed by scaling by each model’s global-mean surface air temperature change over the appropriate period of time (in Fig. 4 ), and finally calculating the multi-model mean.

Timescale dependence For each timescale, the standard deviationterm·mth is calculated for an initial and final time period. Spatial averaging is carried out as described above. For daily through annual timescales, the initial period is 1976–2005 and the final period is 2071–2100. Daily data for 34 CMIP5term models are used. For the 1-month timescale, the annual cycle is removed before the standard deviationterm·mth is calculated. For the 3-month timescale, monthly data with annual cycle removed are averaged in 3-month seasons and the standard deviationterm·mth is calculated. For the 1-year timescale, monthly data is averaged over years. For the 3-year timescale, monthly data are averaged in 3-year periods over the 51-year periods 1955–2005 and 2050–2100 to achieve sufficient sampling.

Observational analysis Daily precipitation station data come from the Global Historical Climatology Network Daily 49 dataset, version 3.22 50 and use the subset of stations in the Global Climate Observing System (GCOS) Surface Network (GSN). This subset of stations has better spatial sampling than the total network. Only stations with data for at least 50% of each period are used. Global-mean surface temperature observations are calculated from NOAA GlobalTemp 51 , 52 . Change in the standard deviationterm·mth of daily precipitation is calculated from 1960–1969 to 1990–1999. The time periods are chosen to maximize the number of stations; we repeated the analysis for start dates in the 1950s and 1970s and end dates from the 1990s through 2016, with similar results.
