Abstract This study provides a proof‐of‐concept for incorporating evolutionary theoryterm·key into forensic anthropologyterm·key practice. Specifically, we test whether innominateterm·key measurements used in the DSP 2term·key sex‐estimation method reflect known patterns of morphological integrationterm·dom and whether variable redundancyterm·key can be reduced without compromising classification accuracy.

Innominateterm·key measurements were obtained from published datasets totaling 3045 individuals. Principal component analysisterm·key (PCAterm) was used to identify clusters of measurements. Relative standard deviation of eigenvaluesterm was used to assess the degree of morphological integrationterm·dom. Posterior probabilitiesterm of sex classification were computed using one variable per cluster (cluster‐based approachterm) and compared to a randomly selected four‐variable approach, consistent with the minimum recommended by the original study. Simulations were used to generate posterior distributions of accuracy and the percentage of samples reaching a decision threshold.

Introduction Skeletal sex estimationterm·dom is typically the first step in constructing a biological profile from skeletal remains. While most practitioners employ a variety of nonmetric and metric methods involving different skeletal regions (Klales 2020 ; Klales and Lesciotto 2025 ), the innominatesterm·key remain the most reliable indicator of sex, followed by postcranial metrics and the skull (Klales et al. 2012 ; Spradley and Jantz 2011 ). Currently, nonmetric methods using innominateterm·key traits (i.e., Klales 2018 ; Klales et al. 2012 ) and metric methods incorporating multiple skeletal regions (i.e., Jantz and Ousley 2005 ) are the most preferred and commonly used in casework (Klales 2020 ; Klales and Lesciotto 2025 ).

Diagnose sexuelle probabiliste (DSPterm), a metric method introduced in 2005, estimates skeletal sex using 10 measurements of the innominateterm·key and a linear discriminant function based on a reference sample of more than 2000 innominatesterm·key (Murail et al. 2005 ). The method was later developed into a freely available software package, DSP 2term·key, and validated using a sample of ~600 innominatesterm·key (Brůžek et al. 2017 ).

Most metric approaches in forensic anthropologyterm·key treat each measurement as an independent unit. However, many innominateterm·key measurements are not independent due to shared developmental processes and genetic architecture. Traits tend to covary as the result of morphological integrationterm·dom (the tendency for morphological complexes to evolve as a cohesive unit), with subsets of traits that show more correlation with one another ( modularityterm·dom ) (Hallgrímsson et al. 2009 ). Previous evolutionary and morphometric studies have shown that, while the human hip exhibited less integration and developmental constraint compared to other Great Apes, certain innominateterm·key measurements, such as those associated with the dimensions of the ischium, remain highly integrated (i.e., they are more correlated with one another than with other traits) (Grabowski et al. 2011 ). Traits such as innominateterm·key height, iliac breadth, and pubic length have also been shown to covary in response to climatic selection (Betti et al. 2014 ). Mallard et al. ( 2017 ) further demonstrated that the innominateterm·key comprises multiple modules, with more trait integration (i.e., a higher degree of correlation) within each module.

In justifying the selection of the 10 innominateterm·key measurements, the developers of DSPterm and DSP 2term·key acknowledged the non‐independence of skeletal traits, grouping these variables into two broader functional units: the sacroiliac region and ischiopubic region (Santos et al. 2020 ). However, this model is likely an oversimplification of the innominateterm·key morphological complex and does not take into account integration within and across these regions.

The validity of DSP 2term·key has been supported in a contemporary US sample, and its use for forensic casework has been recommended (Lesciotto and Klales 2025 ). Still, an assessment of correlations among these measurements used in DSP 2term·key, especially those likely arising from shared developmental and evolutionary constraints, may help identify redundancyterm·key. Testing the accuracy of sex estimationterm·key when correlated (redundant) variables are removed from the estimation model could improve efficiency and inform future model design.

Previously, the authors of the current study called for the incorporation of evolutionary theoryterm·key into our methods and practice (WinburnpersName et al. 2022 ). This study responds to this call by offering a proof‐of‐concept framework that integrates evolutionary theoryterm·key into forensic anthropologyterm·key. Specifically, this study aims to test whether the patterns of correlation among innominateterm·key measurements used in the DSP 2term·key sex estimationterm·key model reflect known patterns of morphological integrationterm·dom in humans. We then assess the redundancyterm·key of these measurements, exploring whether the inclusion of highly correlated traits contributes to duplicated information in the context of sex estimationterm·key.

Materials and Methods The measurements used in DSP 2term·key include: PUM (acetabulo‐symphyseal pubic length), SPU (cotylo‐pubic width), DCOX (innominateterm·key height), IIMT (greater sciatic notch height), ISMM (post‐acetabular ischium length), SCOX (iliac breadth), SS (spino‐sciatic length), SA (spino‐auricular length), SIS (cotylo‐sciatic breadth), and VEAC (vertical acetabular diameter). Their definitions were described in detail elsewhere (Brůžek et al. 2017 ; Lesciotto and Klales 2025 ; Murail et al. 2005 ; Santos et al. 2020 ). SIS and VEAC were considered “rescue” measurements recommended only for incomplete innominatesterm·key (Brůžek et al. 2017 ). Therefore, these two variables were excluded from our analysis. The approximate locations of the eight measurements are depicted in Figure 1 ; however, it should be noted that these are author‐drawn guides and not intended to represent the precise anatomical landmarks used in data collection (see Brůžek et al. 2017 and Lesciotto and Klales 2025 for detailed definitions). According to Santos et al. ( 2020 ), DCOX and SCOX represent overall innominateterm·key dimensions, while the remaining six measurements represent two functional modules: PUM, SPU, and ISMM are grouped together as the ischio‐pubic module, and IIMT, SS, and SA comprise the sacro‐iliac module.

Osteometric data were extracted from published studies (Brůžek et al. 2017 ; Kuchař et al. 2024 ; Machado et al. 2018 ), resulting in a total of 3045 sets of innominateterm·key measurements. For analyses requiring complete data (principal component analysisterm·key (PCAterm) and morphological integrationterm·dom analysis), we used complete observations only. A total of 1855 sets (941 males and 944 females) from Brůžek et al. ( 2017 ) were used as the reference/training dataset. The dataset from Kuchař et al. ( 2024 ) included 272 sets of complete innominateterm·key measurements but lacked associated demographic information. Therefore, this dataset was used as the validation set for morphological integrationterm·dom analysis. For sex classification validation, we used a combined 727 sets of innominateterm·key measurements (367 males and 360 females) from Brůžek et al. ( 2017 ) and Machado et al. ( 2018 ) with varying patterns of missingness: PUM (313 missing), SPU (200), DCOX (149), IIMT (15), ISMM (128), SCOX (220), SS (12), and SA (12). No data imputation was performed.

In analyzing integration as a result of genetic and developmental underpinnings, the use of the genetic variance–covariance matrix ( G ) is required. However, estimating G can be challenging as it requires phenotyping and genotyping a large number of individuals or access to known pedigree information. In most cases, a common and practical assumption is that the phenotypic variance–covariance matrix ( P ) provides a good proxy for G , particularly for morphological traits that tend to exhibit moderate‐to‐high heritabilities and are often influenced by pleiotropic effects (Cheverud 1988 ; Rolian 2014 ). This assumption is appropriate for innominateterm·key metric data, as the average heritability across all innominateterm·key measurements was estimated to be ~0.4 (Xu et al. 2025 ). The only previously published study on genetic correlations among human innominateterm·key traits (Sharma 2002 ) also reported high levels of heritability. However, the study design (i.e., twin study) was demonstrated to have trouble separating common environmental components of phenotypic variance from genetic components (Kruuk 2004 ; Kruuk and Hadfield 2007 ) and should be interpreted with caution. Nonetheless, in the present context, P could be considered a reasonable substitute for G .

PCAterm was used to identify clusters of traits, and the relative standard deviation of eigenvaluesterm (Pavlicev et al. 2009 ) was used to quantify the degree of morphological integrationterm·dom within and between these clusters of traits. Rather than using the DSP 2term·key software, we implemented the DSP 2term·key classification scheme using custom code in the R Programming Language (R Core Team 2025 ) following the methods outlined in Brůžek et al. ( 2017 ). For each sample of the validation datasets, one measurement was randomly selected from each identified cluster, and posterior probabilitiesterm of sex classification were calculated. The cut‐off for assigning biological sex was set at 0.85, in line with more recent studies (Avent et al. 2022 ; Jerković et al. 2020 ), instead of the 0.95 threshold originally recommended by Brůžek et al. ( 2017 ). This process was repeated 10,000 times to provide a posterior distribution of accuracy rates for the cluster‐based approachterm. To compare the performance of the cluster‐based approachterm, we simulated the random drawing of four variables (without regard to cluster structure) for each sample in the validation dataset. Posterior probabilitiesterm for sex classification were then calculated using the same threshold. This process was repeated 10,000 times to provide a null distribution of accuracy rates. For each simulation iteration (for both cluster‐based and randomized approachesterm), individuals were only included if they had complete values for the selected set of variables. Individuals missing one or more of the selected variables were excluded for that iteration, but could be included in subsequent iterations if the required variables were present. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.3 (R Core Team 2025 ).

Discussion This study served as a proof‐of‐concept for integrating evolutionary theoryterm·key into forensic anthropologyterm·key by evaluating whether a morphological integrationterm·dom‐informed approach can improve the efficiency of metric sex estimationterm·key and inform future model design. Specifically, we examined the patterns of phenotypic correlation among innominateterm·key measurements used in the DSP 2term·key sex estimationterm·key model, assessing their consistency with known patterns of morphological integrationterm·dom in humans, and assessed the accuracy of sex estimationterm·key when correlated (redundant) variables were removed. Echoing the results of previous studies (Brůžek et al. 2017 ; Santos et al. 2020 ), we found distinctive clusters among the DSP 2term·key innominateterm·key measurements and replicated these results using a validation sample. We also found greater degrees of morphological integrationterm·dom within clusters compared to the full set of DSP 2term·key measurements or a random correlation matrix of nonintegrated traits, supporting their interpretation as cohesive morphological units. Finally, the classification accuracy of the cluster‐based approachterm, randomly selecting one measurement from each identified cluster, was shown to achieve higher accuracy than that of using four randomly selected measurements, though with lower assignment rates. The results support the hypothesis that selecting one representative measurement per morphologically integrated cluster maintains classification performance while reducing redundancyterm·key in input variables.

The clusters identified here broadly align with previous findings (Betti et al. 2014 ; Mallard et al. 2017 ), with measurements grouped into modules related to the overall pelvic dimensions (PUM, SA, and SCOX, pelvic outlet) and internal pelvic dimensions (DCOX, SS, ISMM, SPU, pelvic inlet). Greater sciatic notch height (IIMT) formed an isolated grouping, likely because variation in this measurement affects the overall orientation and shape of the ilium and thus does not cluster with other modules. The degree of morphological integrationterm·dom was greater within these clusters than across the full set of DSP 2term·key measurements or within a random correlation matrix of nonintegrated traits. While the calculation of relative standard deviation of eigenvaluesterm can be inflated by a reduced number of traits (Mallard et al. 2017 ; Pavlicev et al. 2009 ), this potential bias was likely offset by the large sample size used in this study (Haber 2011 ). Notably, the “best four” variables identified by Brůžek et al. ( 2017 ) in the original DSP 2term·key study also map closely onto the modules reported here: PUM within the pelvic outlet module, DCOX and SPU within the pelvic inlet module, and IIMT in the isolated ilium module. This consistency suggests that DSP2's most informative variables inherently reflect patterns of morphological integrationterm·dom in the innominateterm·key, providing an evolutionary explanation for their utility and offering a framework for evaluating the performance of reduced‐variable sex‐estimation approaches.

The classification accuracy using the cluster‐based approachterm was comparable to that of using four randomly selected variables without regard to morphological modules. This is broadly consistent with the accuracy rates (at least 96%) reported by the original publication (Brůžek et al. 2017 ), previous findings using different combinations of variables (a combined 95% accuracy using all 10 measurements, Quatrehomme et al. 2017 ), and aligns with the most recent validation study using a contemporary US sample (at least 95% accuracy using 10 measurements, Lesciotto and Klales 2025 ). Compared with a previous study of metric sex estimationterm·key accuracy using casework data (Thomas et al. 2016 ), the performance of DSP 2term·key is notably improved, even with just three measurements. While the percentage of individuals assigned a sex using the cluster‐based approachterm was lower (average in the low 30%) compared to the randomized four‐variable approach (mid‐20% to mid‐40%), this is likely due to the use of fewer input variables. The low classification rates even with lower posterior probability thresholds, compared to the original study's findings and the findings of Quatrehomme et al. ( 2017 ) (both ~94% classification rates) using the full set of DSP 2term·key measurements, represent the limitation of applying the cluster‐based approachterm to casework without further model or method improvements. These findings reported in this study, however, suggest that selecting traits from distinct morphological modules can enhance classification efficiency without sacrificing accuracy, and that adding one or more representative traits from different modules could further improve model performance. It is also important to note that DSP2 is not population specific. This highlights the broad relevance of our findings, as patterns of morphological integrationterm·dom in the innominateterm·key are expected to remain consistent across human populations.

While the DSP 2term·key innominateterm·key measurements have generally acceptable intra‐ and inter‐observer errors (Lesciotto and Klales 2025 ; Machado et al. 2018 ; Quatrehomme et al. 2017 ), most are not part of the standard measurements defined in the Standards for Data Collection from Human Skeletal Remains (Buikstra and Ubelaker 1994 ) or Data Collection Procedures for Forensic Skeletal Material 2.0 (Langley et al. 2016 ), and only two of the measurements are used by the program Fordisc (Jantz and Ousley 2005 ). In their evaluation, Lesciotto and Klales ( 2025 ) reported that these measurements are “relatively easy to take” with minimal technical requirements and recommended their use in casework, especially in situations where alternative methods yield ambiguous results, since the classification algorithm of DSP 2term·key is robust and its accuracy rate is high. We recommend that future work aimed at improving the DSP 2term·key model focus on identifying innominateterm·key measurements that both reflect morphological modules that can be taken with ease and with consistent intra‐ and inter‐observer reliability.

The results of the cluster‐based approachterm presented in this study could be further improved by adjusting the posterior probability threshold, as suggested by Jerković et al. ( 2020 ). For example, when a threshold of 0.75 was used, ~60% of the validation samples were assigned a sex while the classification accuracy remained the same. However, setting a strict thresholdterm implicitly treats sex as a fixed binary category. In contrast, the use of posterior probabilitiesterm directly reframes sex as a probabilistic trait derived from the morphometric space of multiple innominateterm·key measurements. When used in this regard, sex‐estimation methods can more effectively acknowledge the biological complexity and continuous variation in human skeletal morphology (Meloro et al. 2025 ). Given that practitioners routinely use multiple methods in casework (Klales 2020 ; Klales and Lesciotto 2025 ), a more flexible approach may be to simply report the posterior probabilitiesterm from different methods directly, rather than using a strict thresholdterm.
