Abstract concepts↗Economic experiments offer a controlled setting for researchers to observe human decision-making and test diverse theories and hypotheses; however, substantial costs and efforts are incurred to gather many individuals as experimental participants. To address this issue, with the development of large language models (LLMs), researchers have recently attempted to develop simulated economic experiments using LLMs-driven agents, called generative agents. If generative agents can replicate human-like decision making in economic experiments, the cost problem of economic experiments can be alleviated. However, despite growing attention, a structured methodology for reliably simulating human behavior using generative agents has not yet emerged. Considering previous research and the current evolutionary stage of LLMs, this study focuses on the reasoning ability of generative agents as a key factor in establishing a framework for this new methodology. phenomenonA multi-agent simulation, designed to improve the reasoning ability of generative agents through prompting methods, was developed to reproduce the result of an actual economic experiment on the ultimatum game. concepts↗concepts↗concept_edgesconcept_edgesconcept_edgesThe results demonstrated that the higher the reasoning ability of the agents, the closer the results were to the theoretical solution, rather than to the real experimental result. The results also suggest that setting the personas of the generative agents may be important for reproducing the results of real economic experiments. These findings provide a foundation for further studies aiming to develop scalable, interpretable, and context-aware LLM-based simulations for experimental economics.

1 Introduction

Economic experiments offer a powerful framework for understanding human behavior under controlled economic conditions (Smith 1976, 1982). By systematically manipulating incentives and institutional settings, researchers can test economic theories, evaluate policy interventions, and gain insights into decision-making processes, which remains a widely used and foundational methodology in economics today (Andrighetto et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2024). However, these experiments often face practical challenges: ensuring statistically significant results requires a large number of participants, which entails considerable monetary compensation and logistical effort (Long and Lang 1992; Jobjörnsson et al. 2023).

To address these limitations, researchers have begun exploring the use of large language models (LLMs) in multi-agent simulations (MAS). content_tags↗In particular, LLM-driven MAS, concepts↗where each agent is powered by an LLM and called a generative agent, has emerged as a novel and potentially transformative approach for simulating humanlike behavior at scale (Park et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2024; Oliński et al. 2024). These generative agents exhibit several notable capabilities (Gao et al. 2024):

1. Adaptive responses to dynamic environments without predefined instructions (Chen and Ding 2023; Franceschelli and Musolesi 2024),

2. Multimodal information processing and autonomous generation of human-level

outputs (Xi et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2024; Zhu et al. 2024),

3. Rich interaction abilities with other agents and humans (Park et al. 2022, 2023), 4. Diversity across agents through differentiated roles and behaviors (Caron and

Srivastava 2023; Qian et al. 2024; Jiang et al. 2024).

While MAS has long been discussed in the social sciences (Axelrod 1997; Epstein 1999; Gilbert and Terna 2000), lacking the rationale on modelling agent behavior previously hindered widespread adoption in experimental economics. The emergence of LLM agents creates new opportunities to augment, rather than replace, traditional economic experiments, potentially offering an additional methodological tool alongside traditional human-subject experiments.

Nevertheless, integrating LLM agents into economic simulations introduces critical challenges. A particularly pressing concern is the presence of diverse and often opaque biases rooted in the training data and development processes of machine learning models (Blodgett et al. 2020; Barocas et al. 2023). These biases can perpetuate or even amplify harmful stereotypes and structural inequities (Bolukbasi et al. 2016; Caliskan et al. 2017; Zhao et al. 2017). Studies have classified these biases into at least three broad categories: those related to race and religion, those related to gender and status, and those linked to politics and culture (Lin et al. 2024). The growing ubiquity of LLMs in research and application thus demands urgent and systematic examination of their behavioral outputs.

The concern extends beyond sociocultural biases to include psychological and cognitive dimensions. For instance, behavioral tendencies measured through economic experiments, such as inequality aversion, risk and loss aversion, and time discounting, have shown divergent patterns in LLMs compared to human partici-

pants (Goli and Singh 2024; Ross et al. 2024). Similarly, when subjected to cognitive and psychological assessments, LLMs display response patterns that differ from those of actual humans (Binz and Schulz 2023; Hagendorff 2024; Hagendorff et al. 2023; Strachan et al. 2024; Opedal et al. 2024; Nguyen 2024; Koo et al. 2024), prompting efforts to identify and mitigate cognitive biases in these models (Echterhoff et al. 2024; Sumita et al. 2025).

This issue intersects with a longstanding insight from the experimental economics literature: human behavior is shaped by heterogeneous psychological, cultural, and demographic factors. The seminal critique of narrow subject pools (e.g., university students from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic societies— “WEIRD” subjects) highlights how experimental findings may fail to generalize across more diverse populations (Henrich et al. 2005, 2010; Oosterbeek et al. 2004; Burks et al. 2009; Brown and Robinson 2006; Bohlmann et al. 2010; Medin et al. 2017). It follows that careful evaluation of how LLM agents replicate not only average behavior but also the variability and biases embedded in human populations is essential for assessing their validity as experimental tools. Hence, real human-subject data remain indispensable as ground-truth benchmarks for calibrating LLM-based simulations and identifying the boundary conditions under which generative agents faithfully approximate, or systematically deviate from, human behavior.

Recent studies have begun to test this potential. Some report that LLM-driven MAS can replicate well-known findings from behavioral experiments (Aher et al. 2023; Horton 2023), while others observe inconsistencies between LLM-based and human-generated data (Brookins and DeBacker 2023; Tsuchihashi 2023; Guo 2023; Phelps and Russell 2024). limitationsconcepts↗These mixed results suggest that the outputs of generative agents are highly sensitive to simulation design choices such as agent persona, prompt formulation, and role instructions (Horton 2023; Phelps and Russell 2024; Han et al. 2024).

A deeper theoretical debate underpins the difficulty in achieving consistent humanlike behavior across simulations. One perspective argues that LLMs fundamentally lack the capacity to fully replicate human behavior due to the structure and scope of their training data (Wang et al. 2025a). According to this view, correcting behavioral discrepancies would require rethinking LLM development from the ground up. In contrast, a second viewpoint, which this study adopts, posits that LLMs may already contain latent structures capable of supporting human-like responses, provided the right methods are used to extract and interpret them.

content_tags↗Recent work by Huh et al. (2024) proposes the “Platonic Representation Hypothesis,” which suggests that as large-scale models increase in generality and capacity, they converge toward a shared latent space that reflects abstract statistical regularities of the real world. This implies that LLMs may already encode latent structures aligned with human cognition and behavior, regardless of their explicit training objectives. In addition, Gekhman et al. (2025) find that LLMs may possess “hidden factual knowledge”: even when surface-level answers are incorrect, internal representations may still privilege correct information. These findings highlight that, although LLM outputs diverge from actual human responses, their outputs can, under carefully controlled prompting conditions, be made to approximate empirical patterns in decision-making tasks.

Recent philosophical work further complicates the question of whether LLMs can truly be said to “know” something. Fierro et al. (2024) survey five major epistemological definitions of knowledge and examine how, or whether, these can be meaningfully applied to LLMs. They suggest that many current LLM evaluation practices may not align with these definitions, particularly with regard to justification and belief consistency.

However, our study is not grounded in any metaphysical claims about what LLMs “know.” Instead, we take a pragmatic stance: if LLM agents can reliably produce outputs that align with human data in economic experiments across both average and biased tendencies, they may serve as a valuable methodological complement. This approach enables researchers to leverage advantages such as scalability, ease of replication, and precise control over experimental conditions—benefits that are often difficult to achieve with human participants. Moreover, LLM-driven MAS allows the exploration of ethically sensitive or logistically challenging scenarios, such as those involving high-risk decision-making or manipulations difficult to pass institutional review.

An illustrative example is the study by Kitadai et al. (2023), which used LLMdriven MAS to simulate behavior in a one-shot ultimatum game (Güth et al. 1982), focusing on whether human-like responses could be elicited from LLM agents by adjusting prompts related to monetary incentives. While the model reproduced the proposer-side behavior observed in human data, it failed to align the responder-side behavior. As discussed further in Sect. 2.3, this shortcoming likely reflects the insufficient reasoning capabilities of the generative agents.

Building on this foundation, the present study investigates whether enhancing the reasoning abilities of LLM-based agents can lead to the emergence of latent humanlike behavioral patterns, including fairness in bargaining and sensitivity to reciprocity. In this work, we concentrate on decision-making and negotiation contexts, where human-like behavioral regularities are empirically well-documented and experimentally tractable. limitationsBroader applications to market-level modeling or institutional design remain important directions for future work. Given the caution raised by Heersmink et al. (2024) regarding the opaque nature of LLMs’ data and processes, it is crucial to proceed with both ambition and prudence. By clarifying the relationship between agent reasoning and behavioral alignment, we aim to provide methodological guidance for integrating LLM-driven MAS into experimental economics. As LLMs with increasingly advanced reasoning capabilities continue to emerge, establishing a principled framework for their experimental use becomes ever more essential. Our contribution thus offers timely methodological insights that can guide future research at the intersection of AI and experimental economics.

The remainder of this paper comprises the theoretical background presented in Sect. 2, the research methodology described in Sect. 3, the results shown in Sect. 4, and the discussion and conclusions presented in Sect. 5.

2 Theoretical Background

2.1 Ultimatum Game

We focus on the one-shot ultimatum game for three main reasons. First, it eliminates history-dependent learning and reputation effects, leaving a unique subgame-perfect equilibrium that provides a clean theoretical benchmark. Second, recent cross-task evaluations by Mei et al. (2024), who compared large-language-model agents with human subjects on several classic economic games, including the ultimatum game, show that the one-shot ultimatum game exhibits the largest human-AI performance gap. This makes the one-shot ultimatum game a stringent test bed: if generative agents can narrow that gap here, the implication for other interaction settings is even stronger. Third, a single-round design keeps prompts concise and prevents chain-ofthought content from leaking across rounds, which could otherwise induce artificial correlations in multi-round simulations. Taken together, these considerations make the one-shot ultimatum game the most interpretable and demanding environment for assessing whether enhanced reasoning enables LLM agents to approximate human bargaining behavior.

The ultimatum game involves a sequential decision-making process in which two players determine the distribution of a divisible good. Although there are both oneshot and repeated variations of this game, our investigation specifically concentrates on the one-shot ultimatum game. It comprises the following two steps (Kitadai et al. 2023).

1. The player making the offer suggests partitioning the divisible good. For example, with 100 coins in play, the offering player may propose taking 70 coins for themselves, leaving the responding player with 30 coins.

2. The player responding has the choice to either accept or reject the proposed division. Upon acceptance, the suggested distribution is implemented; however, in the case of rejection, neither player receives anything.

2.2 Foundations of Using LLMs in Economic Experiments

To clarify the methodological framework of this study, we offer a unified and expanded perspective on LLMs and generative artificial intelligence (generative AI), particularly focusing on the Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT) architecture. Our goal is to situate the use of gpt-4 in a broader conceptual and empirical context that justifies its application in simulating human-like behavior in economic experiments. Generative AI refers to a class of models that autonomously produce new, plausible data samples across various modalities—from text and images to audio and beyond. Unlike discriminative models that classify or predict within existing data boundaries, generative models learn the underlying data distribution itself, enabling them to synthesize entirely new outputs (Goodfellow et al. 2020; Ramesh et al. 2022). This paradigm shift—from recognition to generation—has created models that are not just analytical but creative, capable of mimicking the structure of real-world behavior and expression. In this sense, generative AI offers more than technological novelty; it marks a methodological transformation in how we model social phenomena.

LLMs, including the GPT series developed by OpenAI, represent a key subclass of generative AI. Built upon the Transformer architecture (Vaswani et al. 2017), these

models are trained on Internet-scale corpora and learn to predict the next word in a sequence—a deceptively simple objective that gives rise to complex capabilities when scaled sufficiently. Models such as gpt-4, which incorporate hundreds of billions of parameters and are trained on vast and diverse datasets, exhibit emergent output patterns that resemble analogical reasoning, few-shot adaptation, and contextsensitive dialogue generation (Brown et al. 2020; Bommasani et al. 2021). In particular, gpt-4 demonstrates human-level performance on professional and academic benchmarks, making it a promising tool for generating responses that resemble complex decision-making patterns observed in humans (Nie et al. 2023; Kiciman et al. 2023; Kazemi et al. 2025).

From a behavioral modeling standpoint, gpt-4 can be interpreted as a statistical knowledge base constructed from human language and interaction patterns. Its training corpus contains linguistic patterns that reflect social norms, strategic heuristics, and communicative conventions across cultural contexts (Lorè and Heydari 2024). This enables the model to produce responses that are not only linguistically fluent but also behaviorally plausible, a property that has led to its adoption in simulating agents for bargaining, negotiation, and multi-agent coordination (Dell’Acqua et al. 2023; Phelps and Russell 2024; Park et al. 2023). Crucially, this generative capacity enables LLMs to function as foundation models—general-purpose substrates that can be adapted to various downstream tasks without the need for retraining (Bommasani et al. 2021).

Nevertheless, several limitations must be addressed when deploying LLMs as behavioral agents. First, hallucinations, plausible but incorrect outputs, can emerge, especially under poorly constrained prompts (Bubeck et al. 2023). These are a byproduct of the model’s reliance on learned statistical associations rather than grounded world models. Second, LLMs inherit biases from their training data, which can affect their decision-making in subtle but impactful ways (e.g., favoring certain persuasion styles, exhibiting implicit demographic stereotypes) (Blodgett et al. 2020; Caliskan et al. 2017). Third, these models remain black boxes in terms of interpretability: while their outputs are analyzable, their internal reasoning processes are not directly accessible (Wei et al. 2022; Shumailov et al. 2024).

While these limitations remain open challenges, recent work has shown that prompt design can shape the inferential structure of LLM outputs. We therefore adopt two prompting strategies—(i) few-shot prompting, where the model is shown a small number of annotated examples to guide output style and logic (Brown et al. 2020), and (ii) chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting, which explicitly instructs the model to articulate intermediate reasoning steps (Wei et al. 2022)—not as general mitigation techniques, but as scaffolding methods to influence the reasoning quality of agent decisions in structured tasks. These strategies have been shown to enhance coherence, inference depth, and contextual relevance in complex queries (Wei et al. 2022; Kojima et al. 2022).

We conceptualize LLMs not merely as a linguistic engine, but as a computational agent that can approximate patterns consistent with bounded rationality, social norms, and strategic interaction when prompted appropriately. Some recent studies have suggested that LLM-based agents may exhibit behavior resembling early forms of Theory-of-Mind, such as anticipating others’ responses or responding to false-belief scenarios in controlled tasks (Fair et al. 2022; Phelps and Russell 2024; Strachan et al. 2024). While such behaviors do not imply genuine mental-state attribution, they support the view that LLMs can approximate certain patterns of social reasoning in language-based tasks, making them potentially useful as behavioral proxies in structured economic simulations.

In summary, this study proceeds from a pragmatist perspective: we do not claim that gpt-4 replicates human cognition per se, but we argue that LLMs can approximate certain behavioral patterns observed in economic experiments with a level of fidelity that may justify their use as methodological tools in specific, well-defined simulation contexts. This positioning is consistent with recent proposals in the social sciences and cognitive modeling communities that advocate using LLMs as empirical tools—entities whose outputs can be analyzed, compared, and validated just like those of human participants (Wang et al. 2025b; Xie et al. 2024). With careful prompt design, evaluation protocols, and methodological safeguards, LLMs may be able to offer a promising route to scalable, replicable, and interpretable simulations of human-like behavior.

2.3 Related Studies

Recent attempts to simulate human behavior in economic games using LLM-driven MAS have yielded mixed outcomes. A useful lens for organizing this literature is to contrast studies reporting success in behavioral replication with those that highlight systematic failures.

Notable examples of the former are the following two studies. Horton (2023) used GPT-based agents to replicate outcomes in canonical economic scenarios, including minimum wage policy, and concluded that agent behavior aligned with aggregate human results. Aher et al. (2023) introduced “Turing Experiments,” in which agents played games like the ultimatum game and generated distributions of choices statistically similar to human players under certain conditions. limitationslimitationssensesclaim_relationsHowever, these successes may stem from coarse-grained evaluation criteria: sensesboth studies assessed behavior at the level of aggregate distributions or binary alignment with typical outcomes, senseswithout deeper analysis of underlying reasoning processes or individual-level deviations. Subsequent studies applying finer-grained behavioral criteria and more complex game-theoretic environments have largely highlighted failures of alignment. For example, Brookins and DeBacker (2023) and Phelps and Russell (2024) found that LLM agents tended to favor fairness or failed to adapt dynamically to opponents in

A. Kitadai et al.1 3 1311

bargaining games. Tsuchihashi (2023) and Park et al. (2023) similarly noted that LLM agents did not exhibit human-like bidding or defection patterns in auctions and social dilemmas, respectively.

One recurring pattern is that agents often exhibit prosocial or cooperative tendencies even in settings that reward strategic exploitation. Mei et al. (2024) formalized this observation through behavioral Turing tests and proposed that LLMs encode a latent utility function that LLMs may be implicitly optimizing for perceived social harmony rather than material self-interest. This introduces a bias that systematically deviates from both normative game-theoretic predictions and descriptive human behavior.

In trying to explain these deviations, multiple methodological factors have been explored. For instance, Fan et al. (2024) showed that LLMs perform adequately when preferences are aligned among agents, but their behavior breaks down under conflicting incentives, especially when belief formation is required. Akata et al. (2025) emphasized that prompting strategies such as “social chain-of-thought” can improve agent coordination in repeated games, while Guo (2023) demonstrated that subtle variations in prompt framing significantly shift agent behavior. Other studies point to model-specific traits: Fontana et al. (2024) and Lorè and Heydari (2024) compared different LLMs and found considerable variability in cooperation rates, strategic abstraction, and contextual sensitivity.

Persona design has also been identified as a key factor. Han et al. (2024), Phelps and Russell (2024), and Horton (2023) noted that specifying traits such as age, gender, or personality type can nudge agents toward particular behavioral tendencies. However, the simultaneous variation of multiple design parameters in many studies has made it difficult to isolate causal effects.

Taken together, these findings reveal a fragmented and sometimes contradictory landscape, where various agent design parameters interact in complex ways, highlighting the need for more controlled experiments focusing on individual determinants of behavior. Among these, reasoning ability, the capacity of agents to interpret game structures, anticipate responses, and derive optimal strategies, emerges as a foundational component. Yet few studies have systematically isolated reasoning from confounding variables such as persona, payoff framing, or temperature settings.

The structure of our prompts aligns with recent practices in simulationbased cognitive modeling, where prompt engineering serves as an analog to experimental instructions given to human participants, thereby maintaining methodological consistency with human-subject experiments.

method↗content_tagsThe simulation strategy involved generating 1000 independent decisions for each unique combination of role (proposer/responder), prompt type, and temperature. limitationsWhile this number is not derived from a formal power analysis, it was selected based on two main considerations. First, prior work Kitadai et al. (2023) employed the same sample size and demonstrated that it was sufficient to reveal stable and interpretable distributions of agent behavior. Second, to assess the robustness of our findings, we also tested larger sets of examples (15 and 20 samples) to explore the presence or absence of significant differences in the simulation outcomes. Additionally, we investigate the stability of our results by repeating the selection of 10 examples with different random seeds. Through these robustness check, we confirmed the consistency of results across samples of this size (see Sect. 4). Moreover, the use of chain-of-thought prompting with large models incurs substantial computational cost per generation, making higher sample sizes impractical. Thus, 1000 strikes a balance between statistical resolution and feasibility in simulation-based research.

5.2 Toward the Replication of Human-like Decision Making

In this study, we investigated the reasoning ability of generative agents within LLMsdriven MAS to reproduce the results of economic experiments reported by (Lin et al. 2020). content_tags↗Building on the findings of Kitadai et al. (2023), we aimed to isolate the impact of reasoning ability by deliberately excluding persona settings, including demographic attributes such as age, gender, and personality. This decision was driven by the recognition that persona configurations, while critical to human-like decision making, can introduce variability that hinders the effects of reasoning abilities. mechanisms↗The randomly generated persona attributes in previous studies likely acted as noise, limiting the systematic evaluation of reasoning-related outcomes.

A. Kitadai et al.1 3 1321

The absence of persona settings allowed us to focus on understanding how reasoning ability influences decision-making outcomes and evaluate the alignment of simulated behavior with theoretical and experimental results. As described in Sect. 2.3, this approach revealed that higher reasoning ability does not necessarily bring simulation outcomes closer to experimental data but aligns them more closely with theoretical predictions.

content_tagsOur findings indicate that LLMs, even when endowed with advanced reasoning capabilities, do not naturally exhibit human-like economic behavior unless explicitly guided by prompts that incorporate social context. Consequently, using LLMdriven MAS as stand-ins for real human groups is currently unwarranted. Moreover, as LLMs continue to improve, there is a risk that such systems could misleadingly portray human behavior as excessively rational. Thus, treating LLMs as direct substitutes for human subjects is a flawed approach. This does not preclude the possibility of employing LLMs in economic modeling or behavioral simulation, but doing so effectively will require deliberate methodological scaffolding. If one seeks to explore such potential within the framework of the Platonic Representation Hypothesis by Huh et al. (2024), careful design and contextualization become essential.

limitationsWhile our methodological choice clarifies the role of reasoning ability, it also represents a limitation of this study. Persona settings are known to play a critical role in shaping human-like behavior in simulations because they reflect contextual variables such as culture, age, and gender, which significantly influence human decision-making (Horton 2023; Phelps and Russell 2024; Han et al. 2024). Current LLMs inherently lack these attributes unless explicitly prompted or configured, underscoring the need for future advancements in LLM architecture and experimental design. content_tagsIn fact, we attribute the difference between Fig. 2a, b (the results of Kitadai et al. (2023)) and Figs. 3a, 4a (the results of the simulation in this study) to the existence of persona settings because the other settings of the simulations were the same, suggesting that the settings of the personas of generative agents are essential for applying LLMs-driven MAS in economic experiments.

The discrepancy between experimental results and theoretical predictions in the ultimatum game has been extensively studied within the theoretical paradigms of behavioral economics. By interpreting these discrepancies through the lens of behavioral economics, we can formulate a hypothesis regarding the role of persona settings. First, the human tendency to reject low offers is explained by models of inequity aversion, such as those proposed by Fehr and Schmidt (1999) and Bolton and Ockenfels (2000), as well as fairness equilibrium by Rabin (1993). Furthermore, deviations from the subgame perfect equilibrium in human decision-making correspond to the concept of bounded rationality by Simon (1955). mechanisms↗This connection is further supported by our earlier observation that persona settings influenced the degree of deviation from human behavior: their inclusion may act as a proxy for embedding social preferences and bounded rationality into in LLM-driven MAS.

Future research should systematically explore the interplay between persona configurations and reasoning abilities to achieve a better balance between reproducing theoretical rationality and human-like behavior. This could involve designing prompts to specify demographic characteristics or fine-tuning LLMs on culturally diverse datasets to bridge the gap between LLM-generated behavior and human diversity.

Developing a general framework for persona settings can offer valuable insights into the trade-offs between theoretical predictions and the realism of simulations.

Our findings also have broader implications for human-agent interaction research. Variability in reasoning ability and persona configurations can significantly influence agent behavior, impacting the effectiveness of generative agents in various contexts. Researchers designing experiments with LLMs-driven MAS should carefully consider these factors to enhance the realism, interpretability, and applicability of simulation outcomes.
