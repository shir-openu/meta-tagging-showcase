Basic Sciences, 2026, vol. 2, issue 2 PHILOSOPHY OF SCIENCE SCIENTIFIC EXPLANATION, THEORY CHANGE, AND EPISTEMIC BOUNDARIES IN CONTEMPORARY PHILOSOPHY OF SCIENCE Authors: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20073948 Artemi Sokolov Doctor of Philosophical Sciences, Russia Leonid Averin PhD in Philosophy of Science, Russia Abstract Contemporary philosophy of science is defined less by the defense of a single canonical image of science than by the sustained examination of how scientific knowledge is explained, justified, revised, and bounded. Questions that once appeared separable now appear tightly linked. To ask what counts as a scientific explanation is also to ask what sort of theories science is entitled to accept, what relation observation bears to theory, how scientific change can remain rational across conceptual discontinuities, and whether successful science gives knowledge of unobservable structure or only empirically adequate representation. The modern field emerged in large part from logical empiricism and its concern with explanation, confirmation, and the formal structure of theories, but it was subsequently transformed by historicist, realist, pragmatist, causal, and pluralist interventions. The result is not simple fragmentation. It is a richer and more self-conscious understanding of science as an epistemic practice whose standards of explanation, objectivity, realism, and progress cannot be reduced to a single formal recipe. phenomenonThis article examines scientific explanation, theory change, and epistemic boundaries in contemporary philosophy of science. It reconstructs the major explanatory models of twentieth- and early twentyfirst-century philosophy of science, examines the logic and history of theory change, and clarifies how realism, empiricism, underdetermination, and objectivity define the principal epistemic boundaries of the contemporary field. Modern discussion of scientific explanation begins, in the canonical narrative, with the covering-law tradition associated above all with Carl Hempel. The SEP entry on Hempel identifies him as the principal proponent of the covering-law theory of explanation, while the SEP entry on twentieth-century scientific explanation notes that the deductive-nomological model received its most influential formulation in Hempel’s work. content_tags↗The basic intuition of that model is that to explain an event is to show that it was to be expected given general laws and initial conditions. The attraction of this approach was obvious. It offered a clear logical schema, linked explanation with prediction, and gave philosophy of science a formally elegant way of characterizing scientific understanding. In the context of logical empiricism and the received view of theories, this model appeared to promise a disciplined account of scientific rationality grounded in lawlike generalization and deductive structure. Yet the covering-law model also generated difficulties that proved philosophically decisive. One classic problem is 56 AMES. An argument may fit the formal structure of the model and yet fail to tell us why a phenomenon occurred in the scientifically satisfying way. Another problem, highlighted in the SEP entry on confirmation, is that Hempel’s general framework struggled to capture important “vertical” relations between observed phenomena and hypotheses about underlying structures, causes, and processes. This matters because much of scientific explanation does not proceed by deriving one surface regularity from another. It proceeds by positing entities, mechanisms, and causal structures that are not themselves directly observable but are meant to account for what is observed. The formal elegance of covering-law explanation therefore came at the cost of explanatory depth. As the field developed, causal and mechanistic accounts gained force partly because they seemed better able to capture that missing depth. The SEP entry on Wesley Salmon notes that Salmon rejected the Hempelian received view and developed a conception of explanation centered on exhibiting the causal mechanisms responsible for the occurrence of phenomena. The later SEP entry on causal approaches to scientific explanation describes more recent post-1990 developments in similar terms, while the entry on mechanisms in science shows how the concept of mechanism has become a major organizing principle for explanation in many sciences. These approaches do not merely replace logical derivation with a different vocabulary. They reorient philosophy of explanation toward productive dependence, process, and organized causal structure. An explanation is good not simply because it subsumes an event under a law, but because it shows how the event was generated, sustained, or organized by underlying causal arrangements. This causal turn, however, did not eliminate rival intuitions about explanation. The SEP entry on scientific explanation notes that unificationist accounts remained compelling because successful science often explains by showing that apparently different phenomena are manifestations of a smaller set of patterns or principles. The entry explicitly remarks that theory unification has clearly played an important role in science and that good explanations often connect phenomena previously thought unrelated. This line of thought became especially influential in philosophical accounts that emphasized systematic understanding rather than local causal generation alone. Unification does not deny causation, but it shifts the explanatory emphasis toward the reduction of theoretical fragmentation. A good explanation is one that makes the world intelligible by fitting more phenomena into fewer derivational or conceptual patterns. The philosophical importance of the contrast between causal and unificationist approaches lies in what it reveals about science itself. If explanation is fundamentally causal, then the aim of science is to uncover the productive structures of the world. If explanation is fundamentally unificatory, then the aim of science is to reduce intellectual disunity and provide a more systematic representation of phenomena. In practice, both intuitions have clear scientific support. Biology and medicine often seem to demand mechanismsensitive explanations. Physics has often been celebrated for unification. Contemporary philosophy of science therefore rarely resolves the issue by simple victory of one model over all others. Instead, it treats explanatory pluralism as increasingly unavoidable, especially across disciplines with different explanatory targets and methods. The resulting picture is less monolithic than the old covering-law image, but arguably closer to actual scientific practice. A further complication is introduced by pragmatic accounts of explanation, especially those associated with Bas van Fraassen. The SEP archive on twentieth-century scientific explanation notes that one influential pragmatic line is connected with constructive empiricism and the view that the aim of science is empirical adequacy rather than literal truth about unobservables. On such views, explanation is not assessed solely by metaphysical correspondence or causal revelation, but also by its role within inquiry and its adequacy relative to explanatory interests. This does not make explanation arbitrary. It means that explanatory success may depend partly on context, contrast class, and epistemic aim. The philosophy of explanation thus widens from a purely formal or ontological enterprise into an inquiry about the purposes and standards of scientific understanding. The net effect of these debates is not the collapse of the concept of scientific explanation, but its enrichment. Explanation remains central precisely because different philosophical models reveal different demands placed on science: lawfulness, causation, mechanism, unification, empirical adequacy, and pragmatic usefulness. content_tagsContemporary philosophy of science increasingly treats explanation as a family of related epistemic achievements rather than a single formal relation. This development matters greatly for the rest of the article, because disputes about theory change and realism cannot be understood apart from what one takes explanation to accomplish. A philosophy that sees explanation as causal disclosure will assess theory change differently from one that treats it as improved problem-solving or empirical adequacy. Explanation is therefore not just one topic among many. It is one of the hinges on which wider philosophy-ofscience disputes turn. If explanation concerns how science makes phenomena intelligible, theory change concerns how science remakes its own standards of intelligibility over time. This is why the problem of theory change became so disruptive to older logical-empiricist images of science. A philosophy built around formal confirmation and explanation could still imagine scientific development as broadly cumulative, even if particular hypotheses were abandoned. But the historicist transformation of philosophy of science, associated above all with Thomas Kuhn and related figures, challenged the assumption that there must always be neutral, timeless criteria governing theoretical succession. The SEP entry on scientific progress emphasizes that Popper and Kuhn offered sharply different conceptions of progress, while the entry on historicist theories of scientific rationality notes that historicists stressed the depth of major historical changes and the challenge these posed to the traditional logic of science. Theory change, on this view, is not simply the correction of isolated mistakes within a stable conceptual frame. It can involve changes in standards, problems, exemplars, and even observational salience. Popper’s role in this debate remains fundamental because his falsificationist image of science preserved a strong normative picture of rational criticism even while rejecting the simple accumulation of confirmations. In the SEP entry on scientific progress, Popper is described as understanding progress in relation to approximation toward truth, while the broader philosophical tradition associated with him emphasizes conjecture, refutation, and the elimination of error. A philosophically adequate account of science must therefore balance normative analysis with sensitivity to changing scientific practice. Theory change places pressure on every simple account of explanation and realism. If explanation changes historically, then one must ask which features of explanation are durable. The problem of theory change therefore acts as a bridge from explanatory models to the epistemic-boundary debates treated in the next section. The debate between realism and constructive empiricism is closely tied to underdetermination. The SEP entry on underdetermination explains that evidence may be insufficient to determine what beliefs we should hold, and in the scientific case this means that empirically equivalent or comparably successful theories may outrun what evidence can uniquely settle. If that possibility is genuine and nontrivial, then realism appears epistemically ambitious: it claims more than the evidence strictly forces. Constructive empiricism, by contrast, appears better calibrated to evidential modesty, since it requires only empirical adequacy. Yet realism has its own response. As the realism entry indicates, realist arguments often claim that inference to the best explanation or the “no miracles” intuition gives us reason to regard theoretical success as evidentially stronger than mere empirical fit. The real issue is therefore not whether evidence underdetermines theory in the abstract, but whether scientific success plus explanatory power license moving beyond empirical adequacy to approximate truth. This dispute reveals one of the deepest epistemic boundaries in philosophy of science: the boundary between what scientific practice uses and what scientific belief is rationally allowed to include. Scientists routinely talk about electrons, fields, genes, mechanisms, and black holes as if these were real components of the world. The antirealist challenge is not that such language must be banned, but that its practical usefulness does not settle its literal truth. The realist reply is that a philosophy too cautious to acknowledge the epistemic force of sustained theoretical success risks misdescribing science from within. Philosophy of science thus becomes an inquiry into how epistemic modesty and explanatory ambition should be balanced. This balance is one of the central themes of the article as a whole. The boundary debates of philosophy of science are not exhausted by realism and antirealism. They also concern the status of observation and the meaning of objectivity. The SEP entry on theory and observation stresses that observation has traditionally been seen as the route through which experience constrains theory, but also argues that the strict contrast between theory and observation is less useful than inquiry into their relation. Observation is often instrumentmediated, concept-laden, and dependent on background assumptions about sensitivity and relevance. If so, the epistemic authority of science must be explained without the fiction of completely theory-free data. The problem of objectivity is therefore more subtle than older accounts assumed. content_tags↗The SEP entry on scientific objectivity states that objectivity is an ideal according to which scientific claims, methods, and results should not be influenced by particular perspectives, values, or biases. This is philosophically important because once observation is acknowledged as theory-laden and scientific change as historically deep, objectivity can no longer plausibly mean total absence of perspective. It must instead be reinterpreted as a disciplined achievement of scientific communities, methods, and practices. The authority of science then rests not on impossible neutrality, but on organized procedures for criticism, calibration, replication, and intersubjective control. If scientific objectivity is an achieved property of methods and communities rather than a metaphysical transparency of thought to reality, then the philosopher of science must ask what forms of social, methodological, and conceptual organization make science trustworthy. Philosophy of science therefore faces a delicate task: it must acknowledge the historical and theory-laden character of science without collapsing the distinction between better and worse inquiry. These issues also show why philosophy of science remains indispensable even in highly specialized scientific cultures. The sciences themselves often advance effectively without explicit reflection on realism, incommensurability, or constructive empiricism. The epistemic boundaries of science are not internal technical questions alone. They concern how science relates to truth, evidence, method, and rational belief. Philosophy of science remains the discipline in which those relations are made explicit and critically examined. Because models represent only certain aspects of target systems and because those targets are often too complex to be grasped directly, abstraction and idealization are part of what make scientific understanding possible. The result is a more sophisticated picture of scientific cognition: a model may be inaccurate in many respects and still be indispensable because it isolates a dependency structure, captures a stable pattern, or makes a mechanism manipulable for reasoning. This point is philosophically significant because it complicates any naive equation of representation with resemblance. The representation entry discusses similarity conceptions and structuralist conceptions precisely because the older intuition that models work by looking like the world in the right way turns out to be too simple. Some models are highly abstract mathematical constructions; some are physical surrogates; some are computational; some are idealized narratives. Their scientific legitimacy often depends not on resemblance in a literal sense, but on whether reasoning conducted on the model can yield warranted claims about the target system. The representation entry marks this as a condition of adequacy for accounts of representation: models must support hypothesis formation and knowledge transfer about targets. In this light, the epistemic power of science appears tied not only to correspondence but to disciplined inferential use. This is one reason philosophical accounts of modeling often gravitate toward inferential or practice-oriented conceptions rather than purely pictorial ones. The consequences for philosophy of science are wideranging. First, models make it clear that scientific explanation often trades in controlled distortions. Second, they show that understanding can grow through representations that are not straightforwardly true in every detail. Third, they reopen the boundary question between science and fiction without collapsing the distinction. A toy model or highly idealized model may resemble a fiction in some respects, but it remains scientific insofar as it is tied to evidential practice, inferential control, and target-directed reasoning. This is why philosophy of science increasingly asks not whether a model is literally true, but what sort of representational work it performs and how that work is justified. Scientific rationality thus becomes inseparable from representational competence. This emphasis on idealization also affects how one thinks about theory change. If science advances partly by refining, replacing, or recontextualizing models, then theoretical progress need not consist only in replacing one fully articulated worldview with another. It may also consist in altering what kinds of models are used, how they connect to data, and what inferential roles they play. The pragmatic view of theory structure, as presented in the SEP entry, is particularly relevant here because it resists the idea that theories can be captured fully by one formal core. Instead, theories include practices, skills, and standards. On such a view, theory change may involve changes in representational style and inferential habit as much as changes in explicit doctrine. Philosophy of science thereby becomes more attentive to the internal texture of scientific practice and less dependent on overly sharp divisions between “theory,” “application,” and “context.” Once models and representations move to the foreground, the relation between explanation and understanding also needs to be reconsidered. A central ambition of older philosophy of science was to identify what explanation is in formal or causal terms. Contemporary philosophy is more likely to ask how explanations actually contribute to scientific understanding, and whether different explanatory forms yield different kinds of understanding. The modelbased and pragmatic turns support this shift because they show that explanation is not exhausted by one formal derivation pattern. A scientific representation can improve understanding by allowing one to see dependency relations, manipulate assumptions, compare counterfactual outcomes, or connect different phenomena through a unifying pattern. Even when a strict causal-mechanistic account remains appropriate in some domains, understanding often depends on more than causal listing. It depends on what the representation enables investigators to do intellectually. The semantic view privileges mathematical models. The pragmatic view emphasizes that theory includes nonformal elements such as examples, standards, analogies, and practices. This broadened conception of understanding is also important because it helps make sense of explanatory pluralism without surrendering to relativism. The rise of the new mechanistic philosophy has been one of the most influential reorientations in recent philosophy of science, especially because it changed how explanation across levels is discussed. This mechanistic reorientation also modifies older debates about reduction. The SEP entry on scientific reduction describes the classical Nagelian model as treating reduction as a kind of explanation relation between theories, where one theory is derivable from another with the help of bridge laws. That model is philosophically elegant and historically central, but later work showed that many real cases of scientific explanation do not fit neatly into direct derivation of one theory from another. Mechanistic accounts respond by focusing less on intertheoretic deduction and more on organized constitution and causal production. A biological process, for example, may be explained by identifying the organized cellular or molecular activities that generate it, even if no one has deduced the whole higher-level theory from a lower-level one. The recognition that science is socially organized does not merely complicate objectivity. It also changes how epistemic boundaries are drawn. If social relations, institutional incentives, and background values shape inquiry, then the line between internal scientific reasoning and external influence can no longer be drawn too sharply. Epistemic boundaries in philosophy of science are therefore cross-cutting. They mark not one single frontier between science and non-science, or between knowledge and non-knowledge, but a family of tensions internal to scientific rationality itself. A particularly important consequence follows from this. Philosophy of science is no longer plausibly conceived as the search for a single universal essence of science. The strongest contemporary work does not deny that science is distinctive. It denies only that its distinctiveness can be captured through one formal recipe. Scientific practice is heterogeneous in methods, representational forms, scales of explanation, and disciplinary aims. Yet this heterogeneity does not make scientific rationality unintelligible. It forces philosophy to seek a more flexible account of how science remains 65 AMES. Basic Sciences, 2026, vol. 2, issue 2 answerable to evidence, criticism, and standards of success despite internal plurality. In this sense, the main achievement of late twentieth- and early twenty-first-century philosophy of science has not been the destruction of normativity. It has been the reconstruction of normativity under more realistic assumptions about what science is actually like. The fate of scientific explanation illustrates this reconstruction especially clearly. The field moved from the hope that one logical schema could capture explanation universally to a more differentiated understanding in which causal, mechanistic, unificationist, pragmatic, and modelbased accounts each illuminate important parts of scientific practice. mechanisms↗This transformation did not occur because philosophers lost interest in rigor. It occurred because rigor itself required closer responsiveness to the sciences. Law-centered accounts brought conceptual clarity but struggled with causal depth and explanatory relevance. Mechanistic approaches captured organized production more effectively in many domains, especially biology and neuroscience. Unificationist approaches retained the intuition that understanding often involves seeing more with less. Pragmatic and empiricist approaches showed that explanatory significance also depends on epistemic context and inquiry aims. The result is not a collapse of explanation into anything whatever. It is a more plural and practice-sensitive conception of explanatory achievement. This matters philosophically because explanation remains one of the main places where science seems to reach beyond mere prediction or data organization. If science explains, then it appears to do more than save the appearances. But once explanatory forms diversify, philosophy of science can no longer assume that one metaphysical or epistemic lesson follows uniformly from all explanatory success. Some explanations support realist commitments more strongly than others. Some are tied to mechanisms or causes, while others are strongly idealized and patternbased. Some generate understanding through interventionist reasoning, while others do so through structural simplification or representational control. Contemporary philosophy of science therefore treats explanation as central not because it yields one final answer, but because it reveals the variety of ways science makes the world intelligible. The boundary question then becomes: which kinds of explanatory success justify which kinds of ontological or epistemic commitment? This also clarifies why model-based explanation has become so significant. Once theories are not treated as the only units of analysis, explanation can be located in the inferential and representational practices through which models isolate relevant variables, reveal dependency relations, or support counterfactual reasoning. This does not weaken explanation. It often makes it closer to what scientists actually do. A climate model, a mechanistic pathway diagram, a population-genetic model, or a toy model in economics may each explain differently, yet still genuinely contribute to understanding. Philosophy of science has therefore learned that explanatory legitimacy is compatible with selective idealization and even with deliberate distortion, provided the distortion is disciplined by target-directed reasoning and empirical accountability. That lesson has been one of the decisive routes by which the field moved away from monism without abandoning explanatory seriousness. The same reconstructive pattern is visible in the treatment of theory change. The older dream of a timeless logic of theory succession was weakened not only by Kuhn’s account of paradigms and revolutions, but by the broader historicist recognition that standards of significance, explanation, evidence, and problem choice themselves have histories. Yet the strongest outcome of this historicist turn has not been the abandonment of rationality. It has been a change in what rationality is taken to require. The question is no longer how science could remain rational only if the same formal criteria governed every episode of change. It is how scientific communities respond appropriately to changing conceptual and empirical circumstances while preserving enough continuity of criticism and comparison for theory change to count as more than replacement by fashion. This is why incommensurability, though often dramatized, should be interpreted with care. It reveals that scientific change can involve altered taxonomies, standards, and salient phenomena, but it does not show that comparison becomes impossible or that progress is therefore illusory. Rather, it shows that appraisal is more complex than simple side-by-side measurement against one permanent metric. Later theories may solve problems their predecessors could not formulate clearly, integrate previously separate domains, or reorganize representational practice in ways that make older frameworks look partial or distorted. Historicity thus complicates the image of progress without cancelling it. Scientific rationality survives, but not as mere algorithmic continuity. It survives as disciplined responsiveness to problem-solving success, empirical resistance, explanatory reach, and critical comparison under changing conceptual conditions. This historically informed view also helps philosophy of science resist two temptations. One temptation is nostalgic: to imagine that science would be fully rational only if its standards never changed. The other is deflationary: to infer from changing standards that rationality is merely retrospective self-justification. Contemporary philosophy of science increasingly rejects both. Instead, it studies how scientific communities build continuity through methods, exemplars, measurement traditions, inferential norms, and shared problem contexts even while undergoing major theoretical transformation. In that way, theory change becomes an especially revealing test case for the broader claim of this article: epistemic boundaries are negotiated and revised, but not therefore arbitrary. Their authority depends on how well they continue to support inquiry under altered intellectual conditions. The long dispute between scientific realism and antirealism remains decisive because it asks how far science is entitled to reach beyond the observable. Yet under contemporary conditions this dispute has become more subtle. Realism can no longer rely comfortably on the success of whole theories conceived as monolithic entities, because scientific practice is now understood as heavily model-based, idealized, and plural. Constructive empiricism, by contrast, seems well suited to a science organized around empirical adequacy and representational usefulness. Still, realism persists because many philosophers judge that explanation, intervention, and systematic unification are not adequately captured if science is credited only with saving observable phenomena. The issue is therefore no longer whether science “talks about reality” in some crude sense. It is whether, and to what extent, the success of scientific representation licenses belief in unobservable structures, mechanisms, or relations. In this context, underdetermination plays a special role. It marks the limit point at which empirical evidence alone may fail to settle which scientific commitments are rationally mandatory. Yet underdetermination has not led most contemporary philosophers to abandon all stronger epistemic claims. Instead, it has encouraged finer discrimination. Some commitments may be justified because they are heavily supported by multiple independent explanatory and experimental practices. Others may remain more provisional 66 AMES. Basic Sciences, 2026, vol. 2, issue 2 or only instrumentally licensed. Structural realism is one prominent expression of this middle path, but it is not the only one. More generally, philosophy of science has learned to distinguish levels of commitment. The epistemic question is not always whether one must believe everything a successful science says. It is which parts of its representational apparatus are sufficiently integrated into explanatory, predictive, and manipulative success to warrant stronger belief. This layered account of commitment is one of the strongest signs of philosophical maturity in the field. It preserves the realist insight that scientific success is not epistemically empty, while also preserving the empiricist insight that evidential discipline matters and that not every representational convenience deserves ontological endorsement. Epistemic boundaries are thus redrawn more carefully rather than erased. Science can still be epistemically ambitious, but its ambition is better understood as differentiated rather than all-or-nothing. Philosophy of science now asks, in effect, where science is entitled to be bold and where it ought to remain restrained. That is a more demanding and more realistic question than the older search for one final slogan about truth or empirical adequacy. Perhaps no topic better illustrates the reconstructive character of contemporary philosophy of science than objectivity. Older ideals often suggested that objectivity required freedom from values, perspectives, and social interests. Contemporary work has shown that such a conception is neither attainable nor philosophically adequate. Observation is theory-laden. Inquiry is institutionally organized. Research agendas and standards of relevance can reflect social structures. Yet the philosophical response has not been simply to discard objectivity. It has been to reconceive it as an achievement of well-organized inquiry: a product of criticism, transparency, uptake, responsiveness to evidence, and in many cases epistemic diversity. On this view, objectivity is not what remains when sociality disappears. mechanisms↗It is what becomes possible when social and methodological arrangements are structured so that no single bias or perspective can easily dominate unchecked. This is why feminist and social epistemology have become so important to philosophy of science. Their significance does not lie only in criticizing past exclusions. It lies in showing how better science can depend on the inclusion of perspectives, questions, and forms of criticism previously marginalized. Objectivity remains a regulative ideal, but it is now less often understood as a transcendence of all standpoint and more often as a disciplined relation among standpoints within reliable communities of inquiry. The same holds for values. The crucial issue is not whether science is ever value-influenced, but how values interact with evidence, methods, and criticism so that inquiry remains accountable. This reconstructed objectivity also clarifies scientific authority. Science does not command respect because it has escaped all human conditions. It commands respect insofar as it has developed institutional and methodological forms that often make it better at error detection, disciplined revision, and empirically constrained representation than its alternatives. This authority is therefore conditional and earned rather than metaphysically guaranteed. Philosophy of science remains crucial precisely because it articulates those conditions. It asks what kinds of community structure, methodological pluralism, evidential discipline, and critical openness are needed if scientific claims are to deserve public trust. Such questions are not external to epistemology. They are among its most urgent contemporary forms. The argument of this article can now be gathered into a more general statement. Contemporary philosophy of science is best understood as a post-positivist but still normative field. It no longer expects one formal model of explanation, one fixed structure of theory, one universal scientific method, one timeless criterion of theory choice, or one simple account of objectivity. Yet it has not therefore resigned itself to relativism or skepticism. Instead, it investigates how explanation, representation, realism, theory change, objectivity, and progress can still be defended in reconstructed form under conditions of historical change, model-based science, methodological plurality, and social embeddedness. What has changed is not the seriousness of these ideals, but their philosophical formulation. This suggests that the deepest epistemic boundary in philosophy of science is not the line between science and non-science in the old demarcationist sense, though that question still matters. The deeper boundary lies between stronger and weaker claims that science can make about the world, about its own methods, and about its authority. Explanation tests how much intelligibility science can legitimately claim. Theory change tests how much continuity rationality requires. Realism and empiricism test how much ontology success licenses. Objectivity and social epistemology test how much authority science can sustain without idealized neutrality. Progress tests whether scientific change can still be called improvement when methods, values, and concepts themselves evolve. Philosophy of science remains unified, if at all, not by one answer to all these questions, but by sustained reflection on their interaction. The strongest current image of science to emerge from these debates is therefore one of disciplined pluralism. Science is heterogeneous in theories, models, scales, explanatory forms, and communities. But that heterogeneity need not undermine its epistemic standing. Properly organized, it may be one of the sources of its strength. Philosophy of science, on this view, is not the search for one final essence hidden beneath scientific diversity. It is the inquiry into how that diversity can nonetheless be critically structured, empirically constrained, historically progressive, and publicly authoritative. That is why the field remains not a museum of twentieth-century disputes, but one of the main places where the meaning of scientific knowledge is still being worked out. Objectivity was rethought under the pressure of theory-ladenness, social epistemology, and feminist critique. Yet the cumulative result of these challenges has not been the collapse of philosophy of science. It has been its maturation into a field more attentive to scientific practice, more self-critical about its own abstractions, and more precise about the conditions under which science remains rational, objective, and progressive.
